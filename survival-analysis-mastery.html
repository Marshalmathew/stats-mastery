Survival and time-to-event analysis: censoring, hazards, Kaplan–Meier, Cox and beyond.

How long until a loan defaults, a customer churns, an account is closed or a case is flagged? The outcome is a time, and some subjects are still “alive” when you stop watching (censored). Ignoring that, by averaging the observed times, dropping the censored cases, or fitting a plain classifier on “event within follow-up”, gives biased answers. This page builds the whole toolkit from the ground up: censoring, the survival and hazard functions, the Kaplan–Meier estimator, the log-rank test and its weights, the Cox model and what it does and does not tell you, checking proportional hazards, competing risks, discrete-time (person-period) models for credit and churn, evaluating survival models with censored data, and parametric extrapolation. Every lab simulates from a known truth.

Part I · Time-to-event basics

1Why time-to-event data is different Beginner

For each subject we want the time T until an event, but we often only see that T exceeds some time C at which we lost sight of the subject or the study ended: the observation is right-censored, and we record the time min(T, C) and an indicator of whether it was the event. Censoring carries real information (“survived at least this long”) and must be used, not discarded. Related notions: left truncation (delayed entry: subjects only enter the data if they have already survived to some time, as with a loan book observed from a given date) and interval censoring (event known only to lie between two inspection dates). The main working assumption is non-informative censoring: the reason for censoring is unrelated to the subject’s remaining time to event, given the covariates.

The naive fixes fail: the average of observed times mixes events with censoring and under-states the typical time; the average of event times only (dropping censored subjects) favours early events and under-states it more; treating “censored” as “no event” in a classifier makes the probability depend on how long you happened to watch. The correct estimators use each subject’s time at risk.

2Survival, hazard and cumulative hazard Intermediate

Four equivalent descriptions of the distribution of T: the survival function S(t) = P(T > t); the density f(t); the hazard h(t) = f(t)/S(t), the instantaneous event rate among those still at risk; and the cumulative hazard H(t) = ∫₀ᵗ h(u)du, with S(t) = exp(−H(t)). The mean is ∫₀^∞ S(t)dt and the restricted mean survival time RMST(τ) = ∫₀^τ S(t)dt is the average time event-free up to τ, a quantity that is always estimable. Shapes of the hazard are what distinguish models: constant (exponential), monotone (Weibull: decreasing for shape < 1, as with early churn or infant mortality; increasing for shape > 1, as with wear-out), or rising then falling (log-normal, log-logistic: loans that are most at risk in their second year).

3The Kaplan–Meier estimator Advanced

At each distinct event time t_j let n_j be the number at risk and d_j the number of events. The product-limit estimator is

Ŝ(t) = ∏_{t_j ≤ t} (1 − d_j / n_j) Var[Ŝ(t)] ≈ Ŝ(t)² Σ_{t_j ≤ t} d_j / [n_j (n_j − d_j)] (Greenwood)

It is the non-parametric maximum-likelihood estimate under non-informative censoring. Confidence bands are best built on the log(−log) scale, which keeps them inside [0, 1]. The curve is a step function; its tail is unstable because few subjects remain at risk, so always show the number at risk. The median is the first time Ŝ ≤ 0.5 (not reached if the curve never gets there). The naive estimate “1 − fraction who have had the event by t” treats censored subjects as survivors forever and over-states survival.

4Comparing groups: the log-rank test and its weights Advanced

At each event time the log-rank test compares the observed number of events in group 1 with the number expected if both groups shared the same hazard, then sums the differences over all event times. It is the most powerful test under proportional hazards. Weighting the times gives the Fleming–Harrington family G(ρ, γ): weights S(t−)^ρ [1 − S(t−)]^γ put more weight on early differences (ρ > 0, as the Gehan–Wilcoxon test does) or late ones (γ > 0). If the survival curves cross, the plain log-rank test can have almost no power; use a weighted test, a combination (max-combo), or compare restricted mean survival times. Always accompany a p-value with an effect size and the curves.

Part II · Regression and its assumptions

5The Cox proportional hazards model Advanced

The Cox model writes the hazard for covariates x as h(t | x) = h₀(t) · exp(xᵀβ), with an unspecified baseline hazard h₀(t). The coefficients are estimated by maximising the partial likelihood

PL(β) = ∏_{events i} exp(x_iᵀβ) / Σ_{j ∈ R(t_i)} exp(x_jᵀβ)

in which each event time contributes the probability that the subject who had the event was the one, among all still at risk, to fail. The baseline hazard cancels, yet the estimator is consistent and nearly fully efficient. exp(β) is the hazard ratio per unit of x. The information is carried by the events (so the events-per-parameter rules of the previous chapter apply). Correct handling of censoring matters: dropping the censored subjects, or treating their censoring times as events, distorts both estimates and standard errors.

6From hazard ratios to absolute risk Advanced

A hazard ratio is a relative measure: it says that one group’s instantaneous risk is a multiple of another’s, but not how many events to expect. To predict, estimate the baseline cumulative hazard with the Breslow estimator, Ĥ₀(t) = Σ_{t_j ≤ t} d_j / Σ_{R(t_j)} exp(xᵀβ̂), and then Ŝ(t | x) = exp(−Ĥ₀(t) · exp(xᵀβ̂)). The same hazard ratio therefore implies very different absolute risk differences depending on the baseline risk: a ratio of 2 on a 1% risk adds one percentage point, on a 30% risk about nineteen. The ranking quality of the linear predictor is summarised by Harrell’s concordance index (C): among comparable pairs, the share in which the subject with the higher predicted risk fails first.

7Checking proportional hazards Expert

The Cox model assumes that the hazard ratio is constant over time. Real effects often wane (a retention offer works at first), grow, or cross. Diagnostics: log(−log Ŝ) curves that should be parallel; Schoenfeld residuals plotted against time (a trend means non-proportional); the Grambsch–Therneau test; and adding a covariate × log(time) interaction. When the assumption fails the Cox coefficient is a weighted average of the changing log hazard ratio, with weights depending on the number of events and the censoring pattern, so the “hazard ratio” you report changes with the length of follow-up. Remedies: stratify by the offending variable, model the time-varying effect (piecewise or interaction), report restricted mean survival time differences, or use a model without the assumption (AFT, discrete-time with time interactions).

8Competing risks Expert

When an event of another type removes the subject from risk of the event of interest (a loan prepays before it defaults, a customer dies or is closed by the bank before churning), the events are competing risks. Treating the competing event as censoring and computing 1 − KM answers a hypothetical question (“what if the other event could not happen?”) and overstates the real cumulative probability; with several causes the 1 − KM curves can add up to more than 1. The correct estimate of the probability of cause k by time t is the cumulative incidence function F_k(t) = ∫₀ᵗ S(u−) dΛ_k(u) (the Aalen–Johansen estimator), where S is the all-cause survival. Cause-specific hazards (with Cox models) describe the mechanism; the Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models the cumulative incidence directly and is used for prediction.

Part III · Credit, churn and prediction

9Discrete-time (person-period) models Advanced

Credit and churn data are recorded monthly. The discrete-time hazard is h_t = P(event in period t | at risk at the start of t). Expand each subject into one row per period at risk, with the event indicator equal to 1 only in the period of the event, and fit a logistic regression on a flexible function of time (period dummies or a spline) plus covariates. The survival curve is Ŝ(t) = ∏_{s ≤ t}(1 − ĥ_s), and the model handles censoring, left truncation and time-varying covariates (macroeconomic indicators, utilisation, days past due) naturally. This is the standard route to a term structure of default probabilities (lifetime PDs for IFRS 9 or Ind AS 109 expected credit loss). With small hazards its coefficients are close to those of the Cox model; the price is a large expanded data set.

10Evaluating survival models under censoring Expert

With censored data the usual metrics need care. Harrell’s C only uses comparable pairs and so depends on the censoring pattern; Uno’s C reweights by the inverse probability of censoring (IPCW). At a horizon t* one wants the time-dependent AUC (cases: event by t*, controls: event-free beyond t*) and the Brier score and calibration of predicted risk at t*. Subjects censored before t* have unknown status: dropping them, or counting them as non-events, biases the AUC and the observed risk; IPCW weighting (with the Kaplan–Meier estimate of the censoring distribution), or the Kaplan–Meier estimate of observed risk within risk groups, corrects it. Validate on out-of-time data and report calibration at the horizon relevant to the decision.

11Parametric models and the danger of extrapolation Expert

Parametric survival models assume a distribution for T and fit its parameters by maximum likelihood: each event contributes log f(t), each censored subject log S(t). The accelerated failure time form, log T = xᵀβ + σε, gives coefficients interpreted as time ratios; the Weibull is both a PH and an AFT model. Parametric models give smooth curves and extrapolate beyond the follow-up, which is what lifetime expected-loss calculations and cost-effectiveness models need, but the extrapolation is determined by the assumed family and not by the data. Several families can fit the observed window equally well (similar AIC) and diverge sharply afterwards. Use clinical or business knowledge for the tail, compare candidates visually with the Kaplan–Meier curve, and report the range, not one number. Cure or mixture models allow a fraction of subjects who never have the event.
