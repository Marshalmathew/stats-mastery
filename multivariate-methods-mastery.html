Multivariate methods: seeing the shape of many variables at once.

Real data have many variables that move together. This page works from the geometry of the covariance matrix to the tools built on it: Mahalanobis distance for finding unusual combinations, principal components for compression, factor analysis for latent structure and why its answer is only defined up to a rotation, Gaussian mixtures for hidden groups, and then the places where each tool fails: too many variables for the sample, outliers that hide themselves, a predictive signal in a low-variance direction, distances that lose meaning in high dimension, and components that are not stable. It starts Stage 7, the specialist topics.

Part I · Geometry, distance and components

1The covariance matrix and its geometry Beginner

With several variables measured on the same cases the basic summaries are a mean vector and a covariance matrix Σ, whose diagonal holds the variances and whose off-diagonal entries hold the covariances. Geometrically, a cloud of points that is roughly normal is an ellipse (an ellipsoid in more dimensions). The eigenvectors of Σ are the axes of the ellipse and the eigenvalues are the variances along those axes, so correlation is just the tilt of the cloud. Everything in this page builds on this: PCA rotates to the axes, Mahalanobis distance rescales each axis by its spread, factor analysis splits Σ into shared and unique parts, and mixtures use one Σ per cluster. Sample eigenvalues are biased apart: the largest is too large and the smallest too small, increasingly so when the sample is small.

Σ = V Λ Vᵀ · eigenvectors V = axes · eigenvalues Λ = variances along the axes · total variance = trace Σ = Σλ

2Mahalanobis distance Intermediate

How far is a customer or transaction from the typical profile? The Euclidean distance treats every direction alike. The Mahalanobis distance, d² = (x − m)ᵀ S⁻¹ (x − m), measures distance in units of the data’s own spread along each direction, so a step against the correlation (unusual combinations) counts for much more than a step along it. For normal data d² has a χ² distribution with p degrees of freedom, which gives a principled threshold: flag points with d² above the 99% quantile of χ²_p. It is the basis of multivariate outlier screening, of the covariance-adjusted nearest-profile methods used in AML peer-group comparisons, and of the discriminant rules that come with Gaussian classification. Its weaknesses, estimating S in high dimension and the effect of the outliers on S, are the subject of chapters 7 and 8.

d² = (x − m)ᵀ S⁻¹ (x − m) ~ χ²ₚ for normal data · flag if d² > χ²ₚ(0.99)

3Principal components and how many to keep Intermediate

Principal component analysis rotates the data to the eigenvectors of the covariance (or correlation) matrix and orders the new axes by variance. Keeping the first k components compresses the variables with the least squared reconstruction error of any k-dimensional linear summary. Standardise first (use the correlation matrix) when the variables are in different units, otherwise the largest-scale variable dominates. The number to keep is the difficult part. Kaiser’s rule (eigenvalue above 1), a fixed share of variance (say 80%) and the eye-balled elbow of a scree plot are all common and all unreliable. Parallel analysis is better founded: keep the components whose eigenvalue exceeds what pure noise of the same size would give. Remember also that PCA finds directions of high variance, which are not necessarily the directions that matter for the question (chapter 9).

Part II · Latent structure, mixtures and many variables

4PCA against factor analysis Advanced

PCA and factor analysis look similar and answer different questions. PCA summarises the total variance of the variables. The factor model says each variable is a loading times a few latent factors plus its own unique noise, x = Λf + ε with Var(ε) = Ψ diagonal, so Σ = ΛΛᵀ + Ψ: the covariances come entirely from the common factors. Fitting that model (by principal-axis iteration, maximum likelihood or a Bayesian version) separates shared variance from noise. The practical difference is that PCA loadings absorb each variable’s noise and are biased upward, especially with few variables; the difference shrinks as the number of variables per factor grows. Use PCA to build compact inputs and FA when the aim is to interpret or measure a latent construct, such as a customer-engagement or financial-stress index, and say which was used.

Σ = Λ Λᵀ + Ψ (factor analysis) · Σ ≈ V₁ Λ₁ V₁ᵀ (PCA, truncated)

5Rotation and what a factor means Advanced

A factor solution is not unique. If Λ is a set of loadings, ΛT for any orthogonal matrix T gives exactly the same fitted covariance, communalities and likelihood. The data cannot choose between these rotations, so the analyst does, usually by seeking simple structure: each variable loads strongly on one factor and little on the others. Varimax does this for orthogonal factors; oblique rotations (promax, oblimin) allow the factors to correlate and are more realistic for behavioural constructs. The consequence for model documentation is that a named factor (“credit appetite”) is an interpretation of one rotation, and loadings from two packages can differ for this reason alone. It also means factor scores are defined only up to the rotation, so a scoring system built on factors should freeze the rotation and its code.

6Gaussian mixtures and the EM algorithm Advanced

If the population is a blend of groups, a Gaussian mixture models it as a weighted sum of normal densities, each with its own mean and covariance. Fitting uses the EM algorithm: the E step gives every point a soft membership in each component given the current parameters, the M step re-estimates each component’s weight, mean and covariance from the weighted points, and the likelihood increases at every iteration. Three practical issues dominate. EM finds a local optimum, so use several starts. The number of components cannot be chosen by likelihood, which always improves with more components; use a penalised criterion (BIC) and check stability. A component can collapse onto a single point, so regularise the covariances. Mixtures also give soft cluster probabilities, which is more honest than k-means’ hard labels, but a fitted “segment” may be an artefact of non-normality, so name segments only after checking them against behaviour.

p(x) = Σₖ wₖ N(x | μₖ, Σₖ) · E step: rᵢₖ ∝ wₖ N(xᵢ | μₖ, Σₖ) · M step: weighted means and covariances

7Covariance when there are many variables Advanced

The sample covariance matrix is only reliable when the number of cases is much larger than the number of variables. When p/n is not small it is noisy: eigenvalues spread far beyond the true ones (the Marchenko–Pastur law quantifies this for uncorrelated data), the smallest approach zero, and with p ≥ n the matrix cannot be inverted. All of the tools above that invert Σ then fail: Mahalanobis distance, Gaussian discriminant analysis and minimum-variance portfolios. Shrinkage estimators blend the sample matrix with a simple target (a scaled identity, or a diagonal) using an intensity estimated from the data (Ledoit–Wolf). They are always invertible, well conditioned, and usually closer to the truth, at the cost of some bias. Structured alternatives (factor models, banded or sparse precision matrices) are used when something is known about the structure. A p/n above 0.1 is the signal to move off the plain sample covariance.

Part III · Robustness and limits

8Robust covariance and masking Expert

A classical mean and covariance are fitted to all the data, including the points one wants to find. A cluster of outliers shifts the mean, and above all inflates the variance in its own direction, so their Mahalanobis distances shrink: this is masking, and it grows with the share of contamination and with the outliers’ coherence (a tight cluster of mule accounts hides itself better than scattered ones). Robust estimators fit the bulk of the data and ignore the rest. The minimum covariance determinant (MCD) searches for the half of the points whose covariance matrix has the smallest determinant (the tightest cloud), fits the mean and covariance to them, and rescales for consistency. It tolerates contamination up to almost half of the data. Always compare classical and robust distances: points that the robust distance flags and the classical one does not are the masked ones. Robust estimators are slower and lose a little efficiency, which is a reasonable price for screening.

9The trap in principal components regression Expert

Principal components regression regresses the outcome on the first few components, which removes collinearity and reduces the number of parameters. The hidden assumption is that the components with the largest variance are the ones that predict the outcome. Nothing guarantees that: the outcome can depend on a low-variance direction (a contrast between two highly correlated variables, for instance a spread between two similar rates), and then the leading components miss it entirely, an old result known as Jolliffe’s warning. The number of components should be picked for predictive accuracy (cross-validation) and not for variance explained. Supervised alternatives avoid the problem: partial least squares chooses directions using the outcome, and ridge regression shrinks the low-variance directions only gently. Unsupervised compression before modelling is fine for input hygiene and risky as a hidden feature filter.

10High dimension dilutes distance Expert

In many dimensions intuition about distance fails. Points in a cloud become almost equally far from each other, the nearest neighbour is hardly nearer than a typical point, and the volume of a ball is concentrated near its surface. For anomaly detection the consequence is practical: an anomaly that is unusual in a few variables is hidden by the ordinary noise in the others when the distance sums all of them, so adding irrelevant variables reduces detection power even when the noise is perfectly known. Estimating the covariance makes it worse. Defences: select or build features with a reason (typologies, domain features) instead of feeding every available column; reduce dimension with a method that keeps the relevant directions; use shrunk or robust covariance; run detectors on subsets of variables and combine them; and evaluate every detector on planted anomalies of realistic size, as here, before trusting its alerts.

11Are the components stable? Expert

A principal component is an estimate, and when two eigenvalues are close its direction is poorly determined even though the plane they span is not. The sampling sd of the angle of component j is about √(λⱼλₖ/(n(λⱼ − λₖ)²)) radians for the nearest neighbouring eigenvalue λₖ, so what matters is the eigenvalue gap relative to the sampling error (North’s rule of thumb: λⱼ − λₖ should exceed λⱼ√(2/n)). Interpretations such as “the first component is size and the second is shape”, or an index built from one component, can flip between samples or periods when the gap is small. A bootstrap on the dataset in hand reveals the instability without knowing the truth. Report the stability of any component that is named or used as a score, keep the rotation fixed over time in production, and treat groups of near-equal components as a single subspace.
