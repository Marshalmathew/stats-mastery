Bootstrap & Resampling

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

The bootstrap and resampling, in depth.

When no formula exists for the standard error of your statistic, you let the computer repeat the experiment for you. Resampling is that idea: treat the data as a stand-in for the population, redraw from it thousands of times, and read uncertainty off the results. This page covers why the bootstrap works and when it fails, standard errors, bias and the jackknife, better intervals (BCa and bootstrap-t), regression bootstraps, permutation tests and their traps, Monte Carlo p-values, and the dependence structures that break naive resampling: time series, clustered accounts and paired model comparisons. It ends with the practical case most data scientists meet first: a confidence interval for a model's AUC.

Part I · The idea and its limits

1The plug-in principle Beginner

A statistic θ̂ computed from your sample varies from sample to sample; its sampling distribution is what tells you the standard error and the interval. You cannot redraw from the real population, but you do have its best available estimate, the empirical distribution F̂ that puts weight 1/n on each observed point. The bootstrap replaces the unknown F by F̂:

real world: F → sample x₁…xₙ → θ̂ (one draw of the sampling distribution) bootstrap world: F̂ → resample x₁*…xₙ* (with replacement) → θ̂* (repeat B times)

The hope is that the spread and shape of θ̂* − θ̂ around the bootstrap centre mimic those of θ̂ − θ around the truth. Notice what is not claimed: the bootstrap distribution is centred at θ̂, not at θ. It estimates spread and shape, not location. A resample of size n contains about 1 − (1 − 1/n)ⁿ → 63.2% of the distinct original points, the rest being duplicates.

When it worksFor smooth statistics (means, regression coefficients, quantiles away from ties, most estimators that are smooth functions of means) the bootstrap is consistent: its error shrinks as n grows. Chapter 3 shows where it is not.

The jackknife recomputes the statistic n times, deleting one observation each time. It is deterministic and cheap for small n, but it only uses n perturbed samples, so it fails for non-smooth statistics such as the median. The bootstrap is more general.

Bias correction is not freeThe corrected estimate 2θ̂ − mean(θ*) removes bias but adds noise, because it subtracts a noisy estimate of the bias. Unless the estimated bias is large compared with the standard error (a common rule is more than about 0.25 SE), the corrected estimator often has a larger mean squared error than the raw one.

3When the bootstrap fails Advanced

Consistency needs the statistic to depend smoothly on the data distribution. Classic failures:

  • Extremes (maximum, minimum, high quantiles near the edge): a resample can never exceed the sample maximum, so the bootstrap distribution of the max has an atom at θ̂ with probability 1 − (1 − 1/n)ⁿ ≈ 63.2%, while the real sampling distribution is continuous.
  • Heavy tails: with infinite variance (Pareto tail index ≤ 2) the sample mean is not asymptotically normal and the bootstrap cannot reproduce its behaviour.
  • Boundary parameters, non-smooth statistics and dependent data (Chapters 9–10).
  • Very small n: with n = 10 distinct values the resampling world is a poor copy of the real one.

Remedies include the m-out-of-n bootstrap or subsampling (resample fewer than n points), a parametric bootstrap from a fitted model (including extreme-value models for tails), or transforming the problem.

Part II · Better intervals and regression

4BCa and the bootstrap-t Advanced

The percentile, basic and normal intervals from the confidence-interval page have first-order accuracy: their coverage error shrinks like 1/√n. Two refinements reach second-order accuracy (error ~ 1/n):

The gain is largest for skewed statistics and small samples. For skewed data a classic symptom is unbalanced miss rates: a 95% interval should miss 2.5% on each side, but the t-interval for a skewed mean typically misses mostly on one side.

5Resampling a regression Advanced

A regression offers several things to resample, and they make different assumptions:

MethodResamplesAssumes
Pairs (case) bootstrapwhole rows (xᵢ, yᵢ)rows are iid; robust to heteroskedasticity and some misspecification
Residual bootstrapresiduals eᵢ added back to the fit: y* = ŷ + e*fixed X, homoskedastic errors and a correct mean model

Classical OLS standard errors assume constant variance. When the variance depends on x, they are wrong; heteroskedasticity-robust (HC0, “sandwich”) SEs, the pairs bootstrap and the wild bootstrap stay right, while the residual bootstrap inherits the same flaw as the classical formula.

Part III · Permutation tests

6Permutation tests Intermediate

A permutation test uses resampling without replacement to build the null distribution. If the treatment has no effect on the outcome, the group labels are arbitrary: every reassignment of the n₁ + n₂ observations to the two groups was equally likely. So shuffle the labels, recompute the statistic T many times, and ask how extreme the observed T is among the shuffles:

p = ( 1 + #{ |T*| ≥ |T_obs| } ) / ( 1 + B )

This is exact for any statistic (difference in means, medians, a t-statistic, a rank sum, an AUC), requires no distributional assumption, and is the natural analysis of a randomised experiment, where the labels really were assigned at random. The choice of statistic affects power, not validity.

7Permutation vs bootstrap tests, and the Behrens–Fisher trap Expert

The permutation null is “the two groups have the same distribution”, which is stronger than “the two groups have the same mean”. If you want to test equal means while allowing different variances, shuffling labels is not valid: under H₀ the observations are not exchangeable, because the group with the larger variance and smaller n makes extreme means more likely than the shuffles predict. The permutation test of the raw mean difference can then reject far more than 5% of the time.

Fixes: use a studentised statistic (Welch t), which is asymptotically pivotal, so the permutation distribution is approximately right even when exchangeability fails; or use a bootstrap test that first imposes H₀ (shift each group to a common mean, then resample each group separately).

Lab 7 · Type I error with unequal variances and unequal n

Both groups have true mean 0 (H₀ is true), so a valid test rejects 5% of the time. Group 1 has n₁ observations and an SD equal to the chosen multiple of group 2's SD. 1,000 data sets, 199 permutations each. Ratio 1 means equal variances.

8Monte Carlo p-values and how many shuffles you need Advanced

With B random shuffles, p̂ = (1 + #extreme)/(1 + B) is itself a random number with standard error ≈ √(p(1−p)/B). The “+1” counts the observed arrangement as one of the possible shuffles, which keeps the test valid and means p̂ can never be exactly 0. With samples as small as 10 and 10 there are only C(20,10) = 184,756 arrangements, so exact enumeration is possible; otherwise choose B by what your decision needs:

  • B = 999 or 9,999 is typical for a single p near 0.05 (SE ≈ 0.007 and 0.002).
  • To report p near 10⁻⁴ you need B well above 10⁴; to survive a Bonferroni correction over thousands of tests you may need 10⁶ or a sequential or analytic tail approximation.
  • If p̂ sits near your threshold, add more shuffles: the decision is what is noisy.

Part IV · Dependence and model metrics

9Time series: the block bootstrap Expert

Resampling single observations destroys the dependence between neighbours, so the resamples look like independent data and the bootstrap standard error comes out as if the observations were iid. For a series with positive autocorrelation that is far too small. For an AR(1) process with coefficient φ the true variance of the mean is inflated by roughly (1 + φ)/(1 − φ), so the effective sample size is n(1 − φ)/(1 + φ): with φ = 0.6, 200 observations are worth only 50.

The moving block bootstrap resamples overlapping blocks of ℓ consecutive observations and glues them together, preserving dependence within each block. Variants: circular blocks, the stationary bootstrap (random block lengths), and model-based (residual) bootstraps. Block length is a trade-off: too short and dependence is lost, too long and there are few distinct blocks. A common rule of thumb is ℓ ≈ n^{1/3}.

Lab 9 · SE of the mean of an AR(1) series by block length

The blue curve is the block-bootstrap SE of the mean averaged over 30 simulated series (200 resamples each) for each block length; block length 1 is the ordinary iid bootstrap. Green: the true SE. Dashed yellow: the n^{1/3} rule.

10Clustered data: resample the cluster Expert

Customers with many transactions, branches with many accounts, patients with repeated visits: observations inside a cluster are correlated, so the number of independent units is the number of clusters, not the number of rows. With m clusters of size k and intra-cluster correlation ρ, the variance of the grand mean is multiplied by the design effect 1 + (k − 1)ρ. Resampling individual rows ignores this and understates uncertainty. The rule: resample at the level of independence, that is, draw whole clusters with replacement and keep their rows together.

11Putting a confidence interval on a model's AUC Expert

Validation reports should state a model's AUC (or Gini, KS) with an interval, and a claim that model B beats model A needs an interval on the difference. Resampling is the standard tool, with two details:

  • Stratify by outcome when positives are rare, so that every resample has the original number of positives and negatives. Plain row resampling lets the positive count wander, which adds spurious variability.
  • Compare models on the same cases with a paired bootstrap: draw case indices once and compute both AUCs on that resample. The two models' scores are correlated, so the SE of the difference is much smaller than √(SE_A² + SE_B²), and resampling the two models independently throws that correlation away and understates how much evidence you have.

A bootstrap interval on a fixed test set measures sampling uncertainty of that test set; it does not replace out-of-sample validation, and it does not capture drift. (Analytic alternatives: Hanley–McNeil for a single AUC, DeLong for correlated AUCs.)
