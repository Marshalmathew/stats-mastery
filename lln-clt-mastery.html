LLN & Central Limit Theorem

The law of large numbers & the central limit theorem.

Two theorems hold up most of applied statistics. The law of large numbers says averages settle down. The central limit theorem says how they settle down: with a bell-shaped error whose size shrinks like 1/√n. Every confidence interval, A/B test, Monte Carlo estimate and risk number you will ever quote leans on one or both. This page builds them from the ground up, shows exactly how fast they work, and then breaks them on purpose, with heavy tails, dependent data and correlated loan portfolios, so you know when your intervals are lying.

Part I · The law of large numbers

1Averages settle down Beginner

Take independent, identically distributed draws X₁, X₂, … with true mean μ. The sample mean after n draws is X̄ₙ = (X₁ + … + Xₙ)/n. The law of large numbers (LLN) says that as n grows, X̄ₙ gets arbitrarily close to μ.

X̄ₙ → μ as n → ∞ (if E|X| < ∞)

This is why a casino is predictable while a single spin is not, and why an insurer can price a book of policies it could never price one by one. Randomness does not vanish; it averages out.

The condition that mattersThe theorem needs a finite mean. It does not need a finite variance, and it does not need normality. It does need independence (or something close to it) and identical distributions (or at least comparable ones). Chapters 5 and 6 show what happens when these fail.

2Dilution, not compensation Beginner

The “law of averages” as people say it (“I've lost five times, so a win is due”) is the gambler's fallacy. The LLN says nothing of the kind. A fair coin has no memory. Early luck is never repaid; it is merely swamped by the much larger number of later flips.

proportion of heads = ½ + (heads − tails) / (2n)

The surplus (heads − tails) does not drift back to zero; it performs a random walk whose typical size grows like √n. But the proportion is that surplus divided by 2n, and √n/n → 0. The proportion converges to ½ because the denominator grows faster than the surplus, not because the surplus is corrected.

3Weak and strong laws, and Chebyshev Intermediate

There are two standard versions of the LLN, and they differ in what kind of convergence they promise.

VersionStatementMeaning in practice
Weak LLNFor every ε > 0, P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) → 0At any fixed large n, a big miss is unlikely.
Strong LLNP(X̄ₙ → μ) = 1Along the one path you actually observe, the running average eventually settles and stays settled.

A clean proof of the weak law for finite variance comes from Chebyshev's inequality, which holds for any distribution with variance σ²:

P(|X̄ₙ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(X̄ₙ) / ε² = σ² / (n ε²)

The right side → 0 as n → ∞, so the weak law follows. Chebyshev is a guarantee, but a crude one: it must hold for the worst-behaved distribution, so it is usually far too pessimistic. The central limit theorem (Part II) gives a much sharper approximation, 2(1 − Φ(ε√n/σ)), which is accurate when n is large enough. Lab 3 puts all three side by side.

4The √n law and Monte Carlo Intermediate

The LLN tells you that X̄ₙ → μ. The next question is how fast. Because the variance of an average is σ²/n, the typical error is the standard error:

SE(X̄ₙ) = σ / √n

Error shrinks like n−1/2. This is slow: 4× the data halves the error; 100× the data buys one more correct decimal digit. It is also dimension-free, which is why Monte Carlo simulation works for integrals no grid could handle.

Classic example: throw random points in the unit square; the fraction that lands inside the quarter circle estimates π/4. Each point is a Bernoulli(π/4) trial, so the estimator 4·(fraction) has standard error 4√(p(1−p)/n) ≈ 1.642/√n.

Rule of thumb for planningIf you want an average accurate to ±m with 95% confidence, you need n ≈ (1.96 σ / m)². Estimate σ from a pilot run. Halving m quadruples n; it is always worth asking whether the extra precision changes any decision.

5When the LLN fails: heavy tails Advanced

The LLN needs a finite mean. Two distinct things can go wrong in heavy-tailed data, and it is worth keeping them separate. Take a Pareto distribution with tail index α (P(X > x) = x−α for x ≥ 1):

Tail index αMeanVarianceWhat you get
α ≤ 1infiniteinfiniteNo LLN. The running mean keeps climbing, driven by ever-larger single values.
1 < α ≤ 2finiteinfiniteLLN holds but converges slowly and erratically; the CLT in its usual √n form fails.
α > 2finitefiniteLLN and CLT both hold, though the CLT can need huge n when α is near 2.

A useful diagnostic is the largest observation's share of the total. In well-behaved data it shrinks like 1/n. In heavy-tailed data a single draw can be a large fraction of the whole sum even at n = 1,000, so the “average” is really the average of one or two events. Losses from operational risk, fraud amounts and exposure at default are often in this regime.

Practical consequenceIf a sample mean of losses jumps every time one large claim arrives, do not trust its standard error, and do not trust a normal-based confidence interval. Look at the maximum's share, use medians or trimmed means for the typical case, and model the tail separately (Stage 7 of the roadmap: extreme value theory).

6When the LLN bites slowly: dependence Advanced

With independent data, Var(X̄) = σ²/n. With dependent data that formula is wrong, and it is wrong in the dangerous direction when observations are positively correlated (transactions of one customer, daily returns in a regime, repeated measurements). For a stationary series with autocorrelation ρₖ at lag k:

Var(X̄ₙ) ≈ (σ²/n) · [1 + 2 Σₖ ρₖ] → n_eff = n / [1 + 2 Σₖ ρₖ]

For an AR(1) process, ρₖ = ρᵏ, so the bracket is (1+ρ)/(1−ρ). With ρ = 0.9 the bracket is 19: 1,000 observations are worth about 53 independent ones. The LLN still holds (the mean still converges), but far slower than the n you counted, and an interval built with the iid standard error is far too narrow.

Part II · The central limit theorem

7The theorem and the sampling distribution of the mean Beginner

The LLN says X̄ₙ lands near μ. The central limit theorem (CLT) describes the error around μ. If the Xᵢ are iid with mean μ and finite variance σ², then

Zₙ = (X̄ₙ − μ) / (σ/√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution, as n → ∞

Equivalently, X̄ₙ is approximately N(μ, σ²/n) for large n. Read it carefully:

  • It is about the distribution of the average, not of the data. Your raw data stay as skewed as they ever were.
  • The parent distribution can be almost anything with finite variance: skewed, lumpy, bimodal, discrete.
  • It says nothing about how large n must be. That depends on the parent's shape (Chapter 9).

8Why the normal? Convolutions and the proof sketch Intermediate

Adding independent variables convolves their densities: the density of X + Y is ∫f_X(t) f_Y(s − t) dt. Convolution is a smoothing operation. Each time you convolve, bumps and kinks are averaged out and the shape drifts toward a single smooth hill. The normal curve is the fixed point: convolve it with itself and, after rescaling, you get the same curve back.

The standard proof uses the characteristic function φ(t) = E[eⁱᵗˣ], because convolution of densities becomes multiplication of characteristic functions. Standardise each term so it has mean 0 and variance 1; then φ(t) = 1 − t²/2 + o(t²), and for the standardised sum Zₙ:

φ_Zₙ(t) = [ φ(t/√n) ]ⁿ = [ 1 − t²/(2n) + o(1/n) ]ⁿ → e^(−t²/2)

and e^(−t²/2) is the characteristic function of N(0,1). Only the first two moments survive the limit; everything about the parent's shape (skewness, kurtosis, bumps) is pushed into the o(1/n) remainder, which is why the CLT forgets the parent. The remainders are exactly what controls how fast (Chapter 9).

9How fast? Skewness, Berry–Esseen and interval coverage Advanced

“n ≥ 30” is folklore, not a theorem. Two results give the real picture.

1. The skewness of the mean decays like 1/√n

skew(X̄ₙ) = skew(X) / √n excess kurtosis(X̄ₙ) = excess kurt(X) / n

The mean of a very skewed parent keeps a visible skew for a long time. Exponential data (skew 2) still give skew 0.2 at n = 100.

2. Berry–Esseen: a guaranteed rate

sup ₓ |P(Zₙ ≤ x) − Φ(x)| ≤ C · E|X − μ|³ / (σ³ √n) (C ≤ 0.4748)

The error is O(1/√n), and the constant depends on the third absolute moment relative to σ³: more skewed and heavier-tailed parents need more data. If the third moment is infinite the bound is useless, which is why Pareto data with α < 3 misbehave. The green line is the lattice floor: a discrete sum has jumps, and a smooth normal can never match a jump.

3. What you actually care about: does my confidence interval cover?

The usual interval X̄ ± t·s/√n is justified by the CLT plus Slutsky's theorem (replacing the unknown σ by the estimate s does not spoil the limit, because s → σ by the LLN). Its actual coverage for finite n depends on the parent. For skewed data the errors are one-sided: a right-skewed parent usually produces samples that miss the tail, giving a mean that is too low and an interval whose upper end sits below μ.

10Normal approximations: binomial, Poisson and rare events Intermediate

A binomial count is a sum of n Bernoulli trials, so the CLT gives Bin(n, p) ≈ N(np, np(1−p)). Two refinements matter in practice.

  • Continuity correction. Counts are integers, the normal is continuous. To approximate P(X ≥ k), integrate from k − ½: P(X ≥ k) ≈ 1 − Φ((k − ½ − np)/√(np q)).
  • Rare events. When p is small the binomial is skewed and the normal is poor, especially in the tails, which is exactly where fraud, default and breach probabilities live. The Poisson(np) is usually better there (Core Distributions, Chapter 4).

A common rule of thumb is np(1−p) ≥ 10. It is a rule of thumb for the centre; far tails need more.

11The delta method: CLT for functions of averages Advanced

You rarely care about the mean itself. You care about a rate, a ratio, a log-odds, a variance. If X̄ₙ is asymptotically normal and g is smooth with g′(μ) ≠ 0, then g(X̄ₙ) is too:

√n (g(X̄ₙ) − g(μ)) → N(0, g′(μ)² σ²) so SE[g(X̄)] ≈ |g′(μ)| σ / √n

Derivation: first-order Taylor, g(X̄) ≈ g(μ) + g′(μ)(X̄ − μ), so the variance gets multiplied by g′(μ)². This is how standard errors for log-odds, log-ratios, rates (1/mean) and correlations are obtained. When g′(μ) = 0 the first-order term vanishes and the limit is not normal at the √n scale (you need a second-order expansion, giving a scaled χ²).

12The CLT in practice: proportions, rare events and sample size Intermediate

For a proportion p̂ = k/n, the CLT gives SE = √(p(1−p)/n) and the Wald interval p̂ ± 1.96·SE. The Wilson interval inverts the test instead and is far better behaved for small p or small n. The relative precision of a rate depends on how many events you see, not on n alone:

relative half-width of 95% CI ≈ 1.96 · √((1 − p) / (n p)) → events needed for ±10% ≈ 384 (1 − p)

A fraud rate of 0.2% estimated from 10,000 accounts rests on about 20 events, so its 95% interval is roughly ±45% of the estimate. Counting accounts does not help; counting events does.

Part III · When the theorems break

13Correlated portfolios: the CLT that does not arrive Expert

A bank holds N loans, each defaulting with probability p. If defaults were independent, the loss rate L = (defaults)/N would be approximately N(p, p(1−p)/N): its spread shrinks to zero as N grows, and large portfolios become safe. That is the LLN and CLT working. Now add a common economic factor with asset correlation ρ (the one-factor Vasicek model used in Basel's formulas). Conditional on the factor Z, loans are independent with default probability

p(Z) = Φ( (Φ⁻¹(p) − √ρ · Z) / √(1 − ρ) )

Averaging over many loans gives L → p(Z) as N → ∞: the idiosyncratic noise averages out (the LLN), but the common factor does not. The loss rate stays random however large the portfolio. The independent-normal approximation then understates the tail badly.

Lab 13 · Portfolio loss: independent normal vs one-factor reality

6,000 simulated portfolios. The blue curve is the CLT answer if defaults were independent. The red-dashed line marks the simulated 99th percentile, the blue-dashed line marks what the independent-normal approximation would say, and the table adds the infinite-granularity Vasicek limit.

The general lessonThe LLN and CLT are theorems about independent (or weakly dependent) averaging. Whenever your data share a common driver (a market, an economy, a fraud ring, a device, a branch), the number that matters is the effective number of independent observations, and it can be tiny compared with the number of rows. Always ask: what is n_eff?
