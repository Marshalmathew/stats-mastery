Bayesian Inference

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Bayesian inference: updating beliefs with data, and knowing what the prior is doing.

Frequentist methods answer “how would this procedure behave over repeated samples?”. Bayesian methods answer the question people usually ask: “given the data I have, what do I now believe about the parameter?”. The price is a prior, and the skill is knowing when it matters. This page builds the machinery on models where everything can be computed exactly (conjugate models, so no sampling algorithm is needed until the next page): posterior = prior × likelihood, credible intervals and their frequentist coverage, priors from flat to informative, posterior predictive checks, Bayesian regression as ridge with uncertainty, how priors wash out (or fail to), Bayes factors and the Lindley paradox, and three bank-style applications: shrinking noisy branch rates, A/B decisions with peeking, and probability of default for portfolios with almost no defaults. As always, each lab simulates data with a known truth.

Part I · Updating and priors

1Prior, likelihood, posterior Beginner

Bayes’ theorem applied to a parameter θ says that what you believe after seeing data y is what you believed before, reweighted by how well each θ explains y:

p(θ | y) ∝ p(y | θ) · p(θ) posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

The likelihood is the one used for maximum likelihood; the new ingredient is the prior p(θ), which says which values were plausible before the data. The result is a whole distribution for θ, from which point estimates (the posterior mean or median), intervals and probabilities such as P(PD > 2% | data) follow directly. The simplest case is a rate: with a Beta(a, b) prior and k successes in n trials, the posterior is exactly Beta(a + k, b + n − k). Read a and b as pseudo-counts of successes and failures that came before: the posterior mean (a + k)/(a + b + n) is a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample rate, with weight n/(n + a + b) on the data.

A different kind of statementA 95% credible interval says that, given the model and prior, θ lies in the interval with probability 0.95. A frequentist confidence interval makes no probability statement about the one interval you computed. The Bayesian statement is what people want to say, and it is conditional on the prior being reasonable.

2Conjugate models and sequential updating Intermediate

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior is in the same family, so updating is arithmetic instead of numerical integration. The three workhorses: beta–binomial for proportions (above); gamma–Poisson for counts (Gamma(a, b) prior on a Poisson rate gives Gamma(a + Σy, b + n)); and normal–normal for a mean with known variance σ². In the normal case the quantities that add are the precisions (inverse variances):

1/s₁² = 1/s₀² + n/σ² m₁ = s₁² · ( m₀/s₀² + n·ȳ/σ² )

so the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean, and the posterior is always narrower than either source. A prior of sd s₀ carries as much information as σ²/s₀² observations, a useful way to ask “is this prior sensible?”. Because the data enter through a sufficient statistic, sequential updating gives the same posterior as processing all the data at once: yesterday’s posterior is today’s prior. This is why Bayesian models suit streams of transactions and is the idea behind online credit and fraud scorecards.

3Choosing priors Intermediate

Priors come in a spectrum. Flat or “non-informative” priors (uniform on a proportion; Jeffreys’ prior, Beta(½, ½), which is invariant to reparametrisation) try to let the data speak. They are not neutral: a uniform prior on a probability is not uniform on the log-odds, and for a rare event it says that a default rate of 40% was as plausible as 1%. Weakly informative priors rule out the absurd (PD above 50% for a prime portfolio) without pretending to precision. Informative priors encode real knowledge (an earlier model, external ratings, a regulator’s benchmark) and are valuable exactly when data are scarce. Practical rules: choose the prior on a scale you can reason about, check it by simulating data from it (a prior predictive check: would these datasets look possible?), report a sensitivity analysis with two or three alternative priors, and write down where an informative prior came from. In a regulated model, the prior is a documented assumption like any other.

4Credible intervals and their coverage Intermediate

A 95% credible interval contains 95% of the posterior probability. Two standard choices: the equal-tailed interval (2.5% in each tail), easy to compute from quantiles, and the highest posterior density (HPD) interval, the shortest one, which is better for skewed posteriors and for those piled against a boundary (a PD near zero). Credible intervals answer a different question from confidence intervals, but it is fair to ask how a Bayesian interval behaves in repeated samples. Two facts frame the answer: if θ really is drawn from the prior, the credible interval covers exactly 95% on average over the prior; at one fixed θ its coverage can be higher or lower. And in standard problems with moderate n, Bayesian intervals with reasonable priors have coverage close to nominal, often better than the Wald interval, which fails badly for small proportions.

Part II · Checking, regression and model comparison

5Posterior predictive distributions and model checks Advanced

To predict a new observation ỹ you average the model’s prediction over the posterior instead of plugging in one estimate:

p(ỹ | y) = ∫ p(ỹ | θ) · p(θ | y) dθ

This is wider than the plug-in prediction because it carries parameter uncertainty as well as noise (for a Poisson rate with a gamma posterior, the predictive distribution is negative binomial). It is also the tool for checking the model. A posterior predictive check simulates replicate datasets from the fitted model, computes a statistic T that targets a feature you care about (dispersion, the share of zeros, the maximum, the autocorrelation), and compares the observed T(y) with the replicated T(y_rep). A tail probability near 0 or 1 says the model cannot reproduce that feature. Pick statistics that the model could fail: the mean is usually fitted by construction, so a check on it is uninformative. The usual predictive p-value is conservative because the data were used twice (to fit and to check), so treat extreme values as evidence and middling ones as an absence of evidence.

6Bayesian regression and its link to ridge Advanced

In a linear model y = Xβ + ε with ε ~ N(0, σ²) and a prior β ~ N(0, τ²I), the posterior of β is normal with

mean = (X′X + λI)⁻¹ X′y, covariance = σ² (X′X + λI)⁻¹, λ = σ²/τ²

The posterior mean is exactly the ridge estimate of item 14, so ridge regression is Bayesian regression with a normal prior and the penalty is the prior precision. The Bayesian view adds two things: the covariance (credible intervals for every coefficient and predictions with honest uncertainty) and a principled way to choose λ, as a statement about how large coefficients typically are. A Laplace (double-exponential) prior gives the lasso as its posterior mode, but the posterior mean under that prior is not sparse. With many predictors and few observations, a prior that matches reality can beat OLS by a wide margin, while a prior that is too tight biases every coefficient towards zero and produces intervals that are too narrow to be trusted.

7Priors, data and asymptotics Advanced

As n grows, the likelihood concentrates and, for regular models and any prior that puts positive density near the truth, the posterior converges to a normal distribution centred at the MLE with variance equal to the inverse Fisher information (the Bernstein–von Mises theorem). Credible intervals then agree with Wald confidence intervals, and the prior washes out at rate 1/n: two analysts with different reasonable priors end up in the same place. Three cautions. First, “enough data” depends on the prior’s strength: a prior worth 200 observations is still visible at n = 200. Second, a prior with probability zero on the truth never recovers, however much data arrive (Cromwell’s rule); leave a little mass everywhere you cannot rule out. Third, prior–data conflict (the data are far from where the prior put its mass) is itself a diagnostic: rather than trusting the posterior blindly, ask whether the prior or the model is wrong. A related fact: the posterior depends on the data only through the likelihood, so the stopping rule does not change it (the likelihood principle), though it does change a frequentist p-value and the long-run error rate of a decision rule built on the posterior (chapter 10).

8Bayes factors and the Lindley paradox Expert

To compare two models M₀ and M₁ the Bayesian uses the marginal likelihood p(y | M) = ∫ p(y | θ, M) p(θ | M) dθ, the probability the model gave to the data averaged over its own prior. The ratio of two marginal likelihoods is the Bayes factor and turns prior odds into posterior odds. Marginal likelihoods automatically penalise complexity (an Occam effect): a model with a diffuse prior spreads its probability over many datasets and gives little to the one observed. The corollary is uncomfortable: Bayes factors are sensitive to the prior under the alternative. For a point null H₀: μ = 0 against H₁: μ ~ N(0, τ²), with r = nτ²/σ² and a fixed z-statistic,

BF₀₁ = √(1 + r) · exp( − (z²/2) · r/(1 + r) )

which grows like √n for fixed z: a result that is “just significant” at p = 0.05 supports the null more strongly as the sample grows (the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox), because with huge n a small departure from zero would have produced a much larger z if H₁ were true. The resolution is not to prefer one framework but to be explicit: significance asks whether the data are surprising under H₀, Bayes factors ask which hypothesis predicted the data better, and with a point null the two can disagree sharply. Report the prior on the effect, and ask whether a point null is even sensible. For predictive model comparison between non-nested models, use cross-validation or information criteria such as WAIC and LOO instead of Bayes factors, which are fragile to priors.

Part III · Practice in a bank

9Empirical Bayes: borrowing strength across many units Expert

A bank has hundreds of branches, merchants or segments, each with its own default or fraud rate and very different volumes. The raw rate of a small unit is noisy; ranking units by raw rates puts the small ones at both ends. Empirical Bayes treats the units’ true rates as draws from a common distribution (here a Beta(a, b)), estimates that distribution from all the units, and then applies the ordinary Bayesian update unit by unit: each estimate is (a + x)/(a + b + n), a shrinkage of the raw rate towards the overall mean, stronger for small n. It is the first step towards the full hierarchical (partial pooling) models of item 28, which also account for the uncertainty in a and b that empirical Bayes ignores. The same logic is the fix for the winner’s curse met earlier: after selecting the most extreme units, their raw rates overstate the truth, while shrunken estimates do not.

10Bayesian A/B testing, decisions and peeking Expert

With a beta–binomial model for each arm, the posterior of the difference gives the quantities decision-makers ask for: P(B > A | data), the posterior of the lift, and the expected loss of choosing each arm (the average amount you give up if the choice turns out wrong). A region of practical equivalence (ROPE) turns “is there any difference?” into “is the difference big enough to matter?”, and informative priors from earlier tests (shrinking implausible lifts) make small tests behave. All of these are valid summaries of what the data say now. What is not automatic is the error rate of a stopping rule: “run until P(B > A) > 95%” is a sequential procedure, and checking the posterior at every look gives it far more chances to cross than a single look has. Bayesian updating is unaffected by when you stop, but the long-run false-win rate of the rule is, so tune the threshold (and the number of looks) by simulation under the null, as for the group-sequential and always-valid methods of the experiments page. Choose actions by expected loss against a business cost, and do not read P(B > A) as a guarantee.

11Low-default portfolios and documented priors Expert

Highly rated corporates, banks and sovereigns produce very few defaults, so the maximum-likelihood PD is often exactly zero and a data-only bound is wide. A Bayesian treatment is natural: a beta prior built from external information (agency default studies, a pooled internal history, a regulator’s benchmark), updated with the portfolio’s own record, yields a positive posterior mean and a defensible upper bound. A data-only alternative, the most prudent estimation style bound, takes the upper confidence limit given zero defaults (about 3/n at 95%) and lets the user choose the percentile. Either way, the answer carries an assumption that must be governed: the source and period of the external data and why it applies, the prior strength (how many obligors it is “worth”), a sensitivity table over plausible priors, a back-test of the posterior against later defaults, and how the result flows downstream (use the posterior distribution, not just its mean, in stress tests). Reviewers will ask what the number would be under a prior twice as strong or centred somewhere else, and the lab shows the answer.
