Calibration and proper scoring rules: are your probabilities honest?

A model that says “8% default risk” should be right about 8 of 100 such cases. Ranking well is not enough: pricing, provisioning, capital and any threshold rule use the probability itself. This page teaches what calibration means, how to measure it (reliability diagrams, Brier decomposition, calibration slope and intercept, ECE), why models miscalibrate, how to repair it (Platt, isotonic, base-rate adjustment), why proper scoring rules are the right way to compare probability forecasts, and how to test and monitor calibration without fooling yourself. Every lab simulates forecasts from a known truth so you can see exactly what the diagnostics show.

Part I · Forecasts and scores

1What calibration means Beginner

A probability forecast q for a binary outcome Y is calibrated if, among all cases that receive forecast q, the fraction with Y = 1 is q:

P(Y = 1 | q) = q for every value q the model issues

Calibration is separate from discrimination (does the model rank positives above negatives, measured by AUC). A forecast can have a perfect ranking and terrible probabilities: multiply every probability by three and the AUC does not move. The standard picture is the reliability diagram: group cases by forecast, plot the average forecast against the observed frequency in each group, and compare with the 45° line. Points below the line mean the model over-predicts (forecasts too high); points above, under-predicts. A curve that is flatter than the diagonal at the ends (high forecasts too high, low forecasts too low) is over-confident; steeper, under-confident.

Levels of calibration, from weakest to strongest: in the large (average forecast equals the event rate), weak (calibration intercept 0 and slope 1), moderate (the whole curve follows the diagonal) and strong (calibrated within every subgroup, see chapter 9).

2Proper scoring rules Intermediate

A scoring rule S(q, y) assigns a loss to forecast q when the outcome is y. It is proper if, when the true probability is p, the expected loss is smallest when you report q = p; strictly proper if that is the only minimiser. Proper rules reward honesty, so minimising them (or choosing a model by them) pushes forecasts toward the truth. The common rules are:

Brier: (q − y)² Log-loss: −y log q − (1 − y) log(1 − q) Spherical: −[y q + (1−y)(1−q)] / √(q² + (1−q)²)

When the true probability is p, the expected Brier score of forecast q is p(1 − p) + (q − p)²: noise plus squared miscalibration. The expected log-loss is H(p) + KL(p ‖ q): entropy plus a Kullback–Leibler distance. Both are minimised at q = p. In contrast, accuracy (loss 1 if the thresholded forecast is wrong) and the absolute error |q − y| are improper: their expected loss is minimised by reporting 0 or 1 whenever p ≠ ½, so they cannot be used to tune probabilities. Optimising an improper rule tells the model to exaggerate.

3Brier score decomposition Advanced

If forecasts take K distinct values (or are grouped into K bins and replaced by the bin average), the Brier score splits exactly (Murphy, 1973):

BS = REL − RES + UNC
REL = Σₖ (nₖ/n)(fₖ − oₖ)² (reliability: miscalibration, lower is better)
RES = Σₖ (nₖ/n)(oₖ − ō)² (resolution: how far the bin outcomes spread from the base rate, higher is better)
UNC = ō(1 − ō) (uncertainty of the outcome: fixed by the data)

Calibration is REL; discrimination-like information is RES; UNC is the score of always forecasting the base rate ō. The Brier skill score BSS = 1 − BS/UNC measures improvement over that climatology. A model can reduce BS either by being better calibrated or by having more resolution; the decomposition tells you which. A perfectly calibrated forecast has REL = 0 and BS = UNC − RES.

4Measuring miscalibration: ECE, slope and intercept Advanced

Two summaries dominate practice. The expected calibration error is the bin-weighted average gap, ECE = Σₖ (nₖ/n)|fₖ − oₖ|. It is easy to explain but depends on the binning, and it is biased upward in small samples: even a perfectly calibrated model shows a positive ECE, because observed rates in small bins are noisy. The calibration slope and intercept come from the recalibration model logit P(Y = 1) = α + β·logit(q). Perfect weak calibration is α = 0, β = 1. A slope β < 1 means the forecasts are too extreme (typical of overfitting, chapter 5); β > 1 means too timid. The intercept (read with the slope fixed at 1) shows the average over- or under-prediction. Both come with standard errors, so you can say whether a departure is more than noise.

A smooth, flexible calibration curve (for example a spline or LOESS fit of y on q) is usually better than fixed bins, and should be shown with a confidence band. Report the slope, the intercept, the E/O ratio (expected over observed events), and a curve; use ECE only as a rough summary.

Part II · Why it breaks, how to fix it

5Why models become miscalibrated Advanced

Common causes: Overfitting (including too many predictors for the sample): the fitted logit is too extreme, so out-of-sample forecasts are over-confident and the calibration slope is below 1. Heavy regularisation: shrinkage pulls probabilities toward the base rate, so the model becomes under-confident (slope above 1). Class weights, over- or under-sampling: shift the intercept so that the average forecast no longer matches the real event rate (chapter 7). Model family: naive Bayes and some boosted or deep models are over-confident; random forests and SVMs tend to be under-confident near 0 and 1; tree ensembles pull forecasts toward the middle. Dataset shift: the base rate or relationships change after development. A model that optimises log-loss with a sufficiently flexible link is typically well calibrated on the training data and loses calibration on new data, so calibration must be assessed on data not used for fitting.

6Recalibration: Platt, temperature and isotonic Expert

Recalibration learns a monotone map from the raw score to a probability, using data not used to fit the model. Platt scaling fits a logistic regression of y on the score’s logit (two parameters: intercept and slope; fixing the intercept at 0 gives temperature scaling, popular for neural nets). Isotonic regression fits a monotone step function (pool-adjacent-violators); it can correct any monotone distortion but needs enough data (hundreds of events) and produces a step function with ties, which slightly reduces AUC. Beta calibration is a flexible three-parameter parametric alternative. Use a separate calibration set or cross-fitting; re-using the training data repeats the overfitting you are trying to repair. Monotone maps leave the ranking (AUC) unchanged except for ties.

7Base-rate shift and calibration in the large Expert

A model trained when the event rate was π and deployed when it is π′ is miscalibrated in the large even if nothing else changed. When only the base rate moves (label or prior shift: P(x | y) unchanged), the correct update is a shift of the log-odds:

logit q′ = logit q + log[ (π′/(1 − π′)) / (π/(1 − π)) ]

This is the same correction as for training on a resampled (for example under-sampled) data set: the model learned the training odds, so you add back the difference between the true and the training log-odds. The shift fixes the intercept and leaves the ranking unchanged. If the relationship P(y | x) itself changed (covariate or concept drift) a simple shift is not enough and the model has to be re-fitted or recalibrated on recent data. In credit risk this is the distinction between point-in-time forecasts, through-the-cycle averages and the long-run default rate.

Part III · Choosing, segmenting, deciding, testing

8Choosing a rule: Brier, log-loss, AUC and accuracy Advanced

All strictly proper rules are minimised by the true probability, but they weigh errors differently. Brier is bounded (a confident wrong forecast costs at most 1) and treats errors of the same size alike. Log-loss is unbounded: a forecast of 0.001 for an event that happens costs about 6.9, so it punishes confident mistakes severely, is the likelihood (the basis of AIC and of fitting), and is the more sensitive rule for rare events and tails. AUC, Gini, KS and accuracy depend only on ranking or on a threshold, so they are blind to monotone distortions: two forecasts that differ by an over-confident transformation have identical AUC. Use a proper rule for anything where the probability is used, and compare with the skill relative to a baseline (BSS, or the log-score improvement over the base rate).

9Calibration within subgroups Expert

A model can be calibrated overall and badly calibrated in every segment: the errors of opposite sign cancel in the average. If a segment (product, region, vintage, protected group) has a different base rate than the model assumes, the pooled intercept is too low for one group and too high for the other. This matters for pricing fairness, for regulatory scrutiny of rating grades, and for portfolio provisioning by segment. Check calibration-in-the-large and the slope by segment, show reliability curves by segment, and either add the segment (or a feature that captures it) to the model or recalibrate separately. The more segments you slice, the noisier each estimate is, so use intervals and avoid reading noise.

10Decisions: thresholds, expected cost and net benefit Expert

If a false positive costs c_FP and a false negative c_FN, the cost-minimising rule acts when the probability exceeds t* = c_FP/(c_FP + c_FN) — but only if the probabilities are calibrated. The net benefit of acting on all cases with q ≥ t is NB(t) = TP/n − (FP/n)·t/(1 − t), which values a false positive at the odds of the threshold; plotted against t it gives a decision curve (compared with “act on everyone” and “act on no one”). With miscalibrated forecasts, the cases that clear the threshold are not the cases with the right risk, and the curve falls below that of the recalibrated model. When only the ranking is used (investigate the top 5% by capacity), calibration is irrelevant; when amounts are used (expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD for provisioning, pricing, capital, insurance premiums) calibration is essential.

11Testing calibration and monitoring it Expert

Formal tests help only when read correctly. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test groups cases into g bands (usually deciles of the forecast) and compares observed and expected events: X² = Σ (Oₖ − Eₖ)² / [nₖ π̄ₖ(1 − π̄ₖ)], with about g − 2 degrees of freedom for a model fitted on the same data (g for external forecasts). Spiegelhalter’s test standardises the Brier score: Z = (BS − E[BS]) / SD[BS] with E[BS] = mean of q(1 − q) and Var = (1/n²) Σ q(1 − q)(1 − 2q)² under calibration. For rating grades, regulators typically look at a binomial test of the observed default rate against the grade’s PD (with adjustments for default correlation), and traffic-light rules. All of them share the same problem: with large n they reject trivial miscalibration, with small n they cannot detect serious miscalibration. Report the calibration slope and intercept with intervals and a curve, and treat tests as alarms, not as proof. For monitoring, track the E/O ratio, slope and Brier or log-loss over time with control limits.
