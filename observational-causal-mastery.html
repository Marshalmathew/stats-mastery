Observational Causal Methods

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Observational causal methods: matching, weighting, difference-in-differences, instruments, regression discontinuity and synthetic control.

Often no experiment is possible: a bank cannot randomise who gets a loan, a rule change applies to every branch, a score cut-off was set years ago. The previous page showed what randomisation buys; this page shows what can be recovered without it and what each method has to assume. Methods that rely on observed confounders (matching, propensity-score weighting, doubly robust estimators) need ignorability and overlap. Designs that exploit structure (before-and-after with a comparison group, an instrument, a threshold, a weighted set of donors) trade that assumption for another, such as parallel trends, exclusion or continuity. Every lab simulates data with a known true effect, so the bias of each estimator, and what breaks it, can be measured.

Part I · Selection on observables

1Ignorability, overlap and the propensity score Beginner

If every common cause of treatment T and outcome Y is measured in X, and each customer type has some chance of either treatment, comparing like with like identifies the effect. Formally: ignorability (unconfoundedness) (Y(0), Y(1)) ⟂ T | X, and overlap (positivity) 0 < e(x) < 1 where e(x) = P(T = 1 | X = x) is the propensity score. Rosenbaum and Rubin showed that conditioning on the scalar e(X) is as good as conditioning on all of X, so a high-dimensional adjustment becomes a one-dimensional model of treatment assignment. The propensity score is a balancing score: within strata of e(X) the covariates have the same distribution in both groups, which can be checked directly (a diagnostic that outcome regression does not offer).

Check balance with standardised mean differences SMD = (x̄₁ − x̄₀)/√((s₁² + s₀²)/2) before and after adjustment (below 0.1 is a common standard), and look at the overlap of the propensity-score distributions. Neither check can reveal an unmeasured confounder: ignorability is an assumption about what is not in the data.

2Matching Intermediate

Matching pairs each treated customer with one or more controls who look alike, then compares outcomes within pairs; the estimand is usually the ATT. Matching can be done on covariates (Mahalanobis distance), on the propensity score (usually its logit), or both. Choices that matter: with or without replacement (replacement reduces bias, increases variance), number of neighbours, caliper (maximum allowed distance; treated customers with no close control are dropped, which changes the population the estimate refers to), and exact matching on key variables. Outcomes are not used in matching, so the design can be fixed before the effect is estimated. Matching is not magic: it removes bias only from observed covariates, bias-corrected versions adjust for residual imbalance, and the usual standard error is invalid because the matches are estimated (use the Abadie–Imbens or bootstrap-with-care approaches).

3Inverse-probability weighting and doubly robust estimation Intermediate

Inverse-probability weighting (IPW) re-weights customers so that treated and control groups each resemble the whole population: a treated customer with e(x) = 0.2 stands in for five like her. The normalised (Hájek) estimator is τ̂ = Σ wᵢ Yᵢ T / Σ wᵢ T − Σ wᵢ Yᵢ (1 − T) / Σ wᵢ (1 − T) with weights 1/e(Xᵢ) and 1/(1 − e(Xᵢ)). It needs a correct propensity model; the outcome regression (g-computation) estimator needs a correct outcome model. The augmented IPW (AIPW, doubly robust) estimator combines both:

τ̂_AIPW = mean[ μ̂₁(X) − μ̂₀(X) + T(Y − μ̂₁(X))/ê(X) − (1 − T)(Y − μ̂₀(X))/(1 − ê(X)) ]

It is consistent if either model is right, and with flexible machine-learning nuisance models plus cross-fitting (double/debiased machine learning) it attains the efficient rate under weaker conditions. Doubly robust does not mean doubly safe: if both are wrong, the bias remains, and nothing here cures an unmeasured confounder.

4Overlap, extreme weights and trimming Advanced

When some customer types almost never receive one of the treatments (e(x) near 0 or 1), the data contain little or no information about the counterfactual for them; weights 1/e explode, one or two observations dominate the estimate, and the variance becomes huge. The effective sample size (Σw)²/Σw² shows how many equally weighted customers the weighted sample is worth. Responses: (1) check the overlap plot and report it; (2) trim units with ê outside [α, 1 − α], which stabilises the estimate but changes the estimand to the effect in the overlap region (the answer to a different question, and with heterogeneous effects a different number); (3) use overlap weights (weight e(1 − e), which target the population with most overlap); (4) accept that the effect cannot be learned for non-overlapping customers rather than extrapolating from a model. In credit this is the old problem that the approved population does not span the applicants.

Part II · Panels and instruments

5Difference-in-differences Advanced

When a policy applies to one group (branches in one region, customers on one product) from a known date, compare the change over time in the treated group with the change in an untreated group. Unit-level, time-invariant differences (fixed effects) drop out of the change, and common shocks drop out of the difference of changes:

τ̂_DiD = (Ȳ_treated,post − Ȳ_treated,pre) − (Ȳ_control,post − Ȳ_control,pre)

The identifying assumption is parallel trends: without the policy, the treated group would have moved like the control group. It concerns an unobservable counterfactual; the pre-period can be inspected (an event-study plot of group differences in each pre-period should be flat) but passing a pre-trend test does not prove it, and low power can make a failing trend look flat. Other requirements: no anticipation, no spillover to controls, stable composition of the groups. A level difference between groups is harmless; a difference in trends is fatal and no covariate adjustment cures it unless the trend difference is explained by those covariates.

6Staggered adoption and the two-way fixed effects problem Expert

With units adopting a policy at different dates, the habitual regression Y_it = α_i + λ_t + β D_it + ε_it (two-way fixed effects, TWFE) is no longer a clean comparison. TWFE is a weighted average of many 2×2 DiD comparisons, including ones that use already-treated units as controls for later-treated units. If effects change over time since treatment (build up, fade), those comparisons subtract part of the earlier cohort’s effect: some weights on cohort-time effects become negative and the estimate can be too small, or even of the wrong sign, although every individual effect is positive.

The remedy is to estimate cohort-and-period specific effects ATT(g, t) from clean comparisons (each cohort against never-treated or not-yet-treated units, relative to the period before adoption; Callaway–Sant’Anna, Sun–Abraham, stacked designs, imputation estimators) and then aggregate them as you choose: by event time, by cohort, or overall. Plot the event study with these estimators, not with TWFE. With a constant effect that appears at once and parallel trends, TWFE is fine; the problem is heterogeneity in time and across cohorts.

7Instrumental variables Advanced

If unmeasured confounding drives both treatment X and outcome Y, regression is biased. An instrument Z shifts X but affects Y only through X. Three conditions: relevance (Z moves X), independence (Z is as good as randomly assigned relative to the unmeasured confounders) and exclusion (no direct path Z → Y). The IV (Wald) estimate is the reduced form divided by the first stage:

β̂_IV = Cov(Z, Y) / Cov(Z, X) = (effect of Z on Y) / (effect of Z on X) two-stage least squares: regress X on Z, then Y on the fitted X̂

With heterogeneous effects, IV estimates the local average treatment effect (LATE) for the “compliers” whose treatment is moved by the instrument, under monotonicity. It is not the ATE and it changes with the instrument. Banking instruments in the literature: random assignment of loan officers or reviewers with different strictness (judge-style designs), quasi-random timing of marketing waves, distance to a branch (usually questionable), policy-induced eligibility. The first-stage strength is a number to report: the F-statistic.

8Weak instruments and exclusion violations Expert

An IV estimate is a ratio, and a ratio with a noisy denominator misbehaves. When the first stage is weak (F below about 10), the IV estimator is biased towards OLS, has heavy tails and an unreliable confidence interval; the conventional t-interval undercovers badly. Use the weak-instrument-robust Anderson–Rubin interval, report the first-stage F (effective F for robust standard errors), and prefer fewer strong instruments to many weak ones. Even a strong instrument fails if exclusion does not hold: a small direct effect ζ of Z on Y adds ζ/π to the IV estimate, and a weaker first stage π inflates the same violation. The exclusion restriction cannot be tested with one instrument; with several, over-identification tests only check whether they agree with each other, not that all are valid. Sensitivity analyses ask how large ζ must be to overturn the result.

Part III · Thresholds, synthetic control and practice

9Regression discontinuity Advanced

If treatment switches on at a threshold of a running variable (a score of 650 for approval, an income limit for an offer, age 65), customers just either side of the cut-off are nearly identical, so the jump in the outcome at the threshold is a causal effect at the threshold. In the sharp design the estimate is the difference between two regression intercepts at the cut-off, from local linear fits within a bandwidth h:

τ̂_RD = lim_{x↓c} E[Y | X = x] − lim_{x↑c} E[Y | X = x] (local linear, separate slopes on each side, kernel-weighted)

The assumption is continuity of the potential-outcome regressions at c (nothing else jumps there). The bias-variance trade-off is set by the bandwidth: a narrow window has little bias but few observations; a wide one has precise but biased estimates when the outcome curve is not straight. Use data-driven bandwidths (Imbens–Kalyanaraman, Calonico–Cattaneo–Titiunik with bias-corrected robust intervals), local linear rather than high-order global polynomials, and show the binned scatter. The effect applies at the cut-off only; extrapolating to customers far from it needs more assumptions. Credit-score cut-offs are textbook cases, provided the score is not manipulated (next chapter).

10Fuzzy designs and manipulation of the running variable Expert

In a fuzzy design crossing the threshold changes the probability of treatment rather than switching it on for sure (an offer is made above the score but not everyone accepts; some below get exceptions). The estimate is the Wald ratio of the jump in the outcome to the jump in treatment at the cut-off, which is an IV estimate with “above the cut-off” as the instrument, and gives the effect for compliers at the threshold. The central threat is manipulation: if customers, branch staff or applicants can push the running variable just over the line, those just above are no longer comparable with those just below. Diagnose with a density test (McCrary; Cattaneo–Jansson–Ma): bunching just above the cut-off is a warning. Also check that pre-treatment covariates do not jump at the cut-off, vary the bandwidth, try placebo cut-offs, and exclude a donut hole around the threshold.

11Synthetic control and choosing a method Expert

When one unit (a region, a branch network, a product) is treated and many untreated donors exist, the synthetic control method builds a weighted average of donors, with non-negative weights summing to one, that tracks the treated unit’s outcome path before the intervention; the post-period gap is the effect estimate. Weights are chosen to minimise the pre-period fit error, so the synthetic unit matches both levels and the dynamics of unobserved factors. Inference is by placebo permutation: apply the method to each donor as if it were treated and compare the treated unit’s post/pre error ratio with theirs. It fails when the treated unit lies outside the donors’ convex hull (no weighted average can match it), when the pre-period fit is poor, or when donors are themselves affected.

Choosing among methods: randomise if you can; use matching, weighting or doubly robust estimation if you believe you measured the confounders and overlap is good; use DiD when you have a comparison group with parallel trends and a before-and-after; synthetic control for few treated units and rich pre-period data; IV when a credible instrument exists (and report the LATE); RD when a sharp rule assigns treatment. In every case document the assumption, its diagnostics and a sensitivity analysis, and where possible compare two methods that rely on different assumptions: agreement is weak evidence, disagreement is informative.
