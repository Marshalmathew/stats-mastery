Collinearity, interactions and nonlinearity.

Real relationships are rarely a clean straight line with independent predictors. This page covers what happens when predictors overlap (multicollinearity), when the effect of one variable depends on another (interactions), and when the shape of the relationship is not a line (polynomials, splines, binning). You will see exactly what each does to coefficients, standard errors, predictions and power, and how to choose flexibility by cross-validation instead of by eye.

Part I · Collinearity

1What collinearity does, and what it does not Beginner

Two predictors are collinear when they carry much of the same information. By Frisch–Waugh–Lovell, the coefficient on x₁ comes from the part of x₁ that the other predictors cannot explain; the more x₂ explains x₁, the less variation is left, and the coefficient is determined by less information. For two predictors with correlation ρ:

Var(β̂₁) = σ² / [ Sxx₁ · (1 − ρ²) ] standard error inflation = 1 / √(1 − ρ²)

At ρ = 0.9 the standard error is 2.3 times larger; at ρ = 0.99 it is 7 times larger. Crucially, collinearity inflates variance, not bias: the estimates stay centred on the truth, they are just unstable. It also does not hurt predictions made inside the cloud of observed data, nor the joint effect of the correlated predictors, nor the coefficients of unrelated predictors. It hurts the question “which one of them is responsible?”.

2VIF, tolerance and perfect collinearity Intermediate

With many predictors, pairwise correlations miss overlaps that involve three or more variables. The variance inflation factor of predictor j is VIFⱼ = 1/(1 − Rⱼ²), where Rⱼ² is the R² from regressing xⱼ on all the other predictors. Tolerance is 1/VIF. A VIF of 10 (R² = 0.9) is the traditional alarm level, and the standard error of that coefficient is √VIF times what it would be with uncorrelated predictors. The condition number of the scaled design matrix summarises the same problem for the whole model.

Perfect collinearity (an exact linear relation, such as including all dummies of a category along with the intercept, or a percentage and its complement) makes XᵀX singular: the coefficients are not identified and software drops a column. Near-perfect collinearity gives enormous standard errors and wildly changing signs between samples.

Lab 2 · VIF of a predictor built from others

x₁ and x₂ are independent; x₃ = a·x₁ + b·x₂ + noise. Raise the weights or shrink the noise and watch all three VIFs rise: a variable that is nearly a combination of two others drags them all in, even though no pair is highly correlated.

3Individually insignificant, jointly significant Advanced

When two collinear predictors both matter, each t-test can be insignificant while the joint F-test is overwhelming: the data know that something in the pair drives y but cannot say which. Dropping one because its p-value is large then removes a real effect and biases the other (omitted-variable bias with δ near 1). What to do depends on the question:

Prediction: ignore the collinearity, or use ridge or principal-components regression. Inference on the group: test the predictors jointly. Inference on each one: you need more data, a design that breaks the correlation, or a subject-matter reason to combine them into one index or to constrain their coefficients. Do not use stepwise deletion, which hides the instability and makes the p-values invalid.

Lab 3 · The joint test sees what the t-tests miss

Part II · Interactions

4Interactions, main effects and centring Intermediate

An interaction lets the effect of one variable depend on another: y = β₀ + β₁x + β₂d + β₃(x·d) + ε. Now β₁ is the slope of x when d = 0, β₂ is the effect of d when x = 0, and β₃ is the difference in slopes between the groups. The “main effect” coefficients lose their usual meaning: they describe a particular point (the reference group, x = 0). If x = 0 is outside the data (age 0, income 0), β₂ is an extrapolation with a huge standard error even when the effect of d across the actual data is clear.

Centring x at its mean, x* = x − x̄, makes β₂ the effect of d at the average x and usually makes the main effects both interpretable and better determined. The fitted model is identical; only the parametrisation changes. Never interpret a main effect in a model with an interaction without saying at which value of the other variable.

Lab 4 · Two groups, two slopes

x ranges from 2 to 8. True model: y = 1 + 0.5x + b_d·d + b_x·x·d + noise. Choose whether x is centred. The fitted lines never change, but the coefficient on d changes from “the gap at x = 0” (outside the data) to “the gap at the mean of x”.

5Continuous interactions and marginal effects Advanced

With two continuous variables, y = β₀ + β₁x + β₂z + β₃xz + ε, the effect of x is a function of z: ∂y/∂x = β₁ + β₃z. The right way to report it is the marginal effect across the range of z with a confidence band, not a table of coefficients. The variance uses the covariance matrix of the estimates:

Var(β̂₁ + β̂₃z) = Var(β̂₁) + z² Var(β̂₃) + 2z Cov(β̂₁, β̂₃)

The band is narrowest near the middle of z’s data (where it should be, after centring) and widens at the edges. “Significant” differs between regions: x may matter for high z but not for low z, and the point where the band crosses zero is a finding in itself, but also an estimate with uncertainty. Interaction terms are strongly collinear with their parts unless centred; centring changes the coefficients and SEs of the lower-order terms, never the fit or the marginal-effect curve.

Lab 5 · The marginal effect curve with its band

y = 1 + 0.5x + 0.5z + b·xz + noise, x and z standard normal. The line is the estimated effect of x at each value of z; the band is a 95% CI; the dashed line is the truth. Shrink n to see how wide the band gets away from z = 0.

6Interactions are expensive to detect Expert

In a randomised experiment with a binary treatment T and a binary subgroup G, the main effect of T is estimated from all n units, but the difference in treatment effects between subgroups compares two half-sized estimates: its standard error is twice as large as that of the main effect, so detecting an interaction of the same size needs about four times the sample, and with unequal groups even more. Underpowered subgroup analyses therefore produce exaggerated or sign-flipped interaction estimates (Type M and S errors from the power page) that are widely reported and rarely replicate.

Practical rules: pre-specify a small number of subgroup hypotheses on subject-matter grounds; plan the sample size for the interaction, not the main effect; show the interaction estimate with its interval rather than separate significant and non-significant subgroup p-values (“the difference between significant and not significant is not itself significant”).

Part III · Functional form

7Polynomials: flexible, global and dangerous Intermediate

A quadratic or cubic term is the easiest way to add curvature, and the model is still linear in the coefficients, so OLS applies. But a polynomial is global: every coefficient affects the fit everywhere, so a high degree that bends to match the data at one end swings wildly at the other, and beyond the observed range it explodes. Degrees above 3 or 4 are rarely justified; the raw powers x, x², x³ are also highly collinear (centre or use orthogonal polynomials), and the individual coefficients have no useful interpretation: report fitted curves and marginal effects instead.

Lab 7 · Raising the degree

The truth (dashed) is a smooth curve on x ∈ [−1, 1]. Increase the polynomial degree: training error always falls, test error is U-shaped, and the fit outside the data (the grey shaded zones) diverges.

8Piecewise models and splines Advanced

A spline fits low-degree pieces joined at knots, so the flexibility is local: a bump in one region does not move the fit elsewhere. The piecewise-linear (hinge) basis adds a term (x − κ)₊ per knot, giving a continuous line that changes slope at each knot. A cubic regression spline uses (x − κ)₊³ terms, giving a smooth curve. A step function (binning) is the crudest: a constant per interval, discontinuous at the edges. All are ordinary regressions on constructed columns, so standard errors, tests and prediction intervals carry over; the number of knots is the tuning parameter.

Lab 8 · Three bases, one wiggly truth

Truth: f(x) = 2 sin(0.9x) + 0.3x on [0, 10] (dashed). Pick a basis and a number of knots. Compare the fitted curve and the error on 1,000 new points.

9Choosing flexibility by cross-validation Advanced

Training error always prefers more flexibility, so it cannot choose the number of knots or the degree. K-fold cross-validation splits the data into K parts, fits on K−1 of them, predicts the held-out part, and averages the squared errors: an honest estimate of out-of-sample error for each candidate. Choose the model minimising it, or the simplest one within one standard error of the minimum (the “one-standard-error rule”, which favours smoother fits). Remember that the CV curve is itself noisy, and that a selection made by CV and then tested on the same data needs an outer holdout.

Lab 9 · The cross-validation curve

10Seeing curvature: partial-residual plots Advanced

With several predictors, a plot of y against one of them is muddied by the others. The component-plus-residual (partial-residual) plot adds each point’s residual back to the fitted part for the predictor of interest, e + β̂ⱼxⱼ, and plots it against xⱼ: if the relationship is truly linear the cloud follows the line; a curve or a kink shows exactly which transformation is needed. A smooth through the points (here, binned averages) makes the shape visible. The residual-versus-fitted plot says that something is wrong, the partial-residual plot says where.

11The cost of chopping a continuous variable Expert

Splitting a continuous predictor into groups (above or below the median, quartiles, “high / low”) is convenient and costly. It discards within-group information: a median split of a linear effect keeps only about 64% of the information (a loss equal to dropping 36% of the sample), tertiles about 81% and quartiles about 88%. It makes results depend on arbitrary cut points, assumes a step where the truth is smooth, and when the binned variable is a confounder it leaves residual confounding within bins. If a shape is unknown, a spline is better than bins on every count.
