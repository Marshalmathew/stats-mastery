Hypothesis testing, power and experimental design.

The p-value course taught how to read a test result. This page is about the other half: designing the study so that a real effect has a fair chance of being found, and knowing what to believe when it is or is not. It covers the Neyman–Pearson framework, power and minimum detectable effects, sample-size planning, effect sizes, the post-hoc power fallacy, Type S and Type M errors, variance reduction (CUPED), allocation, cluster-randomised designs, group-sequential monitoring, and power for heavy-tailed business metrics. It is the toolkit behind every A/B test, champion-challenger comparison and pilot you will ever run.

Part I · Power

1Anatomy of a test Beginner

A test chooses between a null hypothesis H₀ (no effect) and an alternative H₁ by computing a statistic and rejecting H₀ when it falls in a rejection region. Two error rates describe it:

H₀ trueH₁ true
Reject H₀Type I error, probability α (the size)Correct: probability 1 − β = power
Keep H₀Correct: probability 1 − αType II error, probability β

α is chosen in advance and controls false alarms; power is a property of the design (sample size, noise, true effect, α). In the standard z-test of a mean, the statistic is N(0,1) under H₀ and N(λ, 1) under H₁ with noncentrality λ = δ/SE = d·√n, the true effect measured in standard errors. Power is the area of the H₁ curve beyond the critical value.

The Neyman–Pearson lemma says that for a simple null against a simple alternative, the likelihood-ratio test has the highest power among all tests of the same size. For one-sided problems in common families the same test is uniformly most powerful; for two-sided problems no uniformly best test exists, which is why practical tests are built from likelihood-ratio, Wald or score statistics (see the p-value course, chapter 7).

2The power function and the minimum detectable effect Intermediate

Power is not one number but a function of the true effect. At d = 0 it equals α; it rises with d, with n, with α, and with lower noise. Four levers, in order of how freely you can pull them: sample size n, noise (variance reduction, Chapter 7), α (rarely negotiable), and the effect itself (not yours to choose). When effects are small, you reach for the first two.

The minimum detectable effect (MDE) is the smallest true effect with a chosen power (usually 80%). For a two-sided test at α = 0.05 with 80% power, the true effect must be about (1.96 + 0.84) = 2.8 standard errors. That is a useful mental yardstick: if your standard error is 1 percentage point, an experiment can reliably detect lifts of about 2.8 points and above.

With σ estimated, the test is a t-test and power comes from the noncentral t distribution; the loss versus the z-test matters only at small n.

3Sample-size planning from power Intermediate

Solve the power equation for n. For a two-sided test with standardised effect d:

two groups, n per group: n = 2 (z_{1−α/2} + z_{power})² / d² one sample: n = (z_{1−α/2} + z_{power})² / d² two proportions p₁, p₂: n = ( z_{1−α/2}·√(2p̄q̄) + z_{power}·√(p₁q₁ + p₂q₂) )² / (p₁ − p₂)²

Four practical rules. (1) Plan for the smallest effect worth acting on, not the effect you hope for. (2) Required n grows like 1/d²: halving the effect quadruples n. (3) Moving from 80% to 90% power multiplies n by about 1.34. (4) Inflate for attrition, non-compliance and unusable units. For non-inferiority the test is one-sided with a margin, so α is not split across two tails.

Part II · Effect sizes and common misreadings

4Effect sizes: saying how big, in a unit people understand Intermediate

5The “observed power” fallacy Advanced

A tempting post-mortem for a non-significant result is to compute the “power” of the test at the effect you observed, and conclude “power was high, so the null is probably true” or “power was low, so the result is meaningless”. Both inferences fail. Observed power is a one-to-one function of the p-value (it only re-expresses it), so it adds no information. A result with p = 0.05 always has observed power of about 50%; any p above 0.05 gives below 50%. A non-significant result with “high observed power” cannot occur.

The right post-hoc questions are: what effects does the confidence interval rule out, and what was the power against an effect size that would matter (chosen before seeing the data)? A related, more useful number is the replication probability: if the true effect were drawn from the data's own uncertainty, the chance that an identical new study is significant. It is lower than observed power and shows how fragile results near p = 0.05 are.

6Type S and Type M errors: what significance does to your estimates Expert

Gelman and Carlin proposed two errors beyond Type I and II for studies whose effects are small compared with their noise:

  • Type S (sign) error: among significant results, the share whose estimated effect has the wrong sign.
  • Type M (magnitude) error, or the exaggeration ratio: among significant results, the average |estimate| divided by the true effect.

The mechanism is the significance filter: when power is low, only unusually large estimates cross the threshold, so the significant ones are inflated, sometimes by a factor of two to ten, and a few have the wrong sign. This is why published (and shipped) effects from small experiments shrink on replication, the winner’s curse of the p-value course (chapter 14).

Part III · Designing for power

7Variance reduction: ANCOVA, CUPED and stratification Advanced

If a pre-experiment covariate X (last month’s spend, prior score) predicts the outcome Y, subtracting its predictable part removes noise without touching the treatment effect. CUPED (controlled-experiment using pre-experiment data) is the regression-adjusted estimator Y* = Y − θ(X − X̄) with θ = Cov(Y,X)/Var(X). Its variance is that of the raw difference times (1 − ρ²), where ρ is the correlation between X and Y: with ρ = 0.7 the variance falls by about half, which is the same as doubling the sample.

Rules: the covariate must be measured before randomisation (otherwise treatment contaminates it); in a randomised experiment the adjustment is unbiased whatever the model; stratifying or blocking the randomisation on a strong predictor gives a similar gain by design. The same idea underlies regression adjustment (ANCOVA) in clinical trials.

8Allocation: who gets the units Advanced

With total sample N, the standard error of a two-group difference is √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂). It is smallest at Neyman allocation n₁ : n₂ = σ₁ : σ₂, so the noisier arm gets more units. With unequal cost per unit (c₁, c₂) the optimum becomes n₁ : n₂ = (σ₁/σ₂)·√(c₂/c₁). With equal variances and costs, 50/50 is optimal. Departures from the optimum cost less than people fear (a 70/30 split with equal variances needs about 19% more data), which is why risk-averse rollouts that give the new treatment a small share are affordable but not free.

9Cluster-randomised designs Expert

When whole branches, regions, advisers or classrooms are randomised, outcomes within a cluster are correlated (intra-cluster correlation ρ), and the design effect is DE = 1 + (m − 1)ρ for clusters of size m. The effective sample size per arm is K·m/DE for K clusters, and the standard error of the arm difference is σ·√(2·DE/(K·m)). Two consequences: power is limited by the number of clusters, not the number of people: as m → ∞ the standard error falls only to σ√(2ρ/K); and with few clusters the test has K − 1 degrees of freedom per arm, so small-K designs lose more power than the normal approximation suggests. Randomise at the level at which the intervention is delivered, and analyse at that level (cluster means, mixed models or cluster-robust standard errors).

Part IV · Sequential monitoring and messy metrics

10Looking more than once: group-sequential designs Expert

Peeking at an ongoing experiment and stopping at the first p < 0.05 inflates the false-positive rate (five equally spaced looks give about 14%). The principled alternative is a group-sequential design: plan K looks and spend the α budget across them with a stopping boundary. Two classic families: Pocock uses the same critical value at every look (about 2.29 for K = 3), which stops early easily but ends with a stiff final bar; O’Brien–Fleming uses a very high bar early (3.47 at the first of three looks) and a final bar close to the fixed-sample 1.96, so it keeps nearly full power. Modern alternatives include α-spending functions (Lan–DeMets) and always-valid p-values or confidence sequences (mSPRT) for continuous monitoring. Remember that estimates after early stopping are biased upward (Chapter 6 again), and plan futility stops as well as efficacy stops.

11Power for heavy-tailed business metrics Expert

Revenue, balances and transaction amounts are right-skewed with a few very large values. The t-test on raw means is valid asymptotically but its power can be poor, because a handful of outliers dominate the variance. Options: transform (log), trim or cap (winsorise), use a rank test (Mann–Whitney), or model the metric in parts (conversion, then amount given conversion). Each changes the estimand: the raw mean answers “what happens to total revenue”, the log test answers “what happens to the typical (geometric-mean) user”, the rank test “does a random treated user beat a random control”. Choose the test whose question matches the decision, then use simulation to plan power: it is the only general tool when the metric or design is messy.
