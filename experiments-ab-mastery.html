Experiments & A/B Testing

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Potential outcomes, randomised experiments and A/B testing: CUPED, validity checks and sequential decisions.

The previous page listed the ways observational comparisons go wrong. A randomised experiment removes them by design: it makes treatment independent of every pre-treatment characteristic, measured or not. This page builds the framework (potential outcomes, the average treatment effect, SUTVA), the analysis (difference in means, randomisation inference), the planning (minimum detectable effect, sample size), the threats that survive randomisation (clustering, spillover, sample-ratio mismatch, non-compliance), the tools that sharpen it (stratification, regression adjustment, CUPED) and the ways of deciding as data arrive (always-valid sequential tests, bandits). The examples are the bank’s: offers, limit tests, collections strategies, champion–challenger comparisons.

Part I · Framework and design

1Potential outcomes and the fundamental problem Beginner

Each unit i has two potential outcomes: Yᵢ(1) if treated and Yᵢ(0) if not. The unit-level effect τᵢ = Yᵢ(1) − Yᵢ(0) is never observed, because only one of the two is realised: this is the fundamental problem of causal inference. What can be estimated are averages: the ATE = E[Y(1) − Y(0)] over the population, the ATT over those who were treated, the CATE within a subgroup. The observed difference in means mixes the effect with selection:

E[Y | T=1] − E[Y | T=0] = ATT + { E[Y(0) | T=1] − E[Y(0) | T=0] } (second term = selection bias)

Two assumptions are built into the notation. SUTVA: one unit’s outcome does not depend on others’ treatments (no interference) and there is a single version of the treatment. Consistency: the observed outcome equals the potential outcome of the treatment actually received. Under randomisation T is independent of (Y(0), Y(1)), so the selection term is zero and the difference in means is unbiased for the ATE. This is the sense in which an experiment needs no model of why customers differ.

2Analysing a randomised experiment: Neyman and Fisher Beginner

With n₁ treated and n₀ control customers the estimator is the difference in means τ̂ = Ȳ₁ − Ȳ₀. Neyman’s view treats the potential outcomes as fixed and the randomisation as the only randomness: τ̂ is unbiased for the sample ATE with variance s₁²/n₁ + s₀²/n₀ − s_τ²/n (the last term, unknowable, is dropped, so the usual unpooled standard error is conservative). The Welch t interval is the standard report. Fisher’s view tests the sharp null that τᵢ = 0 for everyone: under it, all outcomes are fixed and any re-assignment of the labels is equally likely, so the exact null distribution of any statistic is obtained by re-randomising and recomputing. This randomisation test needs no normality, is valid in small samples and with skewed outcomes, and extends to any test statistic (medians, quantiles, rank-based statistics).

For skewed money outcomes (spend, balances) the mean is what the business cares about, but it is noisy; trimmed means, winsorising (decided in advance), log transforms (a different estimand) and larger samples are the usual options. Report the effect with an interval, not just a p-value.

3Sample size, power and the minimum detectable effect Intermediate

Plan before running. For a two-sided test at level α with power 1 − β, comparing two arms of n customers each with standard deviation σ (continuous metric), the smallest effect reliably detected is

MDE = (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β) · σ · √(2/n) n per arm = 2 (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² σ² / δ²

For a conversion rate p₀ replace σ² by p(1 − p) (about p₀(1 − p₀) for each arm). Halving the detectable effect costs four times the sample; rare events (default, fraud, attrition) therefore need very large arms, which is why such experiments are run on proxies or on longer horizons. Choose the primary metric, the MDE that would change the decision (the smallest worthwhile effect, not the hoped-for one), α and the power before launch, and write down the stopping rule: a test that stops when the result looks good is not a test at level α.

4Units of randomisation and clustered designs Intermediate

The unit of randomisation (customer, household, branch, region, day) must match the unit at which treatments are delivered and outcomes interact. When customers in the same branch share an effect (staff behaviour, local conditions) and the treatment is assigned by branch, observations within a cluster are correlated with intra-cluster correlation ρ. Treating the n = K·m customers as independent understates the variance by the design effect

DEFF = 1 + (m − 1)ρ effective sample size = n / DEFF

so with m = 100 customers per branch and ρ = 0.05 the variance is almost six times too small and a nominal 5% test rejects far more often. The remedy is to analyse at the cluster level (cluster means, or cluster-robust standard errors with enough clusters) and to plan the sample size with the DEFF: adding customers within clusters helps little, adding clusters helps a lot. With few clusters (under about 30 in total) use cluster-level permutation tests or small-sample corrections.

Part II · Threats and variance reduction

5Interference and spillovers Advanced

SUTVA fails when a treated customer’s treatment changes other customers’ outcomes: referral and network offers, shared resources (limited inventory, call-centre capacity, a market with fixed demand), social comparison, price competition. Then each unit has more than two potential outcomes, indexed by the whole assignment vector, and the individually randomised difference in means estimates a direct effect under partial exposure (say 50% of the market treated), not the effect of rolling the treatment out to everybody. If treated and control units share an exposure, spillovers cancel in the difference but the quantity of interest (global treatment versus none) is different. Designs for interference randomise clusters (markets, branches, social-graph communities), vary the treated share across clusters, use switchback designs (alternating time periods for the whole system) or model the exposure explicitly.

6Stratification and regression adjustment Advanced

Randomisation guarantees balance on average, not in the sample you got; pre-treatment covariates that predict the outcome can be used to remove that chance imbalance and to shrink the standard error without any bias. Stratified (blocked) randomisation randomises within strata (segments, branches, risk bands). Regression adjustment fits Y ~ T + X: the coefficient on T stays unbiased under randomisation even if the linear model is wrong (Freedman’s caveat for small samples aside), and Lin’s version with the interaction T·(X − X̄) is never worse asymptotically than the unadjusted estimator. If X explains a share R² of the outcome variance, the variance of the estimate falls by about that share:

Var(τ̂_adj) ≈ (1 − R²) · Var(τ̂_unadj) equivalent to a sample (1 − R²)⁻¹ times larger

The covariates must be fixed before treatment (otherwise they absorb part of the effect) and chosen in advance, not by testing which gives the best p-value.

7CUPED: variance reduction from the pre-period Advanced

CUPED (controlled-experiment using pre-experiment data) is regression adjustment with the same metric measured before the experiment. With pre-period metric X and in-experiment metric Y, define Ỹ = Y − θ(X − X̄) with θ = Cov(X, Y)/Var(X) estimated on the pooled data; the treatment effect is estimated as the difference in means of Ỹ. Because X is measured before assignment it is unaffected by treatment and the estimate stays unbiased, while the variance falls by 1 − ρ² where ρ = Corr(X, Y). For user-level metrics with a persistent customer level (spend, balances, activity), ρ can be 0.5 to 0.9, equivalent to halving or better the required sample.

How to get a high ρ: a longer pre-period (averaging more weeks reduces the noise in X), the same metric definition, and extra pre-period covariates (ML-based versions such as CUPAC predict Y from many features). Pitfalls: a covariate measured during or after treatment can be affected by it and bias the estimate; new customers have no pre-period (impute and flag, or fall back to the unadjusted estimate); changing θ after seeing the data is another researcher degree of freedom.

8Validity checks: sample-ratio mismatch and differential attrition Advanced

A randomised experiment can be broken by its implementation. The first check is the sample ratio mismatch (SRM): if the design assigns 50/50 and the data show 50.8/49.2 among 100 000 customers, a chi-square test on the counts gives p ≈ 10⁻⁶, which cannot be chance. Typical causes: a bug that drops users in one arm (a page that fails to load for slow customers, an eligibility rule evaluated after assignment), bot filtering applied differently, redirects, caching, and logging that loses events. The danger is that the missing customers are not a random subset: removing the poorest outcomes from one arm re-creates selection bias (chapter 5 of the previous page) inside a randomised experiment, and the estimate is wrong with a clean-looking p-value.

Routine practice: run the SRM test on every experiment (use a strict threshold such as p < 0.001), check covariate balance on pre-period variables, define the analysed population by pre-assignment information only (“intention to treat”), and treat an SRM as a stop sign, not a footnote. The same applies to attrition in longer studies: compare loss rates and characteristics between arms.

Part III · Compliance, sequential decisions and adaptive allocation

9Non-compliance: intention-to-treat and the complier effect Expert

Often the randomised thing is an offer or an encouragement (a call, an email, an eligibility), and only some customers take it up. Three comparisons are available. Intention-to-treat (ITT): compare by assignment; unbiased for the effect of the offer, diluted by non-take-up, and the right answer for the policy “make the offer”. As-treated or per-protocol: compare those who actually took the treatment with those who did not; these groups differ by self-selection, so it is biased even though the assignment was random. The complier average causal effect (CACE) is the effect for customers who take the treatment only when offered; with random assignment, a monotone take-up and no effect of the offer other than through take-up (exclusion), it is identified by the Wald (instrumental-variable) ratio

CACE = ITT / (take-up in offered − take-up in not offered) = (Ȳ₁ − Ȳ₀) / (D̄₁ − D̄₀)

Always report the ITT; use the CACE for a question about the effect of treatment on those it moves, and say that it is a local effect.

10Sequential testing and always-valid inference Expert

The multiple-testing page showed that repeated looks inflate the false positive rate and that group-sequential boundaries fix the number and timing of looks in advance. Modern experimentation platforms need always-valid inference: a result that can be checked at any time, with any stopping rule, at a guaranteed error rate. The mixture sequential probability ratio test (mSPRT) does this: with the effect estimate θ̂ₙ and its variance Vₙ (for two arms, Vₙ ≈ 2σ²/n), and a normal prior N(0, τ²) on the effect, the mixture likelihood ratio is

Λₙ = √(Vₙ / (Vₙ + τ²)) · exp( θ̂ₙ² τ² / (2 Vₙ (Vₙ + τ²)) ); stop and reject when Λₙ ≥ 1/α

Under the null Λₙ is a nonnegative martingale with mean 1, so by Ville’s inequality P(Λₙ ≥ 1/α for some n) ≤ α, however often you look. The price is a larger sample than a fixed-horizon test when the effect is as planned; the gain is the freedom to stop early on a large effect and an unbiased guarantee against peeking. The inversion gives a confidence sequence, an interval valid at all times. Choose τ near the plausible effect size; group-sequential designs (O’Brien–Fleming spending) remain more efficient when the looks are few and planned. Point estimates after early stopping are biased upward, as for any selected winner.

11Adaptive allocation, bandits and practice Expert

A fixed 50/50 split sends half of the traffic to the worse arm for the whole test. A multi-armed bandit shifts traffic towards arms that look better as data accrue. Thompson sampling allocates each customer to an arm with the probability that the arm is the best under its posterior (for conversion rates: Beta posteriors). It reduces cumulative regret (the reward lost relative to always playing the best arm) and is natural for short-lived decisions (which of several offers to show this week). Its costs: estimates of the weaker arms are noisier and biased, p-values and confidence intervals need specific methods (inverse-probability-weighted or always-valid estimators), non-stationarity (day-of-week, novelty effects) can mislead the allocation, and it is not a substitute for a clean experiment when a precise, defensible effect estimate is the objective.

Practical checklist for a bank experiment: state the decision and the smallest effect that would change it; pick the unit of randomisation and the analysis population before launch; define the primary metric, guardrail metrics (complaints, default, opt-outs) and the horizon (delayed outcomes such as default need long follow-up or surrogates); pre-register the analysis and the stopping rule; run SRM and balance checks; analyse by intention to treat with covariate adjustment; document consent, fairness and regulatory constraints (offers must not discriminate across protected groups; some tests need approval).
