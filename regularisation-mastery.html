Regularisation: ridge, lasso and elastic net.

With many predictors, correlated predictors or modest data, the unbiased least-squares or maximum-likelihood fit is exactly the wrong thing to use: it has high variance and chases noise. Regularisation deliberately accepts a little bias in return for a large drop in variance by penalising the size of the coefficients. This page covers ridge (L2), lasso (L1) and elastic net, their geometry and solution algorithms, how to choose the penalty by cross-validation, what shrinkage does to inference, penalised logistic regression, and practical tools such as the adaptive lasso and stability selection.

Part I · Ridge

1Why shrink: variance dominates when p is large Beginner

The expected prediction error of an estimator is noise + bias² + variance. Ordinary least squares has zero bias but its variance grows with the number of predictors (about σ²p/n for new data). When p is a sizeable fraction of n, OLS fits noise and predicts badly. A penalty pulls the coefficients toward zero, adding a little bias and removing a lot of variance. Ridge regression minimises Σ(yᵢ − β₀ − xᵢᵀβ)² + λ Σβⱼ² with the tuning parameter λ ≥ 0 controlling the shrinkage (λ = 0 is OLS; λ → ∞ gives the intercept-only model). Predictors must be standardised first, otherwise the penalty depends on units; the intercept is not penalised.

2The ridge solution, shrinkage and effective degrees of freedom Intermediate

Ridge has a closed form, β̂(λ) = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy, which is always defined, even when XᵀX is singular (p > n or exact collinearity). In the principal-component basis of X, each direction with variance dₖ² is shrunk by the factor dₖ²/(dₖ² + λ): directions with large variance are barely touched, low-variance directions (where the data are least informative, the cause of collinearity trouble) are shrunk hard. The effective degrees of freedom df(λ) = Σ dₖ²/(dₖ² + λ) falls smoothly from p to 0 and is the honest measure of model complexity. Coefficient paths of ridge shrink smoothly and never reach exactly zero; correlated predictors share the shrinkage.

3Ridge and collinearity: trading bias for variance Advanced

Previous pages showed that collinearity inflates the variance of OLS coefficients but not their bias. Ridge is the direct remedy for prediction: shrinking toward zero introduces a bias in each coefficient (they are under-estimated) but reduces the variance by more, so the mean squared error Var + Bias² falls. There is always some λ > 0 for which ridge beats OLS in MSE for every coefficient, for any true β (the Hoerl–Kennard result); the improvement is largest when predictors are strongly correlated and noise is high. The price is that coefficients lose their unbiased interpretation, and standard errors and p-values are no longer straightforward.

Part II · Lasso and elastic net

4The L1 penalty: soft-thresholding and the geometry of sparsity Intermediate

The lasso replaces the squared penalty by the absolute value: minimise ½n⁻¹RSS + λΣ|βⱼ|. The change has a large consequence: many coefficients are set exactly to zero, so the lasso does estimation and variable selection in one step. With orthonormal predictors the lasso is the soft-threshold of the OLS estimate, β̂ = sign(z)(|z| − λ)₊: small estimates are zeroed, large ones are shrunk by a constant λ, while ridge shrinks every estimate proportionally, z/(1 + λ), and never reaches zero. Geometrically, the penalised problem is the contour of the loss touching a constraint region: a circle for ridge (no corners) and a diamond for the lasso, whose corners lie on the axes, so the first contact is often at a corner where a coefficient is zero.

5Coordinate descent and the lasso path Advanced

The lasso has no closed form but is easy to compute: coordinate descent cycles through the coefficients, each time solving the one-dimensional problem exactly with a soft-threshold, and repeats until nothing changes. Starting at λ_max = max|xⱼᵀy|/n (where all coefficients are zero) and lowering λ in steps, with each solution as the starting point for the next (warm starts), yields the whole regularisation path in about the cost of one least-squares fit. Variables enter the model one by one as λ falls. The tuning parameter is chosen by K-fold cross-validation: the λ with the smallest CV error (λ_min), or the largest λ within one standard error of it (λ_1se), which gives a sparser model. The lasso can select at most n variables, and when predictors are correlated it tends to keep one of a group arbitrarily.

6Elastic net and the grouping effect Advanced

The elastic net mixes the two penalties, λ[α‖β‖₁ + (1 − α)½‖β‖₂²], with α = 1 the lasso and α = 0 ridge. The ridge part gives it the grouping effect: highly correlated predictors with similar effects receive similar coefficients and enter or leave together, whereas the lasso arbitrarily picks one member and its choice flips from sample to sample. The L1 part keeps the sparsity. The elastic net is the usual first choice when predictors come in correlated groups (banking ratios, balance and transaction aggregates over several windows, one-hot encodings of related categories), and it can select more than n variables.

Part III · Choosing and using

7How well does cross-validation choose λ? Advanced

The cross-validated λ is itself a random variable. Different samples give different choices, and the min and one-SE rules trade accuracy for sparsity. Because the CV curve is flat near its minimum, a wide range of λ values predicts about equally well; what matters more is avoiding the extremes. Practical points: standardise inside each fold (not before the split) to avoid leakage; use stratified folds for rare events; repeat CV with several random splits if the data set is small; and keep a final holdout if you will also compare many different models.

8Shrinkage bias and the problem of inference after selection Expert

Penalised coefficients are biased toward zero by design, so they should not be read as effect sizes, and the usual standard errors, t-tests and confidence intervals no longer apply. Refitting ordinary least squares on the variables the lasso selected (post-lasso) removes the shrinkage but brings a different problem: the variables were chosen because they looked strong in this sample, so their refitted coefficients are inflated (the winner’s curse) and naive intervals undercover, while selected null variables look “significant” far more than 5% of the time. Valid inference after selection needs sample splitting, selective-inference corrections or the debiased lasso. Use the lasso for prediction and screening; use a pre-specified model, or a separate sample, for effect estimates and p-values.

9Penalised logistic regression Advanced

Every idea carries over to GLMs: maximise the log-likelihood minus the penalty. Ridge logistic regression solves by penalised IRLS, β ← (XᵀWX + λI)⁻¹(XᵀWz); the lasso version applies coordinate descent to the weighted quadratic approximation at each step (this is how glmnet works). Penalisation cures the two logistic problems of the last page: with separation the MLE diverges, whereas the penalised estimate is finite, and with few events per predictor the penalised model predicts better because it is less overfit. As in linear regression, choose λ by cross-validated deviance or log-loss, and remember that the intercept is shifted by any sampling you do (previous page).

10Adaptive lasso and selection consistency Expert

The lasso shrinks every selected coefficient by the same amount, so it is biased on the large, true effects, and to keep noise variables out it must over-shrink; in consequence it tends to include extra variables and does not recover the true model even as n grows (unless strict “irrepresentable” conditions hold). The adaptive lasso fixes this by weighting the penalty: wⱼ = 1/|β̂ⱼ,OLS|^γ, so strong predictors are penalised lightly and weak ones heavily. Under mild conditions it has the oracle property: with enough data it selects the true variables and estimates them as if they were known. Other relatives: relaxed lasso (refit with less shrinkage), SCAD and MCP (non-convex penalties that stop shrinking large coefficients), the group lasso (select whole blocks, such as all dummies of a categorical variable).

11Stability selection: which variables can you trust? Expert

A single lasso fit says little about which variables are reliably important: a different sample of the same data set could produce a different set. Stability selection repeats the lasso on many random half-samples (at a fixed moderately strong λ) and records how often each variable is selected. True signals are selected in most subsamples; noise variables, even correlated with signals, are selected only occasionally. Variables above a threshold (for example 60 to 80%) form the stable set, with a bound on the expected number of false selections. It is a practical way to present a short list of drivers to a non-technical audience (a regulator, a credit committee) with a stability number instead of a single yes/no.
