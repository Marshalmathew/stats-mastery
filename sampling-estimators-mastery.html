Sampling Distributions & Estimators

13 chapters · 12 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Sampling distributions, standard errors & the bias–variance of estimators.

Every number you compute from data (a mean, a default rate, a Gini, a model coefficient) is a random variable: draw a different sample and you get a different value. Stage 2 of the roadmap is about reasoning honestly about that randomness. This page covers the distribution of an estimate, its standard error, what makes an estimator good (bias, variance, MSE, efficiency), why “unbiased” is not the same as “best”, and how to get a trustworthy standard error when your data are finite, clustered or too awkward for a formula.

Part I · Estimates are random variables

1The sampling distribution Beginner

A parameter is a fixed but unknown feature of the population (its mean μ, median, standard deviation σ). An estimator is a rule that turns a sample into a guess, and the number it gives you is an estimate. Because the sample is random, the estimator is a random variable. Its distribution across all possible samples of size n is its sampling distribution.

estimate θ̂ = T(X₁, …, Xₙ) bias = E[θ̂] − θ standard error = SD(θ̂)

You only ever see one draw from that distribution. Everything in Stage 2 is about what the unseen distribution looks like and how to infer its width from the one draw you have.

Compare the statistics: the sample mean is centred on the truth for every population; the median of a skewed population is centred on the population median, not the mean; the sample SD and 90th percentile are slightly off-centre at small n. That off-centre gap is bias, and the width is the standard error.

2Standard deviation vs standard error Beginner

These two are confused constantly, even in regulatory documents. The standard deviation σ describes the spread of individual observations. The standard error describes the spread of an estimate. For the mean:

SD(data) = σ (does not shrink with n) SE(X̄) = σ / √n (shrinks)

In practice σ is unknown, so you use the plug-in estimate SE-hat = s/√n. That is itself a random quantity: with small samples it can be quite far off, which is why small-sample intervals use the t distribution (wider than the normal).

3Finite populations: the correction factor Intermediate

The σ/√n formula assumes the draws are independent, as if sampling with replacement from a huge population. If you audit 400 of 1,000 accounts without replacement, each pick tells you something about what remains, and the estimate is more precise than σ/√n suggests:

SE(X̄) = (σ/√n) · √((N − n)/(N − 1)) σ² = population variance (divisor N)

The factor √((N−n)/(N−1)) is the finite population correction (FPC). It is negligible when the sampling fraction n/N is below ~5%, and it goes to 0 as you sample everything (a census has no sampling error).

Part II · Bias, variance and trade-offs

5Bias, variance and mean squared error Intermediate

How good is an estimator? Averaged over repeated samples, its typical squared miss is the mean squared error, which splits cleanly:

MSE(θ̂) = E[(θ̂ − θ)²] = Bias² + Variance

Unbiasedness alone is not the goal. An estimator with a small bias and a much smaller variance beats an unbiased noisy one. A famous example: for the variance of normal data, dividing the sum of squares by n−1 is unbiased; dividing by n has a bias of −σ²/n (the sample mean is closer to the data than the true mean is, so deviations are too small); dividing by n+1 is biased more but has the lowest MSE.

Lab 5 · Three divisors for the variance

6,000 samples. For each divisor (n−1, n, n+1) the bars show Bias² (red), Variance (blue) and MSE (yellow) of the variance estimate, in units of σ⁴.

Why n−1 is still the defaultBecause it is unbiased for any distribution with a variance, it is what software reports, and the MSE difference is small for realistic n. Know the others exist: the choice of divisor is a bias–variance trade-off, not a law.

6Unbiased is not preserved by transformations Advanced

If θ̂ is unbiased for θ, then g(θ̂) is generally not unbiased for g(θ). Jensen's inequality says that for a convex g, E[g(θ̂)] ≥ g(E[θ̂]), and for a concave g the inequality reverses. Consequences you meet every day:

  • s² is unbiased for σ², so s is biased low for σ (square root is concave).
  • X̄ is unbiased for the mean time between events 1/λ, so 1/X̄ is biased high for the rate λ: E[1/X̄] = λ·n/(n−1).
  • exp(X̄) overestimates e^μ; log(X̄) underestimates log μ.

This matters when you convert an estimate into a different scale (an average to a rate, a log-odds to an odds ratio, a log-loss back to a loss) and when you report maximum-likelihood estimates, which are generally biased in small samples but consistent.

7Several unbiased estimators: the German tank problem Advanced

During World War II the Allies estimated German tank production from the serial numbers of captured tanks. Suppose tanks are numbered 1, …, N and you capture n of them at random without replacement, seeing a largest serial number M. Three natural estimators of N:

EstimatorFormulaBias
Sample maximumMalways too low (biased)
Corrected maximum(1 + 1/n)·M − 1unbiased, smallest variance among unbiased estimators (UMVU)
Method of moments2·X̄ − 1unbiased but much noisier

Two unbiased estimators can have wildly different variances; being unbiased does not tell you which to use. The best one uses the sufficient statistic (here, the maximum), which squeezes out all the information the sample holds about N.

8Consistency, efficiency and the best estimator of the centre Advanced

Two further properties complete the toolkit.

  • Consistency: θ̂ₙ → θ in probability as n → ∞. A biased estimator can be consistent (variance with divisor n), and an unbiased one can fail to be (using only the first observation).
  • Efficiency: among estimators with the same bias, the one with smaller variance wins. The Cramér–Rao bound gives a floor, Var(θ̂) ≥ 1/(n·I(θ)), where I is the Fisher information. Estimators that reach it are efficient.

Efficiency is relative to the data's distribution. For normal data the sample mean is efficient and the median needs about 57% more data to match it. For heavy-tailed data the ranking flips: the median (or a trimmed mean) can be far better than the mean. For contaminated data (mostly normal with a few wild values) the mean is destroyed by a handful of outliers, which is why robust statistics exist.

9The bias–variance trade-off and shrinkage Expert

Deliberately accepting a little bias can buy a large cut in variance. The simplest case: shrink the sample mean toward zero, θ̂_c = c·X̄ with 0 ≤ c ≤ 1. With X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n):

Bias² = (c − 1)² μ² Variance = c² σ²/n MSE(c) = (c − 1)² μ² + c² σ²/n

Setting the derivative to zero gives the optimal shrinkage factor c* = μ² / (μ² + σ²/n): the signal-to-noise ratio of the estimate. When the noise σ²/n is large compared with μ², c* is small and shrinking helps a lot. The catch is that c* depends on the unknown μ. Empirical-Bayes and James–Stein methods estimate it from many parallel problems (this is the shrinkage lab of the Bayes page), and ridge/lasso regression (Stage 3) use the same idea for coefficients.

Part III · Standard errors in practice

10Exact sampling distributions for normal data Advanced

If the data are iid N(μ, σ²) then three exact facts hold, and they are the foundation of the t-test and of the t and χ² families you met on the Distributions page:

X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n) (n−1)s²/σ² ~ χ²ₙ₋₁ X̄ and s² are independent

From these, (X̄ − μ)/(s/√n) ~ t with n−1 degrees of freedom. The independence of X̄ and s is special to the normal. For skewed data they are correlated (a few large values push both the mean and the spread up), which is one reason t-intervals can misbehave for skewed data at moderate n.

11When there is no formula: bootstrap and jackknife Advanced

For a median, a 90th percentile, a Gini or a model's AUC the SE has no tidy formula. The bootstrap treats your sample as a stand-in for the population: resample n observations with replacement B times, recompute the statistic each time, and take the standard deviation of the B values as the SE. The jackknife instead leaves out one observation at a time. (Item 8 of the roadmap gives the bootstrap its full treatment; this is the first look.)

SE_boot = SD( T(X*₁), …, T(X*_B) ) SE_jack = √( (n−1)/n · Σ (Tᵢ − T̄)² )

12Clustered data: the design effect Expert

Customers within a branch, transactions within a customer, loans within an industry: observations in the same cluster resemble each other. If the intraclass correlation (ICC) is ρ and each cluster has m observations, the variance of the overall mean is inflated by the design effect:

DEFF = 1 + (m − 1) ρ SE_true = SE_iid · √DEFF n_eff = (K·m) / DEFF

Even a small ρ matters when m is large: with ρ = 0.05 and m = 100 the DEFF is almost 6, so 10,000 rows carry the information of about 1,700 independent ones. The practical fix is a cluster-robust SE: treat each cluster as one observation and use the spread of cluster means.

Lab 12 · Naive vs cluster-robust standard errors

K clusters of m observations each (cluster effect variance ρ, noise variance 1−ρ). 1,500 simulated datasets. Blue curve: the naive iid SE; red curve: the true spread.

Regulatory angleIf a model-validation sample contains many accounts per customer or per branch, confidence intervals on a default rate or AUC computed as if rows were independent are too narrow, and the overstated precision shows up as unexplained “instability” out of sample. Always state the unit of independence.
