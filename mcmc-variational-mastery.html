MCMC and Variational Inference

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

MCMC and variational inference: getting answers from posteriors you can only evaluate.

Last page’s models were conjugate, so the posterior had a formula. Almost every real model (a logistic PD model, a hierarchical model, a mixture) does not, and the posterior is known only up to a constant: you can compute p(y | θ)p(θ) at any θ but not integrate it. This page covers the two families of answers. Sampling (Monte Carlo, importance sampling, Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) produces draws whose averages converge to posterior quantities; approximation (Laplace, variational inference) replaces the posterior by a simple distribution fitted by optimisation. For each, the lab shows where it works, where it quietly fails, and the diagnostic that tells you: acceptance rates, effective sample size, R̂, divergences, tail accuracy. Every lab has a known target, so the errors are measurable.

Part I · Sampling the posterior

1Why sample at all? Beginner

A posterior is useful through the questions you ask of it: its mean, an interval, the probability that a PD exceeds a threshold, a prediction. Each is an integral over the posterior, E[g(θ)] = ∫ g(θ) p(θ | y) dθ. Two ways to compute it. Numerical integration on a grid works for one or two parameters and is hopeless beyond: a grid with 100 points per parameter needs 100^d evaluations, which is 10²⁰ for ten parameters. Monte Carlo draws θ₁, …, θ_M from the posterior and averages g(θ_i); its error shrinks like 1/√M regardless of the dimension, which is the whole reason for the method. The catch is that you must be able to draw from the posterior, and for a posterior known only up to a constant that is the hard part: the rest of the page is a toolbox for it.

2Importance sampling Intermediate

If you can evaluate the target p but not draw from it, draw from an easy proposal q and correct with weights w = p/q: E_p[g] ≈ Σ w_i g(θ_i)/Σ w_i. It is unbiased (or consistent, when p is known only up to a constant) but its variance depends on how well q covers p. The effective sample size ESS = (Σw)²/Σw² measures the damage: the weighted sample is worth that many equally weighted draws. If the weights vary wildly (a few huge ones), ESS collapses. The danger zone is a proposal with lighter tails than the target: the variance of the weights is then infinite and no amount of data repairs it, which is why proposals are usually heavy-tailed (a Student t, not a normal). Importance sampling is the foundation of particle filters and of methods that reweight an existing sample (for example to test a different prior), but in high dimensions it is hard to find a proposal that works, which motivates Markov chains.

3Markov chains and Metropolis–Hastings Intermediate

A Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method builds a chain θ₁, θ₂, … whose long-run distribution is the posterior, so that the draws can be averaged although they are dependent. Random-walk Metropolis is the simplest: from the current θ propose θ* = θ + step × noise; accept with probability min(1, p(θ*)/p(θ)), otherwise repeat θ. The target enters only through a ratio, so the normalising constant cancels: exactly what a posterior requires. The chain has the posterior as its stationary distribution (detailed balance), but it needs warm-up to forget its starting point, and its draws are autocorrelated, so you pay in effective sample size. The only tuning knob is the proposal step. Too small and almost every proposal is accepted but the chain crawls; too large and almost every proposal is rejected and the chain stands still. Between lies an optimum, with acceptance about 44% in one dimension and about 23% in many. Metropolis–Hastings generalises this to asymmetric proposals by adding the ratio of proposal densities.

4Gibbs sampling Intermediate

Gibbs sampling updates one parameter (or block) at a time by drawing it from its full conditional distribution given all the others, p(θ_j | θ_−j, y). Every draw is accepted, there is nothing to tune, and for conjugate hierarchical models the conditionals are standard distributions, which is why BUGS and JAGS were built on it. Its weakness is geometry: moves are parallel to the axes, so when parameters are strongly correlated in the posterior the chain zig-zags along a narrow ridge in tiny steps. For a bivariate normal with correlation ρ the autocorrelation of one coordinate is ρ² per step, and n draws are worth n(1 − ρ²)/(1 + ρ²) independent ones: at ρ = 0.95 that is about 5%. Remedies are to sample correlated parameters together (blocking), to reparametrise (centre the covariates, use a non-centred hierarchical form), or to use a sampler that follows the geometry, as in the next chapters.

Part II · Diagnostics and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

5Has the chain converged? Warm-up and R̂ Advanced

MCMC theory promises convergence eventually; practice needs a check now. The standard protocol: run several chains from dispersed starting points, discard an initial warm-up (the early draws, still influenced by the start), and compare chains. R̂ (split R-hat) compares the variance between chains with the variance within them (after splitting each chain in two, so that a chain drifting from one half to the other is also caught); it falls to 1 as the chains agree. The modern rule is R̂ < 1.01 together with enough effective sample size (a few hundred in total for means, much more for tails). Look at trace plots as well: a good chain looks like a fuzzy caterpillar with no trend. R̂ cannot detect what no chain has seen: if every chain stays in the same mode of a multimodal posterior, R̂ is perfect and the answer is wrong, which is why dispersed starts matter. In a model-risk file, document the number of chains, the warm-up, R̂, ESS and the seed.

6Effective sample size and Monte Carlo error Advanced

Correlated draws carry less information. If the autocorrelation at lag k is ρ_k, the integrated autocorrelation time is τ = 1 + 2Σρ_k and the effective sample size is ESS = n/τ. The Monte Carlo standard error of a posterior mean is sd/√ESS, and it is a different thing from the posterior sd: the posterior sd is the uncertainty about θ that no amount of computing removes, while the MCSE is the numerical error you can shrink by running longer. Report both, and make the MCSE small relative to the posterior sd (a few percent) for every quantity you quote. ESS is estimated from the sample autocorrelations with a rule that stops summing when they become noise (Geyer’s initial positive sequence). Two common mistakes: using n in place of ESS (intervals too narrow), and thinning (keeping every k-th draw) to “remove autocorrelation”, which discards information and never increases ESS; thin only to save memory. Modern software reports separate bulk-ESS (for the centre) and tail-ESS (for quantiles), because the tails mix more slowly.

7Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and NUTS Advanced

Random walks explore by diffusion; Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) explores by momentum. Treat −log p(θ) as a potential-energy landscape, give the current point a random momentum, and simulate Hamiltonian dynamics for L steps of size ε with the leapfrog integrator, which follows the landscape’s gradient; then accept or reject with a Metropolis correction for the integration error. A trajectory can travel far along a correlated ridge and still be accepted with high probability, so successive draws are nearly independent even in hundreds of dimensions. It needs the gradient ∇log p (automatic differentiation provides it), a step size ε small enough for stable integration (about the narrowest width of the posterior) and a trajectory length long enough to move far without turning back. NUTS, the No-U-Turn Sampler, chooses the trajectory length adaptively, and the step size is tuned during warm-up; this is the engine of Stan, PyMC and NumPyro. When ε is too large for the local geometry the integrator becomes unstable, the energy error explodes and the software reports divergent transitions: a signal that the sampler cannot explore some region (a funnel in a hierarchical model, for instance) and the results may be biased. Treat divergences as errors to fix, usually by reparametrising, not as warnings to ignore.

8A real model: Bayesian logistic regression Expert

A logistic PD model has no conjugate prior, so its posterior is sampled. The log posterior is the Bernoulli log-likelihood plus the log prior; one evaluation costs a pass through the data, and the efficiency of the sampler decides whether the run takes seconds or hours. Practical points: standardise the predictors (centred, unit scale) so the posterior is not wildly elongated; correlated predictors still produce strongly correlated coefficients (and non-identifiability when collinear: the prior is then what keeps the posterior proper); the Laplace approximation (a normal at the posterior mode with covariance equal to the inverse Hessian) is a cheap, often excellent shape guide for the proposal covariance; and the prior matters for weakly identified coefficients and for rare events, where a weakly informative prior also prevents the infinite estimates of separation. Compare to maximum likelihood: with plenty of data they agree, with little data the posterior is shrunk and wider, and it carries the full joint uncertainty into predictions. Posterior draws of the coefficients give a posterior for every customer’s PD, and so for portfolio-level quantities such as expected loss.

Part III · Approximations and practice

9Variational inference Expert

Variational inference (VI) turns inference into optimisation. Choose a family of simple distributions q_φ(θ) (independent normals, “mean-field”, is the common one) and find the φ that minimises KL(q ‖ p(· | y)), equivalently maximises the evidence lower bound (ELBO) = E_q[log p(y, θ)] − E_q[log q(θ)]. Stochastic-gradient versions (ADVI, reparametrisation gradients) scale to millions of observations and are how Bayesian deep models and large topic models are fitted. What you give up is accuracy where it matters for risk. The direction of the KL makes q avoid regions where p is small, so it is too narrow: mean-field q ignores posterior correlation and underestimates marginal variances (for equicorrelated normals the sd ratio is exactly √((1 − ρ)(1 + (d − 1)ρ)/(1 + (d − 2)ρ)), shrinking as ρ or the dimension grow), it can miss modes, and its tails are thin. Means are usually decent, intervals are not. Use VI for speed, initialisation and prediction; for uncertainty that someone will rely on, validate against MCMC on a subset, or use importance-sampling corrections (Pareto-smoothed) to diagnose the fit.

10Laplace, VI and the exact answer for a rare PD Expert

The Laplace approximation fits a normal at the posterior mode with covariance equal to the inverse of the negative Hessian there: one optimisation and one matrix inversion, and it is exact for a normal posterior. It is the engine of INLA and of many “Bayesian” logistic models in production, and it is the reason maximum-likelihood standard errors and the Bayesian posterior agree with plenty of data (Bernstein–von Mises, item 26). It fails when the posterior is skewed, bounded or multimodal, and the failure is greatest in small samples, exactly the low-default portfolio case: a normal on the log-odds scale places too little mass in the upper tail. The same holds for a fitted normal q (VI). The honest workflow is to approximate quickly, then check the approximation on the quantity you will report (the 95% or 99% upper bound for a PD, the probability of exceeding a threshold) against a grid, MCMC or a more flexible family. With one parameter you can have the exact answer for free, as in this lab, and the comparison shows what is at stake.

11A workflow you can defend, and the tail problem Expert

A defensible Bayesian computation has a standard shape. Before: simulate from the model and prior (prior predictive), and test the sampler on fake data with known parameters, so you know it can recover the truth. During: several chains from dispersed starts, enough warm-up, adaptation turned on, a fixed seed recorded. After: R̂ < 1.01, bulk- and tail-ESS in the hundreds at least, no divergences, trace and rank plots checked, MCSE small beside the posterior sd, posterior predictive checks (item 26) and a prior sensitivity run. Then report the uncertainty: posterior intervals and probabilities, not just the mean. Be especially careful with tails. A 99th or 99.9th percentile, or the probability of breaching a threshold, depends on a handful of draws, and the relative error of an estimated tail probability p from ESS effective draws is about √((1 − p)/(p · ESS)): 1% of 1000 effective draws gives only 10 draws in the tail and about 32% error. Plan the number of effective draws from the quantity you must report, not from what the mean needs.
