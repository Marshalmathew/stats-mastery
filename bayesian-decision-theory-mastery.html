Bayesian Decision Theory

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Bayesian decision theory: from a posterior to an action, and what it is worth to know more.

A model’s output is a distribution; a business needs an action. Decision theory joins them: specify actions, losses or utilities, and choose the action with the smallest posterior expected loss. It shows that the “best” summary of a posterior depends on the costs (mean, median, quantile), that classification thresholds come from cost ratios and not from 0.5, that miscalibrated probabilities spoil them, that uncertainty about parameters belongs inside the decision, and that more data have a computable value. The second half applies the same logic to risk aversion, sequential stopping, sensitivity of a decision to the prior, estimator risk and constrained allocation. This closes Stage 6 on Bayesian methods.

Part I · Losses and decision rules

1Loss, risk and the Bayes action Beginner

A posterior says what you believe; a decision says what you do. To connect them you need three things: the possible actions a, the unknown state θ, and a loss function L(a, θ) that scores how bad action a is if the truth is θ. The Bayes action is the one with the smallest posterior expected loss, E[L(a, θ) | data]. Which summary of the posterior you should report is therefore not a statistical question but a question about costs: squared-error loss gives the mean, absolute-error loss the median, and an “all or nothing” loss (right within a tolerance, wrong otherwise) the mode. For a skewed posterior they are quite different numbers, and each is the best answer to a different question.

a* = argmin over a of E[L(a, θ) | y] · squared loss → posterior mean · absolute loss → median · 0–1 loss → mode

2Asymmetric costs and quantiles Intermediate

In most business decisions the two kinds of error do not cost the same. If running out of stock costs cᵤ per unit and surplus costs cₒ per unit, the loss is linear on each side of the demand, and the Bayes action is the quantile of the predictive distribution at level cᵤ/(cᵤ + cₒ). A cost ratio of 4 means the best stock level is the 80th percentile of demand, not its mean. The same logic sets a provision above the expected credit loss, a liquidity buffer, and an exposure limit: whenever under- and over-estimating cost different amounts, the answer is a quantile of the predictive distribution, and a point forecast alone is not enough to decide.

loss = cᵤ·max(D − a, 0) + cₒ·max(a − D, 0) → a* = the cᵤ/(cᵤ + cₒ) quantile of D

3Decision thresholds from costs Intermediate

For a yes/no action on a calibrated probability p (flag a transaction, approve a loan, send a letter), the expected cost of acting is (1 − p)·c_FP and the expected cost of not acting is p·c_FN. Acting is better when p exceeds c_FP/(c_FP + c_FN). This one line replaces three common habits: using 0.5, using the base rate, and picking the threshold that maximises accuracy or F1 on a validation set. It also shows why different uses of the same model should have different cut-offs: a screening step that costs little to follow up has a low threshold, an automatic account freeze with large harm if wrong has a high one. The threshold belongs to the decision, not to the model, and it depends only on the cost ratio.

act if p > c_FP / (c_FP + c_FN) · with c = c_FN / c_FP: threshold = 1 / (1 + c)

4When the probabilities are not calibrated Advanced

The threshold rule above assumes the score is the probability. Most classifiers are not calibrated: boosted trees and neural networks tend to be overconfident, and a model fitted on resampled data is miscalibrated by construction (item 17). Applying 1/(1 + c) to the raw score then gives the wrong cut-off, and the loss from this can exceed the loss from any difference in ranking quality between models. The remedy is to recalibrate on held-out labelled data (Platt or isotonic regression, item 16), after which the same rule is valid. This separates three questions that are often blurred: ranking quality (discrimination), probability accuracy (calibration), and the cost-based cut-off (the decision), which can each be fixed in turn. A model with a good AUC and poor calibration is a good ranking with a wrong threshold.

Part II · Information, uncertainty and risk

5The value of information Advanced

Should you collect more data before deciding? Decision theory answers with the expected value of perfect information (EVPI): the expected improvement in payoff if you could learn the truth before acting, equal to the expected loss of deciding now. It is an upper bound on what any study can be worth. The expected value of sample information (EVSI) is the value of a specific experiment: average, over the data the experiment might produce, the improvement from the decision you would then make. EVSI rises with sample size and flattens, so subtracting the cost of the data gives an optimal sample size, and sometimes the answer is zero: when the prior is far from the decision boundary relative to the noise, no feasible study changes what you would do. This is the right framework for deciding whether to run a pilot, buy a data feed, or label more cases, and it uses no p-values.

EVPI = E[max(θ, 0)] − max(E[θ], 0) · EVSI(n) = E over data of [value of the best action] − value now

6Plug-in rules against predictive decisions Advanced

A decision that depends on an unknown parameter can be made by plugging in the best estimate or by averaging the loss over the posterior (the Bayes rule). With plenty of data they agree; with little data they do not, because the plug-in ignores the uncertainty in the estimate. For a quantile of demand or loss the effect is systematic: the plug-in quantile is too low, so shortages, or capital shortfalls, occur more often than the target. The posterior predictive distribution has heavier tails (for a normal model with unknown mean and sd, a Student t) and its quantile is calibrated by construction. This is the same message as for prediction intervals in earlier items: report the predictive distribution, not the fitted one. It applies directly to value-at-risk from a short history, to loss reserves for a new product, and to any threshold set from a small sample.

7Utility and risk aversion Advanced

Losses are in money, but a decision-maker may care about more than expected money: a bank holds capital because it is risk averse about large losses. Expected utility theory handles this by evaluating each action with a concave utility function. For exponential utility and a normal outcome the answer is simple: choose the action with the highest certainty equivalent, mean − (λ/2)·variance, where λ is the degree of risk aversion. The variance has two parts, the inherent noise in the outcome and the uncertainty in the parameter, so a risk-averse decision favours options that are well measured. Two cautions: the utility function is a statement about the institution, not a fact the data can supply, so it should be explicit and its effect shown over a range of λ; and for a regulated bank much of the risk appetite is already fixed by capital rules, which act like a constraint on the choice.

CE = E[X] − (λ/2)·Var[X] with Var[X] = outcome noise + posterior variance of the mean

8Sequential decisions and stopping Expert

Most real decisions are made while data are still arriving, and the question is when to stop. For a test of two options the Bayesian rule is to stop when the posterior expected loss of picking the current leader falls below the cost of continuing, a threshold in the same units as the business outcome (here, conversion rate). It is a decision rule: it compares the regret still at stake with the cost of waiting, and under the Bayesian posterior the stopping rule does not change the inference, so peeking is not a problem for the posterior. It is, however, not a significance test. Its frequentist error rates (false-winner rate at zero effect, power at a given effect) should be established by simulation before a regulator or an internal controls function is shown the result. Compare it with the group-sequential designs of item 23, which fix those error rates and ask for a different guarantee.

Part III · Robustness, sequential and constrained decisions

9Is the decision sensitive to the prior? Expert

Sensitivity analysis matters most for the decision, not the posterior. Two priors may give different posteriors and the same decision, in which case the choice of prior is immaterial, or the same prior differences may flip the decision, in which case it is material and must be reported. The practical way is to map the decision over a range of defensible priors (for a rate, the mean and strength of a beta prior) and mark the region where the action changes, then ask how much more data would settle it. When a decision is robust over all the priors a sceptical reviewer would accept, say so; when it is not, say that the data alone do not decide it and the answer rests on judgement, and name the judgement. For model documentation under SR 11-7 or SR 26-2 this map is a compact way to show the effect of an assumption on the outcome that matters.

10Bayes risk, minimax and admissibility Expert

Before the data arrive an estimator can be judged by its risk, the expected loss at each possible true value, which is a frequentist question. The Bayes view averages this over a prior to give the Bayes risk, and the Bayes estimator minimises it. The minimax estimator instead minimises the worst-case risk and needs no prior. In a normal-mean problem the unbiased estimator has flat risk and is minimax, while a shrinkage estimator has lower risk near the prior centre and higher risk far from it; it wins on average if the prior is right and can lose badly where the prior is wrong. A rule is admissible if nothing beats it everywhere, and every Bayes rule with a proper prior is admissible; Stein’s result shows that for three or more means the sample mean is not. Practically: shrinking is a bet on the prior, and the size of the bet should match your confidence in it.

risk R(θ, δ) = E[(δ(y) − θ)² | θ] · Bayes risk = ∫ R(θ, δ) π(θ) dθ · minimax: minimise sup over θ of R

11Allocating a limited capacity Expert

Fraud, AML and collections teams can review only a fraction of the alerts they raise. With a single capacity constraint and a known value for each action, the Bayes allocation is simple: rank cases by expected value and work down the list until capacity runs out. For a review that prevents a loss A if the case is genuine, the value is p·A, minus the cost of the review; ranking by the probability alone ignores exposure, and ranking by exposure alone ignores the chance that the case is genuine. With several constraints (analyst hours by skill, regulatory deadlines) the same idea becomes a linear program on expected values. The ceiling is the oracle that knows the labels; the distance between it and the expected-value ranking is the price of uncertainty, which only better probabilities can reduce. Reported gains should come from a held-out period, and the calibration of p matters as much as its rank order here, since values multiply probabilities.
