Missing Data

Missing data: mechanisms, complete-case analysis, multiple imputation, weighting and sensitivity.

Income left blank, a bureau score that never arrived, outcomes of customers you rejected, patients who dropped out: real data sets have holes, and what you do about them decides whether your estimates are biased, whether your standard errors are honest and whether your scorecard works in production. The answer depends on why the values are missing, not on how many are. This page builds the theory (MCAR, MAR, MNAR), shows when simple fixes work and when they fail, and then covers multiple imputation with Rubin’s rules, weighting and doubly robust estimators, sensitivity analysis and the practical choices in prediction models.

Part I · Mechanisms and simple fixes

1Why missingness matters: MCAR, MAR and MNAR Beginner

Let R = 1 if a value is observed and 0 if it is missing. The missingness mechanism is the probability of R given the data. Rubin’s three classes: MCAR (missing completely at random), where P(R) does not depend on any data, observed or not (a lab sample dropped at random); MAR (missing at random), where P(R) depends only on observed values (younger applicants skip the income field more often, and age is recorded); and MNAR (missing not at random), where P(R) still depends on the missing value itself after accounting for what is observed (high earners hide their income, defaulters do not answer the follow-up call).

MCAR: P(R | Y_obs, Y_mis) = P(R) MAR: P(R | Y_obs, Y_mis) = P(R | Y_obs) MNAR: depends on Y_mis too

“At random” is a misleading name: MAR means the missingness is explainable by recorded variables, so those variables can be used to repair the damage. Which mechanism holds is an assumption that the data cannot fully check, because the check would need the missing values. The percentage missing matters far less than the mechanism: 5% MNAR can hurt more than 40% MCAR. The estimand matters too: the mean of an outcome and a regression slope react differently to the same mechanism, as the lab shows.

2Complete-case analysis: when it is fine Intermediate

Complete-case analysis (listwise deletion) keeps only the rows with no missing values. It is simple, it is what most software does by default, and it is valid more often than people think. For regression, the complete-case estimate of the coefficients is unbiased whenever the missingness depends on the covariates but not on the outcome (after conditioning on them): selecting rows by x does not bias the regression of y on x. It fails when the missingness depends on the outcome, directly (MNAR) or through a mechanism that makes the observed outcome–covariate relation differ from the full one. Missing covariates whose absence depends on the outcome are the classic trap: a MAR mechanism (the outcome is observed) that still biases the complete-case slope.

Even when unbiased, complete-case analysis loses precision: with several covariates each missing a little, the share of complete rows can be small. Rule: use it when the missingness is plausibly independent of the outcome given the covariates, and report how many rows were dropped. In logistic regression, selecting on the outcome (as in case–control sampling) only shifts the intercept, but selecting on the outcome through another variable is not harmless.

3Simple fixes and why they fail Intermediate

Single imputation fills each hole once and then analyses the data set as if it were complete. Mean imputation shrinks the variance and the correlations towards zero and biases estimates unless the data are MCAR (and even then the variance is wrong). Regression imputation (fill with the prediction from the observed variables) keeps means right under MAR but puts every imputed point exactly on the regression line, so correlations are inflated and variances shrink. Stochastic regression imputation adds a random residual, which restores the variance and correlation, but the standard errors are still too small because the analysis treats imputed values as real observations and ignores the uncertainty of the imputation model itself. The missing-indicator method (fill with a constant and add a missing flag) gives biased coefficients in inference; it can be useful in pure prediction (chapter 10). Last observation carried forward and similar rules have no statistical basis.

The common flaw: one filled-in value pretends to be a real observation. The cure is to impute several times and combine the results (chapter 4).

Part II · Multiple imputation

4Multiple imputation and Rubin’s rules Advanced

Multiple imputation (MI) replaces each missing value by M plausible values drawn from their predictive distribution given the observed data, producing M completed data sets. Each is analysed with the ordinary method, and the M results are combined. Three steps: impute (draw from the posterior predictive, which means drawing the imputation-model parameters as well as the residual noise: that is what “proper” means), analyse each data set, pool.

Q̄ = (1/M) Σ Q̂ₘ W̄ = (1/M) Σ Wₘ (within) B = Σ (Q̂ₘ − Q̄)² / (M − 1) (between) T = W̄ + (1 + 1/M) B

The pooled estimate is Q̄, its variance is T, and the interval is Q̄ ± t_ν √T with ν = (M − 1)(1 + W̄ / ((1 + 1/M)B))², which is (M−1)/γ² with γ the fraction of missing information, γ = (1 + 1/M)B / T. The between-imputation variance B is exactly what single imputation throws away. MI is valid when the imputation model is right and the data are MAR given the variables in the imputation model; it is not a way to get information that is not there: with few observed values the between-variance is large and the interval honestly wide.

5How many imputations? Monte Carlo error and the fraction of missing information Advanced

With M imputations the pooled estimate has relative efficiency (1 + γ/M)⁻¹ compared with M = ∞, where γ is the fraction of missing information. With γ = 0.3, M = 5 gives 94% efficiency, which is why the old advice was “3–10 imputations”. That advice is about the point estimate. The Monte Carlo error, the extra randomness because another run of the imputation would give slightly different results, also affects standard errors, p-values and intervals, and with today’s computing power M can be large. A practical rule: take M at least as large as the percentage of incomplete cases (so 30–50 imputations is common), and check that rerunning with another seed changes the estimates and SEs by less than you care about. Report M and the seed.

Note that γ is not the percentage missing: it measures how much information about the parameter is lost, and it is smaller when the other variables predict the missing values well, larger when they do not.

6The imputation model: include the outcome; chained equations Advanced

The imputation model must contain everything the analysis uses, including the outcome, plus any variable that predicts missingness or the missing values (chapter 11). If a covariate is imputed without the outcome, the imputed values carry no relation to y, which pulls the estimated coefficient towards zero. In general the imputation model must be at least as rich as the analysis model (the property called congeniality): an interaction or squared term in the analysis must be reflected in the imputation, otherwise the effect is diluted.

With many variables with holes, writing a joint model is hard. Multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE, also called fully conditional specification) imputes variable by variable: start with a simple fill, then cycle through the incomplete variables, each time regressing one variable on all the others (linear model for continuous, logistic for binary, multinomial for categorical, predictive mean matching for skewed) and replacing its missing values by draws; after some cycles (5–20) save one completed data set, and repeat M times. It is flexible and widely used (mice in R, IterativeImputer or statsmodels in Python), but the conditional models may not correspond to any joint distribution, so check convergence by plotting the means and SDs of the imputed values against the iteration.

7Skewed, bounded and categorical variables: predictive mean matching Advanced

A normal linear imputation model assumes a symmetric, unbounded variable. Incomes, balances and transaction counts are right-skewed, positive or bounded: normal draws can be negative, impossible or have the wrong shape. Options: transform (impute log x, then exponentiate) when a transformation makes the variable close to normal, use a model of the right family (logistic for binary, multinomial for categorical, ordinal), or use predictive mean matching (PMM). PMM predicts the missing variable by regression, then for each missing case finds the k observed cases with the closest predictions and imputes the actual observed value of one of them chosen at random. Imputed values are always real values from the data (never negative if none was, never outside the observed range), keep the observed shape, and are robust to mild model misspecification. It needs enough observed cases near the missing ones and can struggle with a very strong dependence on a few observations.

Part III · Beyond MAR, and practice

8When the data are MNAR: sensitivity and tipping-point analysis Expert

If missingness depends on the missing value itself, no method can recover the truth from the observed data alone: the observed-data distribution is identical under different MNAR stories, so the data cannot choose between them. The honest approach is a sensitivity analysis: state a plausible range of departures from MAR and show how the conclusion moves. The simplest form is delta adjustment (a pattern-mixture model): impute under MAR, then add a shift δ to the imputed values of the group in which dropouts are suspected to do worse (“dropouts in the treatment arm have outcomes δ lower than MAR predicts”). Repeat for a range of δ. The tipping point is the δ at which the conclusion (significance, approval, a monitoring threshold) changes; the question for the experts is then whether such a δ is plausible. Reference-based variants (dropouts follow the control arm) are used in clinical trials; the same logic applies to reject inference and non-response in surveys.

Because the adjustment is added after imputation, the pooled estimate moves by δ times the share of imputed values, so the whole curve comes from one set of imputations.

9Weighting, doubly robust estimators and reject inference Expert

Instead of filling holes, inverse probability weighting (IPW) reweights the observed cases: if a case with covariates x has probability π(x) of being observed, it stands for 1/π(x) cases in the full population. The probabilities come from a model of R on the observed covariates (usually logistic). IPW is simple and needs no model of the outcome, but weights can explode when some π(x) are near zero (the positivity problem) and the estimate is only as good as the selection model. The opposite approach is outcome regression (or imputation): model y given x and predict for everybody. The augmented IPW (AIPW) estimator combines them:

μ̂_AIPW = mean over all i of [ m̂(xᵢ) + Rᵢ (yᵢ − m̂(xᵢ)) / π̂(xᵢ) ]

and is doubly robust: consistent if either the outcome model m or the selection model π is correct (not necessarily both), and efficient if both are. In credit this is the logic of reject inference: the outcome (default) is observed only for accepted applicants, selection depends on application data (MAR given x if the cut-off used only recorded variables), so weighting or modelling the accepted population to represent the through-the-door population is a missing-data problem. If part of the selection used information not in the data (a manual override), it is MNAR and no reweighting removes the bias.

10Missing data in prediction models: indicators, imputation and deployment Expert

When the goal is prediction, not inference, the logic changes. The model has to score new cases that also have holes, using only what will be available at scoring time, so the imputation (or the missing-indicator) must be a deterministic rule learned on the training data and applied identically in production; the outcome cannot be used to impute. Common choices: mean/median fill with a missing indicator, regression or MICE imputation from other predictors, or algorithms that handle missing values natively (tree-based boosting with learned default direction). If missingness is informative (customers who do not report income default more), the indicator carries real signal and adds accuracy; if it is MCAR the indicator is noise. The danger is deployment shift: if the missingness pattern changes (a field becomes mandatory, a bureau feed fails, a channel changes), a model that learned to lean on the indicator mis-scores, while one that imputes from other predictors degrades more gently. Monitor missing rates per feature in production, and document the treatment in the model-risk file.

11Auxiliary variables, diagnostics and reporting Expert

An auxiliary variable is not in the analysis model but helps the imputation: it predicts the missing values or the missingness. Including it (i) can turn an MNAR problem into MAR (if the variable that drives missingness is in the imputation model, the data are MAR given it), and (ii) reduces the fraction of missing information, hence the variance, when it correlates with the incomplete variable. The cost is small; the usual advice is to include all variables of the analysis model plus plausible predictors of missingness and of the incomplete variables (correlation above about 0.4 with them).

Diagnostics and reporting. (1) Tabulate the amount and patterns of missingness per variable and per case. (2) Compare respondents and non-respondents on observed variables (a logistic model for R, or a t-test per variable): differences show the data are not MCAR, but their absence does not prove it, and nothing here can distinguish MAR from MNAR. (3) After imputing, compare the distributions of observed and imputed values; differences are expected if MAR holds with predictors, but impossible values signal a bad model. (4) Check MICE convergence and the Monte Carlo error. (5) Report: percentages missing, the assumed mechanism and why, the imputation model (variables, method, M, software, seed), the pooled estimates with the fraction of missing information and a sensitivity analysis for the MNAR scenarios you consider plausible. A model-validation report for a regulator should state all of this.
