Uncertainty Quantification for ML

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Uncertainty quantification for machine learning: prediction intervals and sets with guarantees, and honesty about when they fail.

A score or a forecast without a statement of its uncertainty invites false precision: a probability of default of 4.2%, a churn score of 0.81, a loss forecast of 12.3 crore. Conformal prediction wraps any model, however complicated, in intervals or sets that contain the true outcome with a stated frequency, using only a held-out calibration set and the assumption that new cases resemble the calibration cases. This page teaches what that guarantee does and does not say (marginal against conditional coverage), how to build it for regression and classification, what breaks it (shift, drift, small groups) and how to repair it, how it differs from ensemble and model-based uncertainty, and how to use it to decide which cases a human should review.

Part I · Intervals and coverage

1Confidence interval or prediction interval? Beginner

A confidence interval describes where a population quantity (the average outcome at x) lies; its width falls like 1/√n and vanishes with enough data. A prediction interval describes where one new individual outcome will fall; it contains the irreducible noise of individuals and cannot be narrower than about ±1.96σ however large the sample. Decisions (approve a loan, flag a transaction, price a policy) concern individuals, so the relevant uncertainty is the prediction interval. Confusing the two is the commonest error: regression output prints standard errors and confidence bands of the fitted curve, which understate the uncertainty of any single case, more so the larger the data.

Prediction interval: ŷ ± tn−2 · s · √(1 + 1/n + (x − x̄)²/Sxx) · Confidence interval: drop the “1 +”

2Marginal against conditional coverage Beginner

“90% coverage” can mean two things. Marginal coverage: over the whole population, 90% of outcomes fall in their intervals. Conditional coverage: for each kind of case (each value of x, each segment), 90% fall in theirs. The first can hold while the second fails badly, whenever the noise level varies: a constant-width interval is too wide where the data are quiet and too narrow where they are noisy, and the cases in the noisy region are exactly those one most wants to be right about. Marginal coverage is attainable with weak assumptions; conditional coverage on every x is not (chapter 9). Always report coverage by segment.

Marginal: P(Y ∈ C(X)) ≥ 1 − α Conditional: P(Y ∈ C(X) | X = x) ≥ 1 − α for every x

3Split conformal prediction and its guarantee Intermediate

Fit any model on a training set. On a separate calibration set of n cases compute scores si = |yi − ŷi|. Let q̂ be the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest score and give every new case the interval ŷ ± q̂. If calibration and new cases are exchangeable, the coverage is at least 1 − α, for any model and any data distribution, in finite samples, with no assumption of normality. The coverage of one calibration set is random: it follows a Beta(n + 1 − l, l) distribution with l = ⌊(n + 1)α⌋, so with few calibration cases a given interval can miss the target noticeably even though the average is right. The guarantee says nothing about width: a bad model gives wide valid intervals.

P(Ynew ∈ [ŷ − q̂, ŷ + q̂]) ≥ 1 − α Coverage given calibration ~ Beta(n + 1 − l, l)

Part II · Scores, sets and shift

4Conformity scores: shaping the interval Intermediate

The recipe works for any score that measures how badly a case disagrees with the model. The absolute residual gives constant-width intervals. The normalised residual |y − ŷ|/σ̂(x) divides by an estimate of the local noise so intervals widen where errors are larger. Conformalised quantile regression (CQR) fits lower and upper quantile models and conformalises max(q̂lo(x) − y, y − q̂hi(x)). The score changes the shape and efficiency but never the marginal guarantee. Two rules: the calibration set must not be used for fitting or tuning (otherwise the scores are too small and coverage fails), and a better model of the spread gives conditional coverage closer to the target and narrower intervals.

s(x, y) = |y − ŷ(x)| / σ̂(x) CQR: s = max(q̂lo(x) − y, y − q̂hi(x))

5Prediction sets for classification Advanced

For classification the output is a set of classes: with score 1 − p̂y the set contains every class k with 1 − p̂k ≤ q̂, and the coverage guarantee is the same. Sets are informative: size one means the model can decide, larger sets flag ambiguous cases, an empty set (possible) flags a case that fits no class. Validity holds even for an overconfident or poorly calibrated model; it only affects the sizes. The marginal guarantee can hide a rare class that is covered far less than the others (its cases are few and harder), which for default, fraud or churn is the class of interest. Class-conditional (Mondrian) calibration, one threshold per class, restores coverage for each class at the cost of larger sets and noisier thresholds.

C(x) = {k : 1 − p̂k(x) ≤ q̂} Class-conditional: q̂k from the calibration cases of class k

6Covariate shift and weighted conformal prediction Advanced

Exchangeability fails when the new population differs from the calibration one: a new segment, a changed portfolio mix, a policy that selects different customers. If only the distribution of inputs changes (covariate shift) and the relationship between inputs and outcome is the same, coverage fails because the model’s errors depend on x. Weighted conformal prediction weights each calibration score by the density ratio w(x) = pnew(x)/pold(x), which can be estimated with a classifier that tells old from new inputs, and recovers valid coverage. The cost is the effective sample size (Σw)²/Σw², which collapses under strong shift, and the density ratio is itself an estimate. A change in the relationship (concept drift) is not repaired by any reweighting.

Weighted quantile of the scores with weights w(xi), plus the test point’s own weight neff = (Σw)² / Σw²

Part III · Uncertainty in practice

7Drift and adaptive conformal inference Advanced

On a live model the world drifts, errors grow, and a fixed threshold calibrated in the past stops covering. Adaptive conformal inference (ACI) treats the working miscoverage level as a quantity to be tracked: after each case, αt+1 = αt + γ(α − errt). Misses widen the next intervals and hits narrow them. Long-run coverage converges to 1 − α under arbitrary drift, a frequency guarantee over time, not per case. The step γ trades reaction speed against jumpy widths. ACI keeps the statement true by widening, not by fixing the model: persistent widening is a retraining signal, and rolling coverage is itself a drift monitor.

αt+1 = αt + γ (α − errt) long-run miscoverage → α

8Ensembles, epistemic uncertainty and conformal Expert

Uncertainty has two parts. Epistemic uncertainty is lack of knowledge about the function (little data, an unexplored region) and shrinks with data; aleatoric uncertainty is the noise of the individual outcome and does not. Ensembles (bootstrap, deep ensembles, dropout) measure the epistemic part through disagreement among members. Used alone as an interval, the spread understates the uncertainty of a new outcome and gets worse with more data because it falls to zero while the noise does not. Combining it with a noise estimate repairs it only if the noise model is right. Conformal calibration around the ensemble mean needs no such assumption, and the ensemble spread is useful as a normaliser in the score (chapter 4).

Var(ynew | x) = epistemic (ensemble spread²) + aleatoric (noise²)

9Conditional validity and small groups Expert

Distribution-free guarantees conditional on every x are impossible in general: any method with finite-sample conditional coverage must give intervals of infinite expected length for non-atomic distributions. What is attainable is coverage conditional on groups with enough cases, by calibrating each group (Mondrian conformal) separately. With n cases in a group, the coverage in that group is again a Beta-distributed random variable with spread of order 1/√n, and with fewer than 1/α cases the interval is infinite. Pooling groups gives stable thresholds but coverage that varies by group; per-group calibration gives group validity but noisy results. Normalised scores, grouping of similar groups and honest group-level monitoring are the practical compromises.

Group g: q̂g = ⌈(ng + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest score in g coverage in g ~ Beta(ng + 1 − l, l)

10Selective prediction: when to abstain Expert

An uncertainty measure earns its keep when it decides which cases to automate and which to send to a human. A selective system abstains on the cases with the widest intervals or largest sets. The value is the gap between the error on the decided cases when abstaining by uncertainty and when abstaining at random, which depends on how much the uncertainty varies between cases and on how well the score ranks them. The coverage guarantee covers the whole population, not the self-selected decided subset, whose accuracy must be measured directly. Capacity for manual review should set the abstention rate. In AML alert triage and credit decisioning, interval width or set size is a defensible triage criterion because it is a stated, auditable quantity.

11Model-based against distribution-free intervals Expert

Model-based intervals (Gaussian regression, Bayesian posteriors) are exact if the model is right and can be miscalibrated if it is not: heavy tails, curvature, heteroscedasticity or a wrong link make the stated 90% something else, usually in a level-dependent way. Conformal intervals need only exchangeability, are valid at every level (given enough calibration cases: 99% needs at least 100), and are often as narrow or narrower under heavy tails. Neither fixes a wrong model’s conditional behaviour. The practical rule is to check calibration on held-out data, the actual-against-nominal coverage plot, for whatever interval is reported, and to prefer a distribution-free wrapper around a model-based interval for regulatory use, since its validity does not depend on the model being right.
