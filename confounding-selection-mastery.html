Confounding & Selection

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Confounding, selection bias, colliders and Simpson’s paradox: when an association is not the effect you want.

Most questions a bank asks are causal: does the limit increase raise spend or default, does a retention call reduce churn, does a new collections strategy work? Data collected without randomisation answers a different question, namely how outcomes differ between the people who happened to get each treatment. The gap between the two has a small number of sources: confounding (a common cause), selection (who enters the data, often through a collider), over-adjustment (controlling for something the treatment causes), measurement (an imperfect control) and chance selection (regression to the mean). This page names them with causal diagrams, simulates each with a known truth so the bias can be measured, and ends with sensitivity analysis and negative controls for what cannot be adjusted away.

Part I · Confounding and causal diagrams

1From association to effect: confounding Beginner

Let X be a treatment (a limit increase, a retention offer) and Y an outcome. The causal effect asks what Y would be for the same people if X were changed; the observed association compares different people. A confounder Z is a common cause of X and Y: customers with better finances get larger limits and repay better, so limits look protective even if they do nothing. If Z is measured, comparing like with like (adjusting for Z) removes the bias; if it is not, the bias remains however large the sample.

In the linear model Y = βX + κZ + ε with X = γZ + e (unit variances), regressing Y on X alone gives not β but

naive slope = β + κγ / (1 + γ²) bias = (effect of Z on Y) × (association of Z with X)

The bias has the sign of κγ and does not shrink with n: more data only makes the wrong answer more precise. Randomisation breaks the arrow Z → X (γ = 0), which is why it is the benchmark. Confounding is a property of the causal structure, not of the data: a variable is a confounder because of how it is generated, and no statistical test of the data alone can establish this.

2Simpson’s paradox Beginner

Simpson’s paradox is confounding seen in a table: a trend that appears in every subgroup reverses when the groups are pooled. A classic bank version: approval rates are higher for one channel than the other in each customer segment, yet lower overall, because that channel receives mostly applications from the hard segment. The numbers are all correct; the paradox is only in the temptation to read the pooled figure causally.

Which table to trust is not a statistical question. If the segment is a confounder (it influences who is in which channel and the outcome), the segment-specific comparison is closer to the effect. If the “segment” is instead a mediator or collider (caused by the treatment, or by both treatment and outcome), the pooled comparison is the right one and stratifying creates bias. The diagram, not the data, decides: that is the lesson of the whole page. In regression form, the pooled slope is a mixture of the within-group slope b and the between-group trend:

pooled slope ≈ b − h·s·Var(g) / (1 + s²·Var(g)) (groups g = 0,1,2 shifted by s in x and by −h in y)

3Causal diagrams: chains, forks and colliders Intermediate

A causal diagram (DAG) draws an arrow from each variable to the variables it directly causes. Only three local structures matter for association:

  • Chain X → Z → Y: Z is a mediator. X and Y are associated; conditioning on Z blocks the path.
  • Fork X ← Z → Y: Z is a common cause (confounder). X and Y are associated through Z; conditioning on Z blocks the path.
  • Collider X → Z ← Y: Z is a common effect. X and Y are not associated; conditioning on Z (or on anything caused by Z) opens the path and creates an association.

Association flows along every path that is not blocked; a path is blocked by conditioning on a non-collider on it, or by not conditioning on a collider (and its descendants). Two variables are d-separated by a set S if all paths between them are blocked given S; in a causal model d-separation implies conditional independence. The collider rule is the counter-intuitive one and the source of most selection bias: it says that “adjusting for more” can create a relationship that was not there.

4The backdoor criterion and adjustment sets Intermediate

To estimate the total effect of X on Y from observational data, close every non-causal (“backdoor”) path from X to Y, meaning every path that starts with an arrow into X, and leave the causal paths open. A set S satisfies the backdoor criterion if it contains no descendant of X and blocks every backdoor path. Then the effect is identified by adjusting for S: E[Y | do(X = x)] = Σ_s E[Y | X = x, S = s] P(S = s). In practice this is a regression with S as covariates (correct if the model is correct) or matching, weighting or stratification on S.

Rules of thumb that follow from the diagram: include common causes of X and Y; exclude mediators (for the total effect), colliders and their descendants, and anything caused by the treatment; optional but useful: causes of Y only (they reduce variance); harmful for precision: causes of X only (they inflate variance, and amplify any residual bias). A variable being “correlated with both” is not enough to make it a confounder; being “pre-treatment” is also not enough (chapter 8).

Part II · Selection and bad controls

5Collider bias and selection Advanced

Selection bias arises when the data are restricted by something caused by both the exposure and the outcome (or by their causes). Conditioning on such a collider, whether by explicit filtering, by who responds, by who is approved or by who survives, induces an association between its causes even if there is none in the population. This is Berkson’s paradox: in hospital patients, two unrelated diseases look negatively associated because each independently raises the chance of admission. The banking version: among approved applicants, income and credit history look negatively related, because approval needs a high combination of the two; weak on one means strong on the other.

Three consequences recur. (1) Relationships estimated on a selected sample can be wrong in sign and not just in size (“higher bureau score, riskier customer”). (2) The distortion depends on how hard the selection is: strongest when the filter is tight and when it is driven by the variables in question. (3) It cannot be diagnosed from the selected data alone; you need to know the selection mechanism or have data on the unselected. The same logic covers volunteer and non-response bias, attrition, survivor bias, and “controlling for” a variable that is a consequence of both the treatment and the outcome.

6Mediators, over-adjustment and “bad controls” Advanced

Suppose a pricing offer X is randomised, and it works partly through the customer’s line utilisation M, which also depends on unobserved appetite for credit U (and U also drives default Y). The total effect of X is the full change in Y, including the path through M; it is estimated by regressing Y on X, with no controls at all (randomisation). The direct effect (the part not through M) is what you would try to get by also controlling for M. It fails whenever U exists: M is a collider on X → M ← U, so conditioning on M links X to U, and U drives Y. A clean randomised comparison becomes biased through an innocent-looking control.

Hence a practical rule: do not control for post-treatment variables unless the question is explicitly about direct effects and you can defend the absence of unmeasured common causes of mediator and outcome (a much stronger assumption than for the total effect). The same holds for outcomes measured after the treatment, such as “among customers who stayed”. Formal mediation analysis uses natural direct and indirect effects; both need sequential ignorability.

7Survivorship, truncation and reject inference Advanced

A credit model is trained on loans that were booked, so the outcome is observed only for approved applicants: the training data are a selected sample. Whether this matters depends on how selection depended on the data. If approval depends only on the observed features X (selection on observables, “missing at random” given X) and the model is correctly specified, the relation of Y to X in the approved region is unbiased; the danger is extrapolation: the model never saw the rejected region, and if the true relationship is curved it will be wrong there. If approval also used information not in the data (a banker’s override, an unrecorded document, a policy exception: U), then among the approved, X and U are correlated through the selection collider, and the estimated coefficients are biased even within the approved region.

Remedies: reject inference (parcelling, augmentation, fuzzy or extrapolation methods; each rests on assumptions that the data cannot verify), exploration (a small random-approval band, the only fully assumption-free source of rejected-region outcomes), through-the-door and cut-off-neighbourhood designs (chapter on regression discontinuity in the next page), bounds (worst-case outcomes for the rejected), and conservative treatment of the rejected region in policy. The same structure covers survivorship bias (analysing only the funds, branches or customers that still exist), left truncation (customers observed only after they entered the book) and immortal-time bias (misclassifying the period during which an event could not have happened).

8Imperfect adjustment: proxies, residual confounding and M-bias Advanced

Noisy confounders. Adjustment works on the variable actually entered. If the true confounder Z (income, say) is measured with error, or only a proxy (a postcode, a salary-credit band) is available, adjusting for the proxy removes only part of the bias: residual confounding remains, shrinking as reliability rises. With measurement error that is itself related to treatment, the leftover can even be amplified. The consequence is that “we controlled for it” should be read as “to the extent that the control measures it”.

M-bias. A pre-treatment variable that is a collider is a bad control even though it is “before” the treatment: with U₁ → X, U₁ → M, U₂ → M, U₂ → Y and no effect of X on Y, M is associated with both X and Y yet adjusting for it produces a spurious effect (conditioning on the collider M links U₁ to U₂). The M-bias is usually modest compared with omitted-confounder bias, which is why practitioners tend to adjust for pre-treatment variables, but it shows that the rule is “block backdoor paths”, not “adjust for everything measured before treatment”.

Part III · Chance selection, sensitivity and practice

9Regression to the mean as selection Advanced

If a measurement is partly stable level and partly transient noise, units selected for an extreme value at time 1 are, on average, less extreme at time 2 with no intervention at all. The expected change of the selected group is −(1 − r)·(its mean standardised score at time 1), where r is the correlation between the two measurements. Banks meet it constantly: accounts flagged for the worst delinquency this month, branches with the worst churn last quarter, customers with the highest utilisation spike. A “before and after” comparison of the treated flagged group then shows improvement that is largely selection on noise.

The fix is a concurrent control group drawn from the same selection rule: among the flagged, randomise who gets the action. Comparing with unflagged units does not work (they have a different mean), nor does a control that was selected on a different measure. Related: Campbell’s law, where selection on a target metric invites gaming, and the winner’s curse (multiple testing page) for selection of the best of many.

10Sensitivity to unmeasured confounding Expert

Unmeasured confounding cannot be tested away, but its required strength can be quantified. Omitted variable bias: if the unmeasured U changes the outcome by κ per unit and the treatment by γ per unit (in a linear model with unit-variance noise), the naive estimate is off by κγ/(1 + γ²). Sensitivity analysis asks how large κ and γ must be to explain the observed estimate (to turn it to zero or to reverse it) and then whether such a confounder is plausible given the measured ones (benchmarking against the strongest observed confounder, e.g. “as strong as income”).

The E-value (VanderWeele and Ding) does this for a risk ratio RR ≥ 1: the minimum strength of association, on the risk-ratio scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to explain away the estimate,

E-value = RR + √( RR·(RR − 1) ) (for RR < 1 use 1/RR); apply to the confidence limit closer to 1 as well

RR = 1.8 gives 3.0: a confounder would need to raise both the odds of treatment and of the outcome threefold. Report sensitivity as part of any observational claim: it states what has been assumed rather than hidden.

11Negative controls, falsification and practice Expert

A negative control outcome N is an outcome that the treatment cannot affect but that shares the same confounders as the real outcome (a pre-treatment outcome, an outcome in an unrelated product, a placebo period). If the analysis finds a “treatment effect” on N, the analysis is confounded. A negative control exposure is a treatment that cannot affect Y but is subject to the same confounding. Falsification tests of this sort are cheap and often more persuasive than another adjustment. Under a proportionality assumption (the confounder affects N and Y in a known ratio), the apparent effect on N can even be used to correct the estimate for Y. If the match is poor, the correction is wrong, and this is the limitation to state.

A practical workflow for a bank causal question: draw the diagram with the business owners (who decides the treatment, with what information?), list what is measured and what is not, choose the adjustment set from the diagram, check overlap (every type of customer has some chance of each treatment), estimate with a method chosen in the next pages, run a negative control and a sensitivity analysis, and document assumptions alongside the estimate for validation and regulators. Prefer an experiment, even a small randomised band, wherever it is feasible.
