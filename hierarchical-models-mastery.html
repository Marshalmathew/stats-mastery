Hierarchical Models

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Hierarchical models: groups that learn from each other, without pretending to be identical.

A bank’s data come in groups: branches, merchants, customer segments, products, vintages, regions. Some groups have thousands of observations and many have a handful. Analysing each group separately (no pooling) gives noisy, extreme estimates for the small ones; lumping everything together (complete pooling) throws away real differences. A hierarchical (multilevel, partial pooling) model treats the groups’ true effects as draws from a common population whose spread is itself estimated: each group borrows exactly as much strength from the others as the data justify. This page works through the normal hierarchical model, where the shrinkage can be seen in closed form, then the issues that decide whether it works in practice: estimating the spread, priors for it, the funnel that breaks samplers, full against empirical Bayes, varying slopes, group-level predictors, new groups, rankings, and the situation where pooling does harm.

Part I · Pooling and shrinkage

1Complete, no and partial pooling Beginner

Suppose J groups each give an estimate ȳⱼ of an underlying group effect θⱼ with noise variance vⱼ = σ²/nⱼ. Three ways to estimate θⱼ. No pooling: θ̂ⱼ = ȳⱼ. Unbiased, but noisy when nⱼ is small, and the most extreme values are largely noise. Complete pooling: θ̂ⱼ = ȳ for all j. Very stable, but wrong by the full group difference if the groups really differ. Partial pooling: assume θⱼ ~ N(μ, τ²), where τ measures how much the groups differ, and estimate θⱼ by a weighted average of ȳⱼ and the overall mean μ, with weights set by the noise variance against τ². When τ is large partial pooling behaves like no pooling; when τ is near 0 like complete pooling; in between it beats both. This is the same idea as the beta-binomial shrinkage of item 26, now with the group spread τ as an explicit, estimable quantity.

2The shrinkage factor Intermediate

With known μ, τ and σ the posterior for group j is normal, and its mean is a weighted average:

E[θⱼ | y] = (1 − Bⱼ)·ȳⱼ + Bⱼ·μ, Bⱼ = (σ²/nⱼ) / (σ²/nⱼ + τ²), Var[θⱼ | y] = (1 − Bⱼ)·σ²/nⱼ

Bⱼ is the shrinkage factor: the share of the group’s total variance that is noise. It is near 1 (shrink almost all the way to μ) for groups with few observations or when τ is small, and near 0 for large groups or very different groups. Two consequences. The posterior variance (1 − Bⱼ)vⱼ is always smaller than vⱼ: pooling reduces uncertainty for every group, most of all for the smallest. And shrinkage cures the winner’s curse: if you rank groups by raw mean, the top ones include small groups with lucky noise, and their raw means overstate their true effects; the pooled means correct this in proportion to each group’s noise.

3Estimating the spread τ Intermediate

In practice τ is unknown, and it is the hardest quantity in the model: it is learned from only J group means. A classic fact: with few groups, or noise large against τ, the maximum-likelihood or moment estimate of τ is very often exactly zero, which silently switches the model to complete pooling. The Bayesian answer is the marginal posterior of τ, p(τ | y) ∝ p(τ) · ∏ N(ȳⱼ | μ̂, σⱼ² + τ²) (with μ integrated out), which for a normal model can be computed on a grid. It is typically wide, skewed to the right and not at zero, and averaging the group estimates over it makes shrinkage depend honestly on how well τ is known. The practical rules: report an interval for τ, expect it to be wide with fewer than about eight to ten groups, and do not read a point estimate of 0 as evidence that groups are identical.

4Priors for the group spread Intermediate

Because τ is weakly identified, its prior is influential when J is small, and choosing it carelessly is the classic way to get a hierarchical model wrong. Good defaults are weakly informative priors on the standard deviation τ, on the scale of the outcome: a half-Cauchy or half-normal (or half-t), or a flat prior on τ when there are enough groups. The older habit of an inverse-gamma(ε, ε) prior on the variance τ² looks “non-informative” but is not: for small ε it concentrates mass near 0, and as the data weaken the posterior of τ follows the prior towards zero, over-shrinking all groups. Whatever prior you choose, check the sensitivity of the group estimates to it, plot the prior against the posterior to see how much the data moved it, and document the choice. If there are very few groups (say fewer than five), τ is essentially set by the prior, and the right response may be an informative prior from earlier or similar data.

Part II · Fitting and structure

5Sampling hierarchical models: the funnel Advanced

Hierarchical posteriors have a shape that defeats naive samplers: when τ is small the group effects θⱼ are forced close together, and when they are close together τ is forced to be small, so the joint density of (θ, τ) looks like a funnel, wide at large τ and a narrow neck at small τ. A sampler with one step size cannot suit both ends; in Hamiltonian Monte Carlo this shows up as divergent transitions near the neck. The standard fix is the non-centred parametrisation: write θⱼ = μ + τ·ηⱼ with ηⱼ ~ N(0, 1) and sample (μ, τ, η) instead, which makes the ηⱼ independent of τ a priori and removes the funnel. It helps most when data per group are weak or τ is small; with strong data the centred form is better, because then the likelihood makes θⱼ nearly independent of τ. Both parametrisations describe the same model; only the geometry the sampler sees changes. Practical consequences: if you see divergences in a multilevel model, try non-centring first, and be suspicious of a model whose τ posterior hits a wall at zero.

6Rates: full Bayes against empirical Bayes Advanced

For counts of defaults, fraud or conversions in each branch, the natural hierarchical model is beta-binomial: xⱼ ~ Binomial(nⱼ, θⱼ), θⱼ ~ Beta(mκ, (1 − m)κ), with population mean m and strength κ. Empirical Bayes estimates (m, κ) once from all the branches (as on the previous page) and treats them as known; full Bayes gives (m, κ) a prior and integrates over their posterior. For point estimates the two nearly agree. For intervals they differ: empirical Bayes ignores uncertainty in (m, κ) and its intervals are too short, an effect that grows as the number of branches falls. The marginal likelihood of (m, κ) is available in closed form (a product of beta functions), so a grid suffices here; in larger models the same job is done by MCMC. The practical lesson is to carry hyperparameter uncertainty through to the quantities you report, especially when you have few groups.

7Varying intercepts and slopes Advanced

Groups can differ in more than their level. In a model y = aⱼ + bⱼx + ε with each group’s intercept aⱼ and slope bⱼ drawn from a bivariate normal with mean (μ_a, μ_b) and covariance Σ, partial pooling shrinks the pair (aⱼ, bⱼ) towards the population mean, and the direction of shrinkage follows the correlation in Σ. For a group with few points, the posterior mean is (Σ⁻¹ + XⱼᵀXⱼ/σ²)⁻¹(Σ⁻¹μ + Xⱼᵀyⱼ/σ²): a precision-weighted average of the population mean and the group’s own least-squares fit, exactly the scalar formula in matrix form. This is the random-slopes mixed model of item 20 viewed as a Bayesian hierarchical model, with the extra benefits that the uncertainty in Σ propagates and that groups with too few points to fit a line (one or two) still get a sensible estimate. The covariance matrix needs a prior too; the standard choice factorises it into scales (half-t) and a correlation matrix (an LKJ prior).

8Group-level predictors Expert

The population distribution need not be a single normal. A group-level regression θⱼ = γ₀ + γ₁zⱼ + uⱼ, uⱼ ~ N(0, τ_u²), lets branch or segment characteristics zⱼ predict the group effects, and partial pooling shrinks each group towards its predicted value. The residual spread τ_u is smaller than the total spread, so the groups are shrunk more, but towards a better target; the net effect is lower error for small groups. This is the multilevel regression that, combined with poststratification (MRP), produces reliable small-area estimates from sparse data, a technique with direct uses for regional or segment-level risk and demand estimates. Two cautions. A group-level predictor correlated with an omitted group-level cause can create an ecological fallacy: associations between group averages need not hold within groups. And with few groups the regression coefficients γ are themselves poorly determined; give them proper priors.

Part III · Practice and limits

9Predicting a new group Expert

A hierarchical model answers two different questions. Existing groups: what is θⱼ for a group we have observed? (shrinkage, above). A new group: what do we expect for a branch, merchant or product that is not in the data? The answer comes from the population distribution: before any data, the new group’s effect is N(μ, τ²) (with the uncertainty in μ and τ carried along), and as its own data accumulate the prediction moves from the population towards the group’s own mean, with the same shrinkage weight as before. Treating the new group as identical to the average (complete pooling) gives intervals that are far too narrow; estimating it from its own data alone gives correct but, for small n, useless intervals. The hierarchical interval is both calibrated and narrower. Cold-start problems (a new branch, a new card product, a newly onboarded merchant) are the natural application, and the calibration of such predictions should be back-tested on groups held out entirely.

10Ranking and flagging groups Expert

Banks rank branches, relationship managers, merchants and models, and flag the worst. A ranking from raw means is dominated by sample size: small groups are over-represented at both ends. Hierarchical estimates improve it in two ways. The posterior means correct for size, and the posterior draws give a full posterior distribution of each group’s rank and the probability that a group belongs in the worst k%. Ranking by that probability (or by the posterior expected rank) is the sensible way to select a watch list, since it accounts for both the size of the estimated effect and the certainty. The rank intervals are often humbling: with realistic noise, even the “worst” groups’ ranks span a large part of the list, which is the honest message for any league table. Report the interval or the probability next to every flag; for supervisory or HR-sensitive uses, make this explicit, since a flag based on noise is an unfair one.

11When pooling hurts Expert

Partial pooling rests on exchangeability: before seeing the data, nothing distinguishes one group from another except its label, so all are draws from the same population. If that is false, shrinkage can do damage: a genuinely unusual group (a fraud ring, a mis-booked portfolio, a branch in a flood zone, a product with a new feature) is pulled towards the pack, and with a normal population its estimate is shrunk exactly as much as an ordinary group of the same size. Remedies: (1) add group-level predictors that explain the unusual group; (2) use a heavy-tailed population distribution (Student t or a scale mixture), which shrinks ordinary groups but lets extreme evidence through; (3) use a mixture population with an “other” component; (4) check with posterior predictive checks and with leave-one-group-out cross-validation, which highlights groups the model predicts poorly. Also watch for the opposite failure: a model that never shrinks (τ forced large by the prior) is no better than no pooling. Choose the population model deliberately and say why.
