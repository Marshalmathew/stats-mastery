Multiple Testing

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Multiple testing and selective inference: FWER, FDR, the winner’s curse and honest confidence intervals.

Test enough hypotheses and something will be significant. A bank screens thousands of customer segments, hundreds of candidate features, dozens of rules in a monitoring system, and an A/B test reports twenty metrics. The risk is not only a false positive; it is also that the selected winners look better than they are (the winner’s curse) and that confidence intervals computed after selection are too narrow. This page builds the error rates (FWER, FDR), the procedures (Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini–Hochberg, adaptive and empirical-null versions), the problems of selection (winner’s curse, subgroups, post-selection intervals, backtest overfitting) and the sequential version of the same problem (peeking), ending with false-discovery risk and governance.

Part I · Error rates and corrections

1The multiplicity problem Beginner

A single test at level α = 0.05 has a 5% chance of a false positive when the null is true. With m independent true nulls the chance that at least one is significant is 1 − (1 − α)^m: 64% for m = 20, 99.4% for m = 100. The expected number of false positives is mα, whatever the correlation. The “family” can be explicit (20 metrics in an experiment) or hidden: choices of outcome, subgroup, cut-off, transformation, model and sample, sometimes called the garden of forking paths or researcher degrees of freedom. If the analysis path was chosen after seeing the data, the effective number of tests is larger than the number reported.

P(at least one false positive) = 1 − (1 − α)^m ≈ mα for small mα E[false positives] = m₀ α

Two error rates are used to control this. The family-wise error rate (FWER) is P(V ≥ 1), where V is the number of false rejections: appropriate when a single false claim is costly (confirmatory claims, regulatory submissions). The false discovery rate (FDR) is E[V/R] with R the number of rejections (taken as 0 when R = 0): appropriate for screening where some false leads are acceptable but their share should be controlled. Controlling the per-test error at α controls neither.

2Controlling the family-wise error rate Intermediate

Bonferroni rejects H_i if p_i ≤ α/m: it controls the FWER at α under any dependence, using only the union bound. It is simple and very conservative when m is large, and loses power exactly when many effects are real. Šidák uses 1 − (1 − α)^{1/m}, slightly less conservative for independent tests. Holm (step-down) orders the p-values p₍₁₎ ≤ … ≤ p₍ₘ₎ and rejects while p₍ₖ₎ ≤ α/(m − k + 1), stopping at the first failure: it controls the FWER under any dependence and is uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni, so there is no reason to use plain Bonferroni when Holm is available. Hochberg (step-up) finds the largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ α/(m − k + 1) and rejects all hypotheses up to it: slightly more powerful, valid under independence or positive dependence. Closed testing and gatekeeping extend these to structured families (primary then secondary endpoints in a pre-specified order).

3The false discovery rate and Benjamini–Hochberg Intermediate

In screening, demanding zero false positives wastes discoveries. The Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure controls the FDR at q: order the p-values, find the largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ k q / m and reject the k smallest. Graphically, draw the line of slope q/m through the origin on the plot of ordered p-values against rank, and reject everything up to the last point under the line. Under independence (and positive dependence, PRDS) the FDR equals π₀ q, where π₀ = m₀/m is the share of true nulls, so BH is conservative when many effects are real. The threshold adapts: with many strong signals, the largest p-value rejected can be far above α/m.

The FDR is a statement about the average false discovery proportion (FDP = V/R) over repetitions, not about the one list in front of you, whose FDP can be higher or lower. The adjusted p-value (BH q-value) of a hypothesis is the smallest q at which it would be rejected; reporting “significant at FDR 10%” is clearer than reporting adjusted p-values as if they were ordinary ones.

4Dependence, adaptive FDR and q-values Advanced

BH is proven under independence and positive regression dependence (PRDS); for arbitrary dependence the Benjamini–Yekutieli version divides q by Σ1/i (about ln m + 0.58), a large loss of power. In practice BH is robust to moderate correlation in the mean FDR, but the variability of the false discovery proportion grows with correlation: with strongly correlated tests the list is all right in most data sets and badly wrong in a few, because a common factor moves all the statistics together (a market-wide move, a batch effect, a shared confounder).

Storey’s adaptive procedures estimate the share of nulls, π̂₀ = #{p > λ} / ((1 − λ)m) with λ = 0.5, and run BH at level q/π̂₀ (equivalently define the q-value, the FDR analogue of the p-value). This gains power when π₀ is well below 1, and is valid for independent or weakly dependent p-values but can be anti-conservative under strong dependence. For correlated tests, permutation-based FDR estimates and factor-adjusted statistics are alternatives.

Part II · Selection and its biases

5The winner’s curse and regression to the mean Advanced

When many noisy estimates are ranked and the top ones are chosen, the chosen estimates are biased upwards: part of their apparent size is luck that got them selected. This is the winner’s curse (or selection bias, or regression to the mean): the best-performing branch, the strongest feature, the most profitable strategy, the segment with the highest uplift will do worse next time on average. The bias grows with the number of candidates, with the noise in each estimate and with the share of candidates with nearly equal true effects.

Remedies: (1) independent confirmation: re-estimate the selected items on new data (split the sample, use a later period, a holdout); (2) shrinkage / empirical Bayes: estimate each effect by θ̂ᵢ = ȳ + B(yᵢ − ȳ) with B = 1 − s²/Var(y), which pulls extreme estimates towards the mean in proportion to their noise; (3) hierarchical models (the same partial pooling as in mixed models); (4) correct inference for selection (chapter 7). The expected true effect of a selected item is the shrunken estimate, not the raw one.

6Subgroup analyses and interaction tests Advanced

Subgroup analyses are multiple testing in disguise: S subgroups give S tests. Two classic errors follow. (1) A treatment with no effect has a 1 − 0.95^S chance of looking significant in some subgroup. (2) A treatment with the same true effect everywhere will often be significant in some subgroups and not in others, which is read as “works in A, not in B”. The fact that one result is significant and another is not does not mean they differ: the difference between “significant” and “not significant” is not itself statistically significant. The valid question, whether the effect differs across subgroups, is answered by a single interaction (heterogeneity) test, with the right size whatever the number of subgroups, and by estimates with intervals for each subgroup rather than verdicts.

Good practice: pre-specify a small number of subgroups with a rationale, test the interaction, adjust the subgroup tests (Holm), treat the overall effect as the default estimate for each subgroup and label the rest exploratory. In credit and marketing the same logic applies to “uplift” in segments and to model performance across portfolios.

7Selective inference: confidence intervals after selection Expert

If you select the parameters that look interesting and then report the usual 95% intervals for them, the intervals do not have 95% coverage for the selected parameters. Selecting an estimate because |y| > 1.96 and then reporting y ± 1.96 is the extreme case: the interval never contains 0, whatever the truth. The same happens with coefficients kept by stepwise regression or lasso, with the top-ranked strategies, and with significant subgroups. Valid options: (1) simultaneous intervals over the whole family (Bonferroni, or Scheffé-type), valid for any selection but wide; (2) sample splitting or an independent replication: select on one part, estimate on the other (cost: power, and the answer depends on the split); (3) conditional (selective) inference: intervals with the right coverage given the selection event, computed from the truncated distribution of the estimate (exact for a normal estimate selected by a threshold); they can be very wide near the threshold, which is honest; (4) data-fission and related randomisation methods; (5) Bayesian shrinkage with a hierarchical prior.

8Backtest overfitting and the multiplicity of model search Expert

Trying K strategies, feature sets or hyperparameters and keeping the best is a multiple-testing procedure whose statistic is the maximum. The maximum of K independent noise estimates grows with K: for standard normals it is about √(2 ln K), so with 1000 strategies of zero true edge the best in-sample (annualised) Sharpe ratio, whose standard error is √(252/T) for T days, is roughly 3.2 standard errors. Its out-of-sample Sharpe is zero on average. The same structure underlies feature selection on many candidates, tuning many hyperparameters on one validation set, and picking the best of many rules in transaction monitoring. The corrections are the ones in this page: account for the number of trials (deflated Sharpe ratio, whose benchmark is the expected maximum under the null), hold out data not touched by the search (and touch it once), use cross-validation over the whole search procedure, report all the trials and the dispersion of their results, and compare the final choice with simple benchmarks.

Part III · Large scale and practice

9Large-scale inference: local fdr and the empirical null Expert

With thousands of tests the data themselves tell you about the null. In the two-groups model each z-score comes from the null density f₀ with probability π₀ and from an alternative f₁ otherwise, so the mixture density is f = π₀f₀ + π₁f₁. The local false discovery rate is fdr(z) = π₀ f₀(z) / f(z): the posterior probability that a case with score z is null. Reject where fdr(z) is small (say below 0.2); the tail-area FDR is the average of fdr over the rejected region. The theoretical null f₀ = N(0,1) is correct only if the analysis is clean. Unmeasured confounding, batch effects, correlated noise and model misspecification typically make the bulk of the z-scores wider (N(μ₀, σ₀²) with σ₀ > 1); applying BH with the theoretical null then produces many false discoveries. Efron’s empirical null estimates (μ₀, σ₀) from the central part of the histogram (here by the median and MAD), which is where the nulls dominate.

10Peeking: sequential testing and group-sequential boundaries Expert

Looking at an experiment repeatedly and stopping when p < 0.05 is multiple testing over time, and the looks are strongly correlated (the same accumulating data). With 5 looks the false positive rate of the “stop at the first p < 0.05” rule is about 14%; with 20 looks it is about 25%; with continuous monitoring it approaches 1. The cure is a pre-specified group-sequential design: boundaries on the z-statistic at each look chosen so that the overall error is α. Bonferroni (α/L at each look) is valid but wasteful; Pocock uses a constant boundary (2.41 for 5 looks), spending the error evenly, and makes it easy to stop early; O’Brien–Fleming uses a boundary c√(L/k) that is very strict early (about 4.6 at the first of 5 looks) and close to the usual 1.96 at the end, so it keeps almost full power at the final look. Alpha-spending functions generalise this to unequally spaced looks, and always-valid (mixture-sequential or e-value) methods allow unlimited looks. Estimates after early stopping are biased, another instance of selection.

11False discovery risk, replication and governance Expert

Whether a “significant” finding is true depends on how often true effects are tested, not only on α. The positive predictive value of a significant result is PPV = π·power / (π·power + (1 − π)α), with π the prior probability that a tested hypothesis is real. With π = 0.1, power 0.6 and α = 0.05, PPV = 0.06/(0.06 + 0.045) = 57%: nearly half of the discoveries are false. Analytic flexibility (bias u: the share of analyses that would have been negative but are made positive by choices) raises the effective α and the effective power and lowers the PPV. An honest replication has the same α and power, so it confirms true effects with probability equal to the power and false ones with probability α; the surviving list is much cleaner.

Governance: write the analysis plan before looking (hypotheses, primary and secondary outcomes, subgroups, corrections, stopping rules); log all the tests run, including the ones not reported; use confirmatory data (holdout, later period, new customers) before deployment; report effect sizes with intervals and say which results are exploratory; in a model-validation file, document the number of candidate models and features tried and how the final choice accounts for it.
