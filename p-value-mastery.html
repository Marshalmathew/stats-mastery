Part I · Foundations

1The big idea: surprise under a skeptic’s assumption Beginner

A friend hands you a coin and swears it’s fair. You flip it 20 times and get 15 heads. Suspicious? Probably a bit. But how suspicious? Fair coins don’t give exactly 10 heads every time — randomness wiggles. We need a way to measure how surprising the result is if the coin really were fair.

That is the entire idea of a p-value:

Definition (plain English)The p-value is the probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one you got, assuming the skeptic’s hypothesis (the “null”) is true.

Notice the two moving parts. “Assuming the null is true” — we build an imaginary world where nothing interesting is happening. “At least as extreme” — we count the observed result and everything more dramatic, because any single outcome is improbable (one exact sequence of 20 flips has probability ≈ 1 in a million, even a perfectly typical one).

Lab 1 · Is this coin fair?

Bars are the probability of each head-count if the coin is fair. Red bars = outcomes at least as extreme as yours. The p-value is the red area.

Things to try. (1) 20 flips: 14 heads gives p ≈ 0.115 — unremarkable. 15 heads gives p ≈ 0.041, 16 gives ≈ 0.012. (2) Set 200 flips and 110 heads: that’s the same 55% heads rate but p ≈ 0.18 — more data would make the same rate convincing. (3) Press “Flip a truly fair coin” many times — notice how often a perfectly fair coin yields p < 0.05 (about 1 time in 20).

The sentence to tattoo on your brainA p-value is about the data, given a hypothesis: P(data this extreme | H₀). It is not “the probability H₀ is true” — that would be P(H₀ | data), a different quantity (Chapter 14 shows the gap can be huge).

2The formal machinery Beginner

Every p-value, from a coin flip to a Cox regression, comes from the same six-step recipe:

  1. State the hypotheses. H₀ (the null: no effect, no difference, coefficient = 0) and H₁ (the alternative).
  2. Pick a test statistic T — one number that summarises how far the data are from H₀ (a difference in means, a z-score, a χ²…).
  3. Know the null distribution of T — how T behaves across repeated experiments if H₀ were true. This is the heart of the whole thing.
  4. Compute T from your data: tobs.
  5. Compute the p-value as the tail area of the null distribution beyond tobs.
  6. Decide and report: compare with a pre-chosen α, and report the effect size and a confidence interval.
right-tailed: p = P( T ≥ t_obs | H₀ )
left-tailed: p = P( T ≤ t_obs | H₀ )
two-sided: p = P( |T| ≥ |t_obs| | H₀ ) (= 2 × one tail for a symmetric T)

3Seeing the p-value: the null world Beginner

Here is the most useful way to understand p-values: simulate the null world. Run 10,000 imaginary experiments where H₀ is true, compute the test statistic each time, and ask what fraction look at least as extreme as yours. That fraction is the p-value — the formulas are just a shortcut for this simulation.

4One-sided, two-sided, α, and decisions Beginner

One-sided vs two-sided

If you ask “is the new fraud rule different from the old one?” extremes in both directions count as evidence → two-sided. If you ask only “is it better?” and a worse result would be treated the same as no result → one-sided. When the observed direction agrees with your prediction, pone = ptwo / 2. That halving is tempting, which is exactly why you must choose the direction before looking. Switching to one-sided after seeing the data is a quiet form of p-hacking. Default to two-sided unless there’s a genuine, pre-declared reason.

The significance level α and the decision rule

In the Neyman–Pearson framework you fix α in advance (commonly 0.05) — the long-run false-alarm rate you’re willing to tolerate — and then: if p ≤ α, reject H₀; otherwise fail to reject. Never “accept H₀”: not finding evidence isn’t finding evidence of absence (Chapter 11).

Two traditions, often mashed togetherFisher (1925): p is a continuous measure of evidence against H₀ — report it, interpret it, no hard cutoff. Neyman–Pearson (1933): fix α and power, make a binary decision, control long-run error rates. Modern practice blends both, which is the source of much confusion. Be explicit about which one you’re doing.

Reporting well

  • Give the exact p (“p = 0.031”), not just “p < 0.05” — and “p < 0.0001” rather than “p = 0.0000”.
  • Always accompany it with the effect size and a confidence interval.
  • State α, the test, one/two-sided, and any multiplicity correction before seeing results.
  • Prefer “the data are/are not compatible with H₀ at the 5% level” over “proved/disproved”.
The 0.05 line is a convention, not a law of natureFisher suggested it as a convenient rough guide. A proposal in 2018 (Benjamin et al.) argued for 0.005 for new discoveries. Physics uses ~3×10⁻⁷. The right threshold depends on the cost of errors and the prior plausibility of the claim.
Part II · Everyday tests

5The t-test lab Intermediate

When σ is unknown you estimate it with s, which adds uncertainty, so the null distribution of the statistic has heavier tails than the normal — the Student t. Fewer degrees of freedom = heavier tails = you need a bigger statistic to be “significant”.

Lab 3a · t versus normal

Three flavours to know: one-sample (mean vs a fixed value), two-sample (use Welch’s version — it doesn’t assume equal variances and costs almost nothing), and paired (take within-pair differences, then it’s a one-sample test). Assumptions that actually matter: independent observations (violations are the most dangerous), and roughly well-behaved means (CLT helps with large n; heavy skew/outliers hurt at small n).

Lab 3b · Two groups, live

Group A has mean 100. Group B has mean 100 + true difference. Samples are drawn fresh with the button; sliders reuse the same random draws so you can watch one dataset change.

Experiments. Set the true difference to 0 and press “Draw new samples” 20 times: expect ≈1 “significant” result, purely by luck. Now set a true difference of 4 with small n: the p-value jumps around wildly between draws — p-values are noisy statistics, not stable properties of the world.

6Counts and tables: χ² and Fisher’s exact test Intermediate

For categorical outcomes (confirmed-fraud vs not, churned vs retained) the test compares observed counts with the counts expected under independence. Large χ² → the table is far from what independence predicts. Fisher’s exact test calculates the p-value directly from the hypergeometric distribution (no large-sample approximation), so it’s the safe choice when any expected count is small (< 5).

Lab 4 · 2×2 table: two alert rules, confirmed-mule rate

Notice the p-value answers “could this table arise if both rules had the same confirmation rate?”, while the odds ratio (with its CI) and Cramér’s V answer “how big is the difference?”. You need both.

7Models: Wald, likelihood-ratio and score tests Intermediate

In regression (logistic credit-risk models, Cox models, GLMs) each coefficient’s p-value usually comes from a Wald test: z = β̂ / SE(β̂), compared with a normal (or t) distribution. Two siblings test the same null with different geometry:

  • Wald — uses only the fitted model: how many standard errors is β̂ from 0? Quick, what your software prints.
  • Likelihood-ratio (LR) — compares the log-likelihood with and without the term: 2(ℓ₁−ℓ₀) ~ χ²df. Usually the most trustworthy in small/moderate samples, and the one for testing several coefficients at once.
  • Score (Rao) — uses only the null-fitted model; the basis of many classical tests (log-rank, Cochran–Armitage).

All three agree asymptotically; with small samples, rare events or large coefficients they can disagree materially. Beware the Hauck–Donner effect: with very large effects (or quasi-separation) the Wald SE inflates faster than β̂, so the Wald p-value gets larger while the evidence gets stronger. Prefer LR there.

Part III · Errors and evidence

8Type I & II errors, power and sample size Intermediate

α is what you choose (a property of the procedure when H₀ is true). Power is what you must earn: it depends on the true effect size, the noise, the sample size and α. Underpowered studies are the root of most evidence problems — they miss real effects and make the “significant” ones exaggerated (Chapter 14).

Lab 6 · Power, α and β on one picture

Two-sample comparison; effect d in SD units (Cohen’s d). Grey curve = null world. Blue curve = the real world with effect d. Red = α, yellow = β (missed effects), green = power.

n per group ≈ 2 · ( z1−α/2 + zpower )² / d² (80% power, α=.05 → n ≈ 15.7 / d²)

Rules of thumb: halving the detectable effect needs ~4× the sample. Going from 80% to 90% power costs ~34% more data. Minimum detectable effect (MDE) — the smallest effect your design can reliably detect — is the honest way to describe a study’s limits. And never compute “observed power” after the fact: it is just a re-expression of the p-value and tells you nothing new.

9The p-value is itself a random variable Intermediate

This chapter contains the most clarifying fact in the course. If H₀ is true and the test statistic is continuous, then the p-value is uniformly distributed on [0,1]. Every value is equally likely; p = 0.03 is exactly as probable as p = 0.83. That is why a 0.05 threshold gives a 5% false-positive rate: P(p ≤ 0.05) = 0.05 by construction (the probability integral transform).

When H₁ is true, p-values pile up near 0 — and the stronger the effect/sample, the stronger the pile. This is the signature (“p-curve”) that distinguishes real effects from noise.

Lab 7 · 2,000 experiments, 2,000 p-values

Each run simulates 2,000 two-sample t-tests. Start at d = 0 (null true): flat histogram. Then raise d.

ValidityA p-value is valid if P(p ≤ u | H₀) ≤ u for all u. Discrete tests (Fisher’s exact, binomial) are usually conservative: their p-values are “super-uniform”, so the actual error rate is below α. Mid-p corrections reduce that conservatism.

10Effect size, confidence intervals, and the big-n trap Intermediate

A p-value mixes two things: how big the effect is and how precisely you measured it (sample size). Roughly, z ≈ effect ÷ standard error, and the SE shrinks like 1/√n. So with enough data any non-zero effect becomes “significant”. Significance answers “is it distinguishable from exactly zero?”, never “does it matter?”

Lab 8 · Same tiny effect, ever larger samples

Fix a true standardised effect and watch p collapse as n grows, while the effect never gets bigger. The bar at right shows whether the effect clears your “smallest effect of interest”.

This is the daily reality with bank-scale data: millions of rows make everything “significant”. The fix is to decide in advance the smallest effect of interest (SESOI) and ask whether the confidence interval lies above it. Reverse trap: a wide CI that spans both “nothing” and “huge” (p = 0.12) is inconclusive, not evidence of no effect.

CI ↔ p-value dualityA 95% CI contains exactly the parameter values that a two-sided α = 0.05 test would not reject. So “CI excludes 0” ⇔ “p < 0.05” (same test). The CI is richer: it shows magnitude and precision. Remember that “95%” describes the long-run behaviour of the procedure, not a 95% probability for this particular interval.

Effect-size cheat: Cohen’s d (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large — conventions, and context-dependent), odds ratio, risk difference, Cramér’s V, R². Always translate to units a stakeholder understands (“2.1 percentage points fewer false alerts”).

Part IV · How p-values go wrong

11Ten misconceptions Advanced

Decades of surveys show that large fractions of researchers — and statistics teachers — endorse some of these. Try to explain why each is wrong before opening it.

1. “p = 0.03 means there’s a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true.”No. p is computed assuming H₀ is true, so it can’t also be the probability of H₀. P(data | H₀) ≠ P(H₀ | data). Getting the latter needs a prior (Bayes’ theorem) — see Chapters 14 and 16.
2. “1 − p is the probability the effect is real.”No, same reason. With p = 0.03 and a skeptical prior (say only 10% of ideas pan out), the chance the “discovery” is false can be 30% or more.
3. “p > 0.05 means there is no effect / the groups are equal.”No. “Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.” A non-significant result with a wide CI is simply inconclusive. To claim equivalence you need an equivalence test (TOST, Chapter 17).
4. “p < 0.05 means the effect is important.”No. Significance depends on n. Tiny effects with big samples are significant (Lab 8). Importance is judged by effect size against a domain-defined threshold.
5. “A smaller p means a bigger effect.”No. p = effect × precision mixed together. p = 1e-9 with n = 5 million can be a smaller effect than p = 0.03 with n = 50.
6. “p = 0.05 means there’s a 95% chance the result will replicate.”No. If the true effect equals your observed one, the replication’s power is only ~50% at p = 0.05. Replication probability depends on the true effect and the replication’s power, not 1 − p.
7. “If I use α = 0.05, only 5% of my significant findings are false.”No. α is P(reject | H₀ true) — conditioned on the null. The share of false findings among discoveries (false-positive risk / FDR) depends on the prior and power; it’s often much larger than 5% (Lab 12).
8. “p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 are fundamentally different.”No. They are practically identical evidence. As Gelman & Stern put it, “the difference between significant and not significant is not itself statistically significant.” If group A is significant and group B isn’t, you must test A vs B directly.
9. “The p-value is the probability that the result was due to chance.”It is calculated under the assumption that chance alone is operating, so it can’t be the probability of that assumption. It also silently assumes every other model assumption (independence, distribution, no p-hacking…) is correct.
10. “A statistically significant result means the research hypothesis is true.”It means the data are unusual under H₀ and its auxiliary assumptions. Confounding, measurement error, selection, leakage or a violated independence assumption can all produce a small p with a false scientific conclusion.
The ASA’s six principles (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016)(1) P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified model. (2) They do not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true or that data were produced by chance alone. (3) Decisions should not rest only on whether p passes a threshold. (4) Proper inference needs full reporting and transparency. (5) A p-value does not measure effect size or importance. (6) By itself, a p-value is not a good measure of evidence about a model or hypothesis.

12Multiple testing Advanced

Each test at α = 0.05 has a 5% false-alarm chance when H₀ is true. Run many tests and the chances compound. With 100 true-null features you expect 5 “significant” ones; with 20 independent tests the probability of at least one false positive is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. This is exactly what happens in feature screening, segment-level monitoring, rule-by-rule performance reviews and any “look at everything” analysis.

Two notions of error control:

  • FWER (family-wise error rate): P(at least one false discovery). Controlled by Bonferroni (reject if p ≤ α/m) and the uniformly more powerful Holm step-down. Use when a single false claim is costly.
  • FDR (false discovery rate): expected proportion of false discoveries among the discoveries. Controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg (BH): sort p(1) ≤ … ≤ p(m), find the largest i with p(i) ≤ (i/m)·q, reject all up to i. Far more powerful; ideal for exploratory screening. (Valid under independence/positive dependence; BY handles arbitrary dependence at a cost.)

13Peeking, p-hacking and the garden of forking paths Advanced

The p-value’s guarantee holds only if the analysis was fixed before seeing the data. Anything that makes the analysis depend on the data breaks it:

  • Optional stopping — checking the p-value repeatedly and stopping when it crosses 0.05 (the classic A/B-test sin).
  • Subgroup slicing, outcome switching, flexible outlier exclusion, transforming until something works, switching to one-sided, HARKing (hypothesising after results are known).
  • Forking paths (Gelman & Loken): even without conscious hacking, if you’d have analysed the data differently had it looked different, the effective number of tests is much larger than the number you ran.

14False-positive risk and the winner’s curse Advanced

What most people want from a p-value is P(H₀ | significant). To get it you need two things the p-value doesn’t contain: the prior plausibility of real effects and the power. If you test 1,000 ideas where only 10% truly work, with 80% power and α = 0.05:

true positives = 100 × 0.80 = 80 false positives = 900 × 0.05 = 45
false-positive risk = 45 / (80 + 45) = 36% — not 5%

The winner’s curse (Type M and Type S errors)

Conditioning on “p < 0.05” selects for lucky overestimates. In an underpowered study, the only estimates big enough to clear the bar are exaggerated — and occasionally have the wrong sign. Gelman & Carlin call these Type M (magnitude) and Type S (sign) errors. It is one reason published effects shrink on replication, and why “significant” coefficients in small segments overstate reality.

Part V · Expert toolkit

15Resampling: permutation tests and the bootstrap Expert

You don’t need a textbook formula if you can simulate the null. A permutation test asks: if group labels were meaningless (exchangeable), how often would randomly relabelled data show a difference this large? Shuffle labels thousands of times, recompute the statistic, and count. Any statistic works (median difference, AUC, KS distance), and no normality is required — validity rests on exchangeability under H₀, ideal for randomised experiments.

p = ( #{ |T*| ≥ |T_obs| } + 1 ) / ( B + 1 ) ← the “+1”: the observed labelling is itself one permutation, so p is never 0

The bootstrap resamples with replacement within each group to estimate the sampling variability of a statistic; it’s the workhorse for confidence intervals of awkward statistics (Gini, KS, ratios of percentiles). Caveats for both: they assume independent (or properly blocked) units. With clustered data (many transactions per customer) or time series, shuffle/resample whole clusters or blocks.

16The Bayesian lens: how much evidence is a p-value, really? Expert

A Bayes factor (BF₀₁) says how much the data shift the odds between H₀ and H₁: posterior odds = prior odds × BF. A p-value can’t do this, but there’s a famous calibration: for any “reasonable” prior on the alternative, the most evidence p can represent against H₀ is bounded:

BF₀₁ (minimum) ≥ −e · p · ln(p) for p < 1/e (Sellke, Bayarri & Berger, 2001)

At p = 0.05 this gives ≥ 0.41: the data can raise the odds of H₁ by at most ≈ 2.5×. Starting from 50/50, that’s a floor of ~29% on P(H₀). A surprisal view (Greenland) agrees: s = −log₂ p bits; p = 0.05 is only 4.3 bits — about as surprising as 4 heads in a row from a fair coin. Not so dramatic.

Lab 14a · Calibrating a p-value

Lindley’s paradox

Hold p fixed at exactly 0.05 and increase n. The effect that produced p = 0.05 shrinks toward zero, and a Bayesian with a diffuse alternative increasingly prefers the null. Frequentist “reject H₀!” and Bayesian “H₀ is now more probable” can both be correct at once, because they ask different questions. Practical lesson: for very large n, p just under 0.05 is weak evidence; use stricter thresholds and effect sizes.

17Equivalence tests, combining p-values, and choosing a test Expert

Proving “no meaningful difference”: TOST

To show a new model/process is equivalent to the old (within ±Δ), flip the question: make “the difference is outside [−Δ, Δ]” the null, and test two one-sided hypotheses (difference ≤ −Δ, and difference ≥ +Δ). Equivalence is claimed if both are rejected; the TOST p-value is the larger of the two. Equivalent to checking whether the 90% CI sits inside the margins. Δ must be chosen from domain knowledge before looking.

Lab 15 · TOST

Combining independent p-values (Fisher’s method)

If several independent studies/segments test the same null, X² = −2 Σ ln pᵢ ~ χ²2k under H₀. Many modest p-values can combine into strong evidence — but remember the independence requirement and the risk of including only the studies you liked.

18Practitioner playbook: modelling with big, messy, regulated data Expert

Huge n ⇒ everything is significantDrift tests (KS, χ²) on millions of rows flag trivial shifts. Use effect-size monitors (PSI bands, KS statistic magnitude, standardised differences) with thresholds tied to business impact; keep p-values as secondary context.
Dependent observations ⇒ p-values too smallMultiple accounts per customer, transactions per account, repeated months: naive SEs assume independence and overstate evidence. Use cluster-robust SEs, mixed models, or block/cluster bootstrap and permutation.
Screening hundreds of featuresSelecting variables by p-value then reporting that same model’s p-values is invalid (post-selection inference). Use a hold-out set, stability selection, FDR control, and judge features by incremental out-of-sample performance.
Rule / threshold tuningComparing many thresholds, segments or rule variants is multiple testing in disguise. Pre-register the candidate set, correct (BH for screening, Holm for confirmatory), then validate on untouched later-period data.
Rare eventsFraud and default counts can be tiny; normal approximations break. Use exact or Wilson intervals, Fisher/binomial exact tests, or profile likelihood. Report the MDE for the sample you have.
Time ordering & leakageRandom shuffles of time series data leak the future and produce absurdly small p-values. Respect temporal order; use out-of-time tests.
Documentation checklist (validators, auditors and regulators appreciate this) 1) Hypothesis and test chosen before analysis · 2) α, one/two-sided, multiplicity method · 3) Power / MDE justification for the sample · 4) Effect sizes with confidence intervals, not only p · 5) Assumption checks (independence, distribution, stability) and robustness runs · 6) Every comparison you ran, including the ones that failed · 7) Out-of-sample confirmation of any selected result · 8) Plain-language statement of the practical (business/risk) significance.
