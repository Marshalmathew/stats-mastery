Extreme Value Theory

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Extreme value theory: reasoning about the losses you have rarely or never seen.

Bank capital, stress limits and fraud thresholds are set by rare, large events, and the statistics that serve the middle of the distribution (means, standard deviations, normal quantiles) serve them badly. This page covers the theory and practice of the tail: how to recognise heavy tails and why moments fail, the two classical approaches to extremes (block maxima with the GEV, and peaks over threshold with the generalised Pareto), return levels and the cost of extrapolation, the Hill estimator, clustering of extremes, VaR against expected shortfall, diversification when tails are heavy, uncertainty about the tail index, and the aggregation of operational losses. Every lab simulates from a known tail, so the estimators can be judged against the truth.

Part I · Heavy tails and their consequences

1Light tails and heavy tails Beginner

The tail of a distribution is the probability of very large values, and how fast it falls decides what kind of data you have. Thin tails (normal, exponential) fall so quickly that extreme values are practically absent and no single observation matters in a large sample. Heavy tails follow a power law, P(X > x) ≈ x−α: the tail index α measures how heavy (small α, heavier), a straight line on a log-log plot of the survival function. Between them sit the lognormal and similar distributions, which are not power laws but are heavy enough to behave like them over realistic ranges. In banking, losses from fraud, operational events, large exposures and market crashes are in the heavy-tailed family, and most standard statistics were designed for the thin-tailed one. A practical test is the maximum-to-sum ratio: if one observation accounts for a large share of the total, the tail is heavy.

Pareto: P(X > x) = (x/xₘ)^(−α) · log P(X > x) = −α log x + const · smaller α = heavier tail

2Moments and why averages fail Intermediate

A distribution with tail index α has finite moments only of order below α. If α ≤ 1 the mean is infinite; if α ≤ 2 the variance is infinite; if α ≤ 4 the kurtosis (and so the standard error of the sample variance) is infinite. Everything built on those moments breaks down quietly: the law of large numbers needs a finite mean, the central limit theorem needs a finite variance (otherwise the sample mean converges to a stable law whose tails are themselves heavy), and correlation, regression and PCA need second moments. Sample versions still produce numbers (a sample always has a finite variance), but they do not converge, and they are driven by whichever huge values happened to be drawn. Before reporting a mean, standard deviation, correlation or confidence interval for loss data, estimate the tail index and check that the quantity exists.

E|X|ᵏ < ∞ only if k < α · α ≤ 1: no mean · α ≤ 2: no variance · α ≤ 4: no kurtosis

3Block maxima and the extreme value distribution Intermediate

The central limit theorem describes sums; extreme value theory describes maxima. The Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem says that if the maximum of a block of independent observations, suitably rescaled, converges to a non-degenerate limit, the limit is a generalised extreme value (GEV) distribution with three parameters: location μ, scale σ and shape ξ. The shape is the important one: ξ > 0 (Fréchet) for heavy tails with tail index α = 1/ξ, ξ = 0 (Gumbel) for exponential-type tails such as the normal, and ξ < 0 (Weibull) for a finite upper bound. The block-maxima method takes the maximum of each block (a year, a month), fits a GEV by maximum likelihood, and reads off return levels. It needs many blocks, it discards all but one value per block, and the convergence can be slow, so it is mostly used when only maxima are recorded.

G(x) = exp{ −[1 + ξ(x − μ)/σ]^(−1/ξ) } · ξ > 0 heavy tail (α = 1/ξ) · ξ = 0 Gumbel · ξ < 0 bounded

Part II · Fitting the tail

4Peaks over threshold and the generalised Pareto Advanced

Instead of one value per block, use every value above a high threshold u. The Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem says that for a wide class of distributions the excesses X − u over a high threshold follow a generalised Pareto distribution (GPD) with scale σ and shape ξ, the same ξ as in the GEV. This peaks-over-threshold (POT) method uses the data far more efficiently than block maxima. The choice of threshold is the main practical issue: too low and the GPD approximation is biased, too high and few points remain. Two diagnostics are standard. The mean excess plot E[X − u | X > u] against u is roughly linear above a good threshold, with slope ξ/(1 − ξ). The parameter stability plot shows ξ̂ against the threshold and one looks for a region where it is flat within its error band. For a tail probability beyond the threshold, P(X > x) ≈ (k/n)[1 + ξ(x − u)/σ]^(−1/ξ), with k exceedances out of n.

P(X − u > y | X > u) ≈ [1 + ξy/σ]^(−1/ξ) · mean excess = (σ + ξu)/(1 − ξ) · se(ξ̂) ≈ (1 + ξ̂)/√k

5Return levels and the cost of extrapolation Advanced

The question a risk manager asks is rarely “what is ξ”, it is “what loss do we exceed once in 100 years”. The return level x_T is the value exceeded on average once every T years. Under POT it is x = u + (σ/ξ)[(n p/k)^(−ξ) − 1], where p is the per-observation exceedance probability 1/(T × observations per year). The GPD extrapolates beyond the data by assuming the tail has the fitted form. The alternatives fail in typical ways: a normal fit understates long-period losses badly for heavy-tailed data, and an empirical quantile cannot exceed the sample maximum. All estimates for periods beyond the length of the data are extrapolations, with uncertainty that grows with the ratio of the return period to the record length, and the interval, not the point estimate, is the honest result. Regulators’ 99.9% and 1-in-200-year standards sit exactly in this regime.

x_T = u + (σ/ξ) [ (n·p / k)^(−ξ) − 1 ], p = 1 / (T × observations per year)

6The Hill estimator and the tail index Advanced

For Pareto-type tails the simplest estimator of the tail index is the Hill estimator, computed from the k largest observations: α̂ = [ (1/k) Σ ln(X₍ᵢ₎ / X₍ₖ₊₁₎) ]⁻¹. It is consistent and asymptotically normal, with relative standard deviation about 1/√k, but it assumes a pure power-law tail from the (k+1)-th largest value upward. Real distributions are only asymptotically Pareto, so the choice of k is a trade-off: small k means high variance, large k means bias from the non-Pareto body (a ‘slowly varying’ correction). The Hill plot of α̂ against k is the standard diagnostic and is notoriously hard to read. Reports should give a range of k, a bootstrap interval, and a comparison with the GPD-based estimate. Remember too that the Hill estimator is for positive tails and is badly affected by contamination from data errors in the largest values.

α̂ₖ = [ (1/k) Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ ln X₍ᵢ₎ − ln X₍ₖ₊₁₎ ]⁻¹ · relative sd ≈ 1/√k

7Clustering of extremes Expert

Extreme value theory for independent data counts exceedances as separate events. Real loss and return series have serial dependence: a volatile day is followed by volatile days, a fraud attack lasts hours, a credit event hits several accounts together. Extremes then occur in clusters. The extremal index θ ∈ (0, 1] summarises this: the mean cluster size is 1/θ, and θ = 1 means no clustering. The runs estimator splits exceedances into clusters (separated by more than r non-exceedances) and counts clusters per exceedance. Two practical consequences follow. First, return periods and risk measures should refer to clusters (events), not to single days. Second, applying the POT likelihood to all exceedances as if they were independent understates the uncertainty, because the effective sample is the number of clusters, so intervals are too narrow. The remedy is to decluster (fit to cluster maxima), or to use methods that account for the dependence.

Part III · Risk measures and aggregation

8Value at risk against expected shortfall Expert

Value at risk (VaR) at level q is the q-quantile of the loss; expected shortfall (ES) is the mean loss beyond it. ES sees how bad things are past the threshold and is coherent (sub-additive), which is why it replaced VaR for market risk in the Basel framework. For a Pareto tail ES = VaR × α/(α − 1), finite only for α > 1. Estimating each from data is a different statistical problem: a quantile is estimated at a stable rate, but ES averages the few largest observations, whose variability is that of the tail itself (with infinite variance when α < 2, the sample ES converges slowly and is typically biased downwards). Backtesting is also harder for ES, since it is not elicitable by itself. In practice, estimate ES from a fitted tail model (POT), not from the empirical average, report its uncertainty, and keep VaR for backtesting.

VaR_q = F⁻¹(q) · ES_q = E[X | X > VaR_q] · Pareto: ES_q = VaR_q · α/(α − 1)

9Diversification with heavy tails Expert

With thin tails, diversification works: the VaR of an equal-weight portfolio of m independent assets falls roughly as 1/√m. With heavy tails the sum is dominated by its largest term, a one-big-jump principle: P(Sm > x) ≈ m·P(X > x). Then VaR of the sum scales like m^(1/α) and VaR of the average like m^(1/α − 1). For α > 1 this still improves with m, though far more slowly than the thin-tailed 1/√m; for α < 1 it grows, so the portfolio is riskier than a single asset by the VaR measure, which therefore fails subadditivity (ES does not, but is infinite for α ≤ 1). Real loss data may sit near these boundaries: operational risk events, cyber losses and some insurance lines have estimated tail indices around 1 to 2. Capital relief for diversification across such categories should be assessed with tail-aware models and not with correlations, and dependence in the extremes (copulas, item 32) matters even more.

10Uncertainty about the tail parameter Expert

Tail parameters are estimated from few points by construction: a 99.9% VaR uses data in the top 0.1%. The sampling distribution of the shape estimate is skewed and its normal approximation is poor when k is small, so the ordinary Wald interval ξ̂ ± 1.96 se undercovers and can include implausible values. Profile likelihood intervals invert the likelihood-ratio test and follow the real shape of the likelihood; they behave better in small samples. Because return levels depend on ξ through a power, a modest interval for ξ becomes a large and asymmetric interval for a high quantile: the upper end can be many times the point estimate. The right response is to report intervals (profile or bootstrap), to run the analysis under a range of plausible tail indices (a scenario analysis), and to avoid statements such as “the 1-in-1000 loss is X” without that range. A Bayesian treatment with a sensible prior on ξ is a natural alternative, especially when external data can inform the prior.

11Operational losses: frequency and severity Expert

Operational risk capital is classically computed by the loss distribution approach: a frequency distribution (Poisson) for the number of events per year and a heavy-tailed severity distribution for each loss; the annual aggregate loss is the compound sum, and capital is its 99.9% quantile. Closed forms are rare, so it is simulated, but the heavy tail means the capital number is dominated by whether, and how large, a few huge events occur. For subexponential severities the single-loss approximation says the aggregate quantile is about the severity quantile at 1 − (1 − q)/λ (plus the mean of the other losses), which gives a quick check on simulations and a clear view of what drives the result: the tail index. Pitfalls: severity data are collected above a reporting threshold, small samples give a wide interval for α, a few external events can dominate, and the simulated 99.9% figure has high Monte Carlo error unless many years are simulated. Reconcile every capital figure with the approximation and with sensitivity to α.

VaR_q(S) ≈ F⁻¹(1 − (1 − q)/λ) + (λ − 1)·E[X] · the aggregate tail is the tail of the largest single loss
