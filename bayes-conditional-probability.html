Conditional probability & Bayes' theorem.

Probability is how you reason when you don't know everything. Conditional probability is how you reason after you learn something. Bayes' theorem is the rule for turning “how likely is this evidence if X were true?” into the question you actually care about: “how likely is X, given this evidence?” This page builds that rule from nothing and then points it at fraud alerts, PD estimation, shrinkage and the classic traps.

Part I · Conditioning

1Conditional probability is zooming in Beginner

P(A | B) reads “the probability of A given B”. When you learn that B happened, every outcome outside B is ruled out. What is left is a smaller world, and you re-measure A inside it:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) (needs P(B) > 0)

Numerator: the part of the world where both happen. Denominator: the whole of the new, smaller world. Conditioning is restriction and renormalisation: cut away what no longer applies, rescale so the remainder sums to 1.

The most common mistake in all of probabilityP(A | B) ≠ P(B | A). “Most fraudsters trigger rule R” (P(R | fraud) high) does not mean “most accounts that trigger rule R are fraudsters” (P(fraud | R) may be tiny). The two are linked by Bayes' theorem, and the link depends on how common A and B are.

Lab 1 · The probability rectangle

The whole rectangle is all accounts. Width splits A (fraud) from ¬A (legitimate); height inside each slab splits B (rule fires, bottom) from ¬B. Area = probability. Conditioning on B means keeping only the two bottom bands: P(A | B) is the red area divided by (red + yellow).

Try making fraud rarer (P(A) = 1%) with the other sliders fixed. P(B | A) stays at 80% — the rule still catches the same share of fraud — but P(A | B), the share of alerts that are real, collapses. That is Chapter 3 in miniature.

2Chain rule, total probability and independence Beginner

Rearrange the definition and you get the multiplication (chain) rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(B) · P(A | B) = P(A) · P(B | A). Stringing events along a path multiplies the conditional probabilities at each step:

P(E₁ ∩ E₂ ∩ E₃) = P(E₁) · P(E₂ | E₁) · P(E₃ | E₁ ∩ E₂)

The law of total probability adds up the routes: if E₁ and its complement ¬E₁ partition the world,

P(E₂) = P(E₂ | E₁)·P(E₁) + P(E₂ | ¬E₁)·P(¬E₁)

Two events are independent when learning one tells you nothing about the other: P(A | B) = P(A), equivalently P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B). The ratio P(A ∩ B) / (P(A)·P(B)) is the lift: 1 under independence, above 1 when they attract, below 1 when they repel.

Lab 2 · A two-step risk tree

E₁ = “new beneficiary added”, E₂ = “large transfer within 24 hours”. Read off every path probability, the total probability of E₂, and whether the events are independent.

Set P(E₂ | E₁) equal to P(E₂ | ¬E₁): the lift snaps to exactly 1.00 and the events are independent — knowing E₁ no longer moves your belief about E₂.

Part II · Bayes at work

3Bayes' theorem and the base-rate fallacy Beginner

Put P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A)P(A) = P(A | B)P(B) together and solve for the conditional you want:

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B) with P(B) = P(B | A)P(A) + P(B | ¬A)P(¬A)

Vocabulary: P(A) is the prior (before the evidence), P(B | A) is the likelihood (how expected the evidence is if A is true), P(A | B) is the posterior (after the evidence). Bayes is the exchange rate between the two directions of conditioning.

Natural frequencies make it trivial

Picture 10,000 accounts. With 0.1% fraud prevalence, 80% sensitivity and a 2% false-positive rate: 10 are fraud, 8 of them alert. Of the 9,990 legitimate accounts, 2% = 200 alert. Total alerts: 208, of which 8 are real. P(fraud | alert) = 8/208 = 3.8%. The headline number “the rule catches 80% of fraud” says nothing about the 96% of alerts that are false.

The base-rate fallacyPeople anchor on the likelihood (80%, 2%) and ignore the base rate (0.1%). When the thing you're hunting is rare, even a small false-positive rate applied to the huge legitimate population swamps the true detections. Precision is controlled as much by prevalence as by model quality.

4Odds form, likelihood ratios and stacking evidence Intermediate

Bayes is multiplication in disguise once you use odds (odds = p/(1−p)):

posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio LR = P(evidence | A) / P(evidence | ¬A)

For a signal that fires: LR⁺ = sensitivity / false-positive rate. For a signal that stays silent: LR⁻ = (1 − sensitivity) / (1 − FPR). An LR of 10 means the evidence is ten times more expected if the account is fraudulent. The prior stays separate from the evidence: the same evidence moves a 0.1% prior to 1% but moves a 20% prior to 71%. Taking logs makes updates additive: log-odds posterior = log-odds prior + Σ log LRs.

If signals are conditionally independent given the class, their LRs multiply: that is the engine of naive Bayes classifiers and of scorecard-style logistic models. The big caveat: correlated signals double-count the same information.

5Alert thresholds: precision, recall and the cost of errors Advanced

A detection model outputs a score. Fraudulent accounts tend to score higher, but the two populations overlap. Choosing the alert threshold t is a Bayesian decision: you trade false alarms (analyst time) against missed fraud (losses), weighted by how rare fraud is.

precision = P(fraud | alert) = π·TPR / (π·TPR + (1−π)·FPR) recall = TPR = P(alert | fraud)
posterior odds at score s = [π/(1−π)] × f_fraud(s) / f_legit(s)

The decision-theoretic rule: alert on an account when its posterior fraud probability exceeds cFP / (cFP + cFN), where cFP is the cost of a false alarm and cFN the cost of a miss. If a miss is 150× as costly as a false alarm, alert even when the account is only 0.7% likely to be fraud. The model here: legitimate scores ~ Normal(0, 1), fraud scores ~ Normal(d, 1), where d measures how well the model separates them.

Part III · Updating beliefs with data

6Bayesian updating: Beta–Binomial Intermediate

Treat an unknown probability θ (a PD, a response rate) as a quantity with a distribution representing your uncertainty. Start with a prior, observe data, get a posterior. With a Beta(a, b) prior and k successes in n trials,

posterior = Beta(a + k, b + n − k) posterior mean = (a + k) / (a + b + n)

Read a and b as pseudo-counts of earlier “defaults” and “non-defaults”. Two beautiful properties: order doesn't matter (updating one loan at a time gives exactly the same posterior as updating on all of them at once), and the prior's influence fades as n grows. The 95% credible interval is the central range holding 95% of the posterior probability — a direct statement about θ.

Lab 6 · Learn a default rate one loan at a time

The hidden truth θ is set below. Feed in loans (each defaults with probability θ) or click outcomes yourself. Watch the posterior sharpen and the posterior mean (bottom chart) converge — and compare how different priors behave in the early, data-poor phase.

Pick the “wrong & confident” prior and feed in 100 loans at θ = 4%: the posterior is still dragged toward 20%. A strong wrong prior needs lots of data to be overturned. Flat or weak priors let the data speak quickly but give noisy early estimates. That trade-off is the whole art of prior choice.

7Conjugate pairs: Normal–Normal and Gamma–Poisson Advanced

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior has the same form as the prior, so updating is just parameter arithmetic. Besides Beta–Binomial, two workhorses:

  • Normal–Normal (estimating a mean with known noise σ): the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean. Precision = 1/variance, and precisions add: 1/τ² = 1/τ₀² + n/σ², μ = (μ₀/τ₀² + n·x̄/σ²) · τ².
  • Gamma–Poisson (estimating an event rate λ, e.g. alerts per day): prior Gamma(a, rate b), data k events in n days → posterior Gamma(a + k, b + n). Posterior mean (a+k)/(b+n) is a weighted average of the prior mean a/b and the observed rate k/n.

8Shrinkage: borrowing strength across segments Advanced

You have 15 customer segments and want each one's default rate. A segment with 20 loans and 0 defaults has a raw rate of 0% — but surely the true rate isn't exactly zero. The Bayesian answer treats the segment rates as draws from a common Beta distribution (a prior learned from the whole book), and estimates each one by the posterior mean:

shrunk rate = (a + kᵢ) / (a + b + nᵢ) = weighted average of the book-wide mean and the segment's own rate

The weight on the segment's own data is nᵢ/(a+b+nᵢ): large segments keep their own rate, small segments are pulled toward the book average. This is partial pooling — the idea behind hierarchical models, empirical Bayes, credibility theory in insurance, and the cure for the winner's curse from the p-value course.

Part IV · Traps and the big picture

9Classic traps: Monty Hall and the prosecutor's fallacy Advanced

Monty Hall

Three doors, one car. You pick a door; the host, who knows where the car is, opens another door with a goat and offers a switch. Switching wins with probability 2/3: your first pick was right 1/3 of the time and the host's action does not change that, so the remaining 2/3 is concentrated on the one other closed door. If the host doesn't know and merely happens to reveal a goat, the same observation gives 1/2 — the host's process is part of the evidence, which is why Bayes requires you to think about how the data were generated.

Lab 9a · Simulate the game

The prosecutor's fallacy

“The chance of such a DNA match by coincidence is 1 in a million, so the chance the defendant is innocent is 1 in a million.” This confuses P(match | innocent) with P(innocent | match). If the pool of possible sources is 5 million people, you'd expect about 5 innocent matches; the defendant is one of ~6 matching people, so on the DNA alone the chance of guilt is about 1 in 6. The same error appears in fraud analytics whenever “the pattern is rare among legitimate accounts” is read as “accounts with the pattern are probably fraudulent”.
