Imbalanced & Rare Events

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Imbalanced data and rare events: fraud, AML, default and churn done properly.

When 1 case in 200 is the event you care about, ordinary habits fail: accuracy rewards doing nothing, ROC curves hide an avalanche of false alerts, coefficient estimates are noisy and biased, resampling tricks quietly break the probabilities, and a test set with 12 events cannot tell a good model from a lucky one. This page works through the whole problem: the right metrics and base-rate arithmetic, operating points under alert budgets, how many events you really need, what undersampling, oversampling, SMOTE and class weights do (and do not do), the exact prior correction, rare-event bias and Firth’s method, cost-sensitive learning, evaluating with few events, and estimating rates when almost nothing has happened. Every lab simulates from a known truth.

Part I · Metrics and base rates

1The accuracy paradox Beginner

If 0.5% of transactions are fraudulent, a “model” that never flags anything is 99.5% accurate and useless. With a confusion matrix of TP, FP, FN, TN the standard rates are: recall (sensitivity, TPR) = TP/(TP + FN); specificity (TNR) = TN/(TN + FP); precision (PPV) = TP/(TP + FP); F1 = the harmonic mean of precision and recall; balanced accuracy = (recall + specificity)/2; and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), a correlation between predicted and actual labels. Recall and specificity are properties of the classifier alone, but precision, accuracy and F1 also depend on the prevalence, which is why the same model looks different in every population.

2ROC curves versus precision–recall curves Intermediate

The ROC curve plots TPR against FPR, and its AUC does not depend on prevalence: it is the probability that a random event outscores a random non-event. The precision–recall (PR) curve plots precision against recall; its baseline is the prevalence (a random model has precision π everywhere), and it collapses as the event becomes rarer, because the number of false positives scales with the huge negative class. Average precision (AP) summarises it. A point at FPR = 1% looks excellent on an ROC curve, but with 1,000,000 non-events it means 10,000 false alerts. For rare events, read the PR curve, partial AUC at low FPR, or recall at a fixed alert volume rather than the full ROC-AUC.

3Base rates, precision and alert fatigue Advanced

By Bayes’ theorem, a flag has precision

PPV = sens · π / [ sens · π + (1 − spec)(1 − π) ]

With sensitivity 90%, specificity 95% and prevalence 0.5% the PPV is only 8%: 11 of 12 alerts are false. The binding constraint in AML, fraud and collections is usually operational capacity: investigators can review m cases a day. Then the question is how many true events are in the top m (the gain curve) and what each hit costs in reviewed cases (the inverse of precision). Raising the quality of the score helps most at small m, where you can afford to be selective; no threshold fixes a population with a tiny base rate and a weak model.

4Choosing the operating point Advanced

Three common ways to pick a threshold: (1) maximise F1; for calibrated probabilities the F1-optimal threshold equals half the maximal F1 value, so it is rarely 0.5; (2) minimise expected cost, flag when the calibrated probability exceeds c_FP/(c_FP + c_FN), where a missed fraud or default costs far more than a false alert; (3) fix the capacity and flag the top m cases. They give different answers, and the right one is set by the business problem, not by a default of 0.5. The threshold should be chosen on data not used to fit the model, and its uncertainty is large when events are few.

Part II · Fitting with few events

5Events, not rows Advanced

For a binary outcome the information about the effects comes from the smaller class. For a single standardised predictor the variance of the log-odds coefficient is about 1/[n π(1 − π)], so 10 million rows with 20 events carry about as much information as 20 events with a few thousand controls. The classic guide is at least 10 events per candidate parameter (EPV); modern criteria (Riley et al.) ask for enough events to keep shrinkage above 0.9, optimism in R² small and the overall risk estimated to within a given precision, and often need more than 10 EPV. With too few events the fit overfits: forecasts are over-confident (calibration slope below 1), coefficients are too large, and out-of-sample performance is poor.

6Resampling and class weights: what they do and do not do Expert

Random undersampling drops non-events; random oversampling duplicates events; SMOTE invents synthetic events by interpolating between neighbouring ones; class weights give each event a larger weight in the loss. They are popular, but they add no real information about events (duplicates and interpolations are functions of the existing ones) and they change the effective prevalence the model sees, so the fitted probabilities no longer match reality. For well-specified models such as logistic regression, studies generally find no consistent gain in discrimination and a clear loss of calibration; for some flexible learners they may help the ranking in the low-score region. Two rules: resample only within training folds (resampling before splitting puts copies of validation events in the training data and inflates every metric) and, if you need probabilities, undo the prior shift (chapter 7).

7Prior correction and the cost of discarding data Expert

If non-events are kept with probability r and events are all kept, the logistic model fitted to the sample has the same slopes but an intercept shifted by −ln r (King and Zeng). The correction is exact for this sampling design: corrected intercept = fitted intercept + ln r (equivalently, add ln of the ratio of population odds to sample odds). The price is variance: the discarded non-events carried information. For small prevalence π, the variance of the coefficients is inflated by roughly (1 + π/r), and more when the predictors are strong, so keeping r far above the prevalence costs little, while pushing the sample to 1:1 at a rare prevalence of 1% (r ≈ 1%) doubles the variance. Undersampling is therefore best seen as a computational shortcut for huge data, not a way to improve a model, and when used the intercept must be corrected.

8Rare-event bias, separation and Firth’s method Expert

Maximum likelihood for logistic regression is only asymptotically unbiased; with few events the coefficients are biased away from zero and, as King and Zeng showed, the predicted probability of a rare event tends to be too small. Worse, with few events and several predictors a combination can separate events from non-events perfectly (complete or quasi-complete separation): the likelihood then increases forever and the maximum likelihood estimate is infinite, producing absurd coefficients and standard errors. Firth’s penalised likelihood (a Jeffreys-prior penalty) removes the leading bias term, always gives finite estimates, and handles separation; profile-likelihood intervals are then preferred to Wald intervals. One caveat: the Firth penalty pulls predicted probabilities toward ½, so for very rare events the forecasts can be too high; variants such as FLIC and FLAC, or a recalibrated intercept, fix this. Ridge or Bayesian priors serve a similar purpose.

9Cost-sensitive learning: weights versus thresholds Expert

If a miss costs w times a false alert, the best rule on calibrated probabilities is to flag when p ≥ 1/(1 + w). Training a model with weight w on the events multiplies the odds by w, so the weighted model’s probability is ≥ ½ exactly when the original p ≥ 1/(1 + w): weighting and moving the threshold are the same decision rule for a correct model. The difference is statistical: the weighted fit has higher variance (it up-weights a few events) and loses calibration, whereas fitting plainly and then choosing the threshold keeps usable probabilities. Loss modifications such as focal loss follow the same logic; they matter for flexible learners, not for the decision-theoretic principle.

Part III · Evaluating and estimating

10Evaluating with few events Expert

Every metric inherits the noise of the small class. The standard error of the AUC is driven by the number of events and non-events (Hanley and McNeil); with 20 events the 95% interval for an AUC of 0.85 is roughly ±0.1, and the PR-AUC and recall at a fixed alert volume are noisier still. Practical rules: use stratified splits and folds so every fold has events (random 5-fold CV with 25 events can leave a fold with 1 or 2); use repeated CV or bootstrap and report intervals; pool out-of-fold predictions to draw one PR curve; keep an out-of-time test set, and never choose the threshold on the test set. A model improvement smaller than the interval is not evidence.

11Estimating a rare rate: when almost nothing has happened Expert

A low-default portfolio with 0 defaults in 500 obligors does not have a default rate of 0. Estimating a rare proportion needs intervals that behave. The Wald interval p̂ ± 1.96 √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n) collapses to a point at 0 events and has poor coverage. The Clopper–Pearson (exact) interval guarantees at least 95% coverage (conservative); Wilson and Jeffreys (a Beta(½,½) prior) are close to nominal on average. At zero events the 95% upper bound is about 3/n (the “rule of three”); in credit risk the Pluto–Tasche approach for low-default portfolios builds a prudent upper estimate in the same spirit (and the Basel framework asks for conservative margins). Rating-grade back-testing with a handful of defaults has little power, so combine grades, use external information, and report upper bounds, not point estimates alone.
