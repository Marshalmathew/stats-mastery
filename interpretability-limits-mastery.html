Part I · Post-Hoc Attributions vs True Mechanisms

1The Interpretability Illusion: Explaining $f(x)$ vs Explaining Reality Beginner

A machine learning explanation is almost universally an explanation of the prediction function $f(x)$, not the data-generating physical process $P(Y \mid X)$. When practitioners say "Feature $X_1$ is the most important driver of patient mortality," they usually mean that the model's output changes substantially when $X_1$ varies. If the model was trained on observational data with unobserved confounding, selection bias, or reverse causality, $f(x)$ will faithfully exploit these spurious statistical associations.

Consider an automated triage model predicting pneumonia mortality. In raw hospital records, asthmatic patients diagnosed with pneumonia have a lower observed mortality rate than non-asthmatics. Why? Because asthmatics are immediately escalated to intensive care units and receive aggressive early steroid therapy. A machine learning model learns: Asthma = True → Risk = -0.42. A post-hoc explainer (like SHAP or LIME) will correctly report that asthma has a large negative attribution. But advising a doctor to withhold ICU admission because "the explanation proves asthma protects the patient" is fatal.

Lab 1 · The Confounder Trap: Spurious Attributions

In this simulation, true treatment $T$ has a harmful effect on outcome $Y$ ($\beta_{\text{true}} = +1.5$), but severe unobserved illness $U$ causes doctors to treat ($U \to T$) and independently increases survival effort ($U \to -Y$). Drag the confounding strength to observe how the model's feature attribution flips signs and contradicts reality.

Key Takeaway: Post-hoc explainers have zero awareness of causal structures. They explain why the algorithm made a decision given its trained parameters, not what will happen if you intervene on the feature in the physical world.

2Permutation Feature Importance & Correlation Breakage Intermediate

Permutation Feature Importance (PFI), introduced by Leo Breiman for Random Forests, measures the increase in prediction error after randomly shuffling the values of feature $X_j$ across the validation set: $$\text{PFI}(j) = L(y, f(X^{\pi_j})) - L(y, f(X))$$ While conceptually intuitive and model-agnostic, PFI relies on a lethal hidden assumption: that features are statistically independent.

When features $X_1$ and $X_2$ are strongly correlated (e.g. $X_1 = \text{Annual Income}$ and $X_2 = \text{Monthly Savings}$), shuffling $X_1$ while holding $X_2$ fixed creates synthetic data points that lie far off the data manifold—such as an annual income of $15,000 paired with monthly savings of $20,000. Evaluating $f(x)$ on these impossible feature combinations forces the model to extrapolate into regions where it was never trained, producing wild prediction artifacts. Furthermore, when two collinear features jointly carry signal, each individual feature can be shuffled with minimal loss of accuracy because the other feature acts as a redundant backup, causing both to appear deceptively unimportant.

Lab 2 · Permutation Importance under Collinearity: The Masking Dilemma

Simulate two correlated predictors $X_1$ and $X_2$ with correlation $\rho$. Observe how as $\rho \to 1$, the permutation importance of each individual feature plummets even though their joint predictive power remains high.

Key Takeaway: PFI systematically underestimates the importance of correlated feature groups through redundant substitution and simultaneously pollutes validation loss by extrapolating into impossible off-manifold spaces.

3Partial Dependence vs Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) Intermediate

Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) attempt to visualize the marginal effect of feature $X_j$ on $f(X)$ by averaging the model's predictions over the empirical marginal distribution of all other features $X_{\setminus j}$: $$\hat{f}_{PDP}(x_j) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_j, x_{i,\setminus j})$$ Like PFI, PDP computes predictions on rectangular grid combinations $(x_j, x_{i,\setminus j})$. When features are correlated, PDP evaluates points in empty regions where $P(X_j, X_{\setminus j}) \approx 0$, creating severe visual distortions.

Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) plots, introduced by Apley & Zhu (2020), solve this fundamental flaw. Instead of replacing $X_j$ globally, ALE calculates local differences in small intervals around $x_j$, conditioning on the actual local conditional distribution $P(X_{\setminus j} \mid X_j)$, and then integrates (accumulates) those local derivatives: $$\hat{f}_{ALE}(x_j) = \int_{z_0}^{x_j} \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial f(X_j, X_{\setminus j})}{\partial X_j} \,\middle|\, X_j = z\right] dz - \text{constant}$$ ALE never evaluates the model in empty feature space, isolates main effects from interactions, and remains statistically rigorous under severe correlation.

Lab 3 · PDP Extrapolation vs ALE Real-Manifold Tracking

Toggle correlation $\rho$ between $X_1$ and $X_2$ where true model $f(x) = x_1 + 2 x_2^2$. Watch how PDP hallucinates non-existent curvature into empty space while ALE faithfully tracks the local gradient.

Key Takeaway: Always prefer ALE over PDP for observational datasets with correlated features. PDP averages across impossible feature combinations; ALE integrates genuine local conditional derivatives.

Part II · Shapley Values & Local Explanations

4Shapley Values & Cooperative Game Axioms Intermediate

Originating in cooperative game theory (Lloyd Shapley, 1953), Shapley values answer the question: if $p$ players collaborate to achieve a total payout $v(S)$, how should the payout be fairly distributed? In machine learning, the players are the features, the game is the model prediction $f(x)$, and the payout is the deviation from the baseline expectation $\mathbb{E}[f(X)]$.

The Shapley value $\phi_j$ is the unique attribution method that simultaneously satisfies four essential axioms:

  1. Efficiency: $\sum_{j=1}^p \phi_j = f(x) - \mathbb{E}[f(X)]$ (all attribution sums to the prediction difference).
  2. Symmetry: If $v(S \cup \{j\}) = v(S \cup \{k\})$ for all subsets $S$, then $\phi_j = \phi_k$.
  3. Dummy (Null Player): If feature $j$ contributes nothing to any subset ($v(S \cup \{j\}) = v(S)$), then $\phi_j = 0$.
  4. Additivity: For combined games $u + v$, $\phi_j(u + v) = \phi_j(u) + \phi_j(v)$.
The exact formula weights the marginal contribution over all $2^p$ possible coalitions: $$\phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq \{1,\dots,p\}\setminus\{j\}} \frac{|S|!(p - |S| - 1)!}{p!} \Big( v(S \cup \{j\}) - v(S) \Big)$$

Lab 4 · Interactive 3-Feature Shapley Decomposition

Explore an interactive 3-player cooperative credit model. Adjust the synergy parameter $\gamma$ to see how non-linear feature interactions are split equally across all contributing coalitions.

Key Takeaway: Shapley values provide the only mathematically unique attribution that satisfies the 4 fairness axioms. However, calculating exact values requires evaluating all $2^p$ subsets, necessitating approximations (TreeSHAP, KernelSHAP) in practice.

5Marginal vs Conditional SHAP: The Off-Manifold Dilemma Advanced

To evaluate a feature subset $S$, what is the value function $v(S)$? How do we "remove" features $\bar{S}$ from a trained model that expects a complete vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^p$? There are two fundamentally conflicting philosophies:

  • Interventional / Marginal SHAP: Replaces absent features with random samples from their marginal distribution: $v_{\text{int}}(S) = \int f(x_S, x_{\bar{S}}) dP(X_{\bar{S}})$. This enforces true to the model. A feature with zero weight in $f(x)$ receives exactly $\phi_j = 0$ (Dummy axiom holds). But like PFI and PDP, it synthesizes off-manifold points by pairing $x_S$ with unrealistic $x_{\bar{S}}$.
  • Observational / Conditional SHAP: Conditions on the present features: $v_{\text{obs}}(S) = \mathbb{E}[f(X) \mid X_S = x_S]$. This enforces true to the data. It never evaluates off-manifold points. But it violates the Dummy axiom: if $X_1$ has true model weight 0, but is correlated with $X_2$, $X_1$ will receive non-zero attribution because observing $X_1$ updates our expectation of $X_2$!

Lab 5 · Marginal vs Conditional SHAP under Correlation

Set true model $f(x) = 2.0 X_1 + 0.0 X_2$. $X_2$ has zero weight in the model! Adjust correlation $\rho(X_1, X_2)$ and observe how Conditional SHAP falsely attributes credit to the dummy variable $X_2$ while Marginal SHAP keeps $X_2 = 0$ but hallucinates off-manifold.

Key Takeaway: There is no free lunch in feature attribution: you must choose between evaluating the model on impossible data points (Marginal SHAP) or attributing importance to features the model never actually used (Conditional SHAP).

6LIME: Local Surrogates & Sampling Radius Instabilities Advanced

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations, Ribeiro et al. 2016) builds a local linear surrogate model $g(z) = w^T z$ to approximate complex black-box $f(x)$ around an instance $x$: $$\xi(x) = \arg\min_{g \in G} \sum_{z \in Z} \pi_x(z) \big(f(z) - g(z)\big)^2 + \Omega(g)$$ where $\pi_x(z) = \exp\left(-\frac{D(x, z)^2}{\sigma^2}\right)$ is an exponential proximity kernel with kernel bandwidth $\sigma$.

LIME suffers from two acute statistical vulnerabilities:

  1. Sampling Variance: Perturbations $z$ are drawn randomly from Gaussian noise. Different random seeds on the same instance can produce completely conflicting explanation weights.
  2. Hyperparameter Sensitivity ($\sigma$): If $\sigma$ is too small, the sample contains virtually no variance and the fit is ill-conditioned. If $\sigma$ is too large, the surrogate spans multiple non-linear curvature regions, causing local feature weights to flip signs.

Lab 6 · LIME Kernel Bandwidth & Surrogate Instability

Drag the kernel width $\sigma$ on a non-linear decision boundary. Observe how changing the arbitrary neighborhood radius flips the local surrogate slope from positive to negative.

Key Takeaway: LIME explanations are highly sensitive to user-chosen sampling radii and random seeds. A model explanation that changes when you re-run the script with seed 43 instead of seed 42 cannot be used for high-stakes regulatory decisions.

7The Rashomon Effect & Model Multiplicity Advanced

Named after Akira Kurosawa’s film where witnesses describe the same event in contradictory ways, Leo Breiman (2001) coined the Rashomon Effect for machine learning: in almost any empirical dataset, there exists a vast Rashomon set $\mathcal{R}(\epsilon)$ of models that achieve virtually identical predictive loss: $$\mathcal{R}(\epsilon) = \left\{ f \in \mathcal{F} \;\middle|\; L(f) \le L(f^*) + \epsilon \right\}$$ While these models predict equally well (e.g. all within 0.1% test AUC), their internal mechanisms, coefficient signs, and top-ranked feature importances can be diametrically opposed.

When an organization trains a single model (say, gradient boosted trees with seed 101) and presents its SHAP plot as "the ground truth explanation of our data," they are ignoring thousands of equally accurate models that would assign zero importance to their top feature and high importance to an omitted variable. This phenomenon is known as Predictive Multiplicity (Marx et al., 2020).

Lab 7 · The Rashomon Multiplicity Explorer

Inspect 5 distinct models from the Rashomon set of a collinear dataset ($\Delta \text{Loss} < 0.2\%$). Compare how their top feature rankings flip and contradict each other despite identical test performance.

Key Takeaway: Never confuse model explanation with domain truth. If two models achieve the same empirical risk with contradictory feature attributions, the explanation is a property of the model's arbitrary inductive bias, not nature.

Part III · Causal Limits & Adversarial Vulnerabilities

8Feature Attribution Is Not Causal Effect Advanced

Perhaps the most dangerous misuse of explainable AI is treating feature attribution $\phi_j$ as an estimate of causal intervention $\frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X)]}{\partial X_j}$. In business and policy settings, leaders routinely ask: "SHAP says Marketing Spend has attribution +0.8 and Customer Age has +0.1; therefore, increasing marketing spend by \$10k will generate 8x more return than targeting older customers."

This inference is completely unjustified. Feature attributions measure associative model dependencies: $$\text{Attribution} \approx \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_j} \ne \frac{d \mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X_j = x_j)]}{d x_j}$$ If $X_j$ is a mediator, a collider, or an effect of an unobserved common cause (e.g., website visits before buying), the attribution will be enormous while the true interventional effect of artificially forcing website visits is exactly zero. Valid causal effects require identification via DAGs, the backdoor criterion, and exogenous variation—things statistical explainers are mathematically incapable of providing.

Lab 8 · Attribution vs Causal DAG Interventions

Compare the SHAP attribution of feature $X_1$ with its true causal effect $\frac{d\mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X_1)]}{dX_1}$ across different causal structures (Confounder, Mediator, Collider).

Key Takeaway: Feature attribution tells you how the model processes information; causal inference tells you how the world responds to action. Never prescribe real-world interventions based solely on SHAP or LIME attributions.

9Adversarial Fooling of Post-Hoc Explainers Expert

Can a model lie about its own internal reasoning? In landmark work, Slack et al. (2020) demonstrated that post-hoc explainers like LIME and SHAP can be systematically fooled using adversarial scaffolding.

Explainers draw perturbation samples $z$ from synthetic distributions (e.g. isotropic Gaussians or marginal permutations). Because real data lies on a lower-dimensional manifold, off-manifold synthetic perturbations are easily distinguishable from real on-manifold instances by a simple binary classifier: $$f_{\text{adv}}(x) = \begin{cases} f_{\text{biased}}(x) & \text{if } x \text{ is on-manifold (Real Customer: discriminate on Protected Attribute)} \\ f_{\text{innocuous}}(x) & \text{if } x \text{ is off-manifold (Explainer Perturbation: predict using benign features)} \end{cases}$$ When queried by an auditor or regulator using LIME or SHAP, the model detects the perturbation distribution and switches to a harmless explanation! The auditor concludes the model is 100% fair and blind to protected attributes, while in production it actively discriminates.

Lab 9 · The Scaffolding Exploit Simulator

Test an adversarial model that discriminates on protected feature $Z$ on real data, but detects perturbation points generated by SHAP/LIME and feigns neutrality.

Key Takeaway: Post-hoc explainers are not security or audit guarantees. Because they sample off-manifold, any adversary can construct a dual-personality model that hides biased decisions from explainers.

10High Dimensions, Non-Linearities & Feature Dilution Expert

In high dimensions ($p \gg 100$), explainability encounters the geometric curse of dimensionality:

  • Feature Dilution: If a credit bureau includes 50 correlated credit inquiries instead of 1 aggregate score, tree models split across all 50 features arbitrarily. The individual attribution of each inquiry drops by a factor of 50, disguising an overwhelming risk factor as negligible background noise.
  • Distance Dilution in LIME: In high dimensions, all pairwise Euclidean distances converge to the same value ($D_{\max} / D_{\min} \to 1$). Exponential kernels $\pi_x(z)$ lose discriminative power, assigning nearly equal weights to all perturbed points.
  • High-Order Interactions: When models contain complex multi-way interactions (e.g. $X_1 \cdot X_2 \cdot X_3 \cdot X_4$), splitting the interaction evenly across main-effect attributions hides the non-linear trigger conditions.

Lab 10 · Collinear Dilution & Importance Dispersal

Add redundant collinear copies of a primary risk signal. Watch how individual feature importances vanish below the significance floor while the true total group importance remains invariant.

Key Takeaway: Always group correlated features before computing attributions. Group-SHAP and hierarchical clustering prevent signal dilution from blinding stakeholders to critical risk factors.

11Regulator-Grade Explanations & Statistical Standards Expert

Regulatory frameworks such as the **EU AI Act (Article 13)**, **US Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA - Adverse Action Notices)**, and **Federal Reserve SR 11-7 (Model Risk Management)** require explanations that are:

  1. Faithful: Reflective of actual model mechanics rather than post-hoc rationalization.
  2. Actionable (Recourse): Guiding individuals on what realistic, feasible changes will alter the decision.
  3. Robust: Stable under minor input perturbations and sample re-runs.

Counterfactual Explanations & Actionable Recourse: A counterfactual explanation finds the minimum cost change $\delta$ such that $f(x + \delta) = y^*$: $$\delta^* = \arg\min_{\delta} \text{dist}(x, x+\delta) + \lambda \ell(f(x+\delta), y^*) + \text{Plausibility}(x+\delta)$$ Crucially, mathematical nearest-counterfactuals often prescribe absurd actions (e.g., "reduce your age by 5 years" or "decrease debt while keeping income at zero"). Actionable recourse requires causal constraints and immutable feature protections.

Lab 11 · Nearest Counterfactual vs Feasible Causal Recourse

Compare unconstrained mathematical counterfactuals (which extrapolate off the data manifold) with feasible causal recourse that respects feature correlations and immutable constraints.

Key Takeaway: Adverse action notices and counterfactual explanations must restrict recourse recommendations to realistic paths along the data manifold that respect causal constraints.
