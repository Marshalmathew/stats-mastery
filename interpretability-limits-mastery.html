Interpretability and Its Limits

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Interpretability and its statistical limits: what feature importance, partial dependence, Shapley values and counterfactuals can and cannot tell you.

A model that predicts well is not yet a model that can be defended. Regulators, auditors and the CEO ask which features drive a score, why this customer was declined and what they could do about it. The standard tools (permutation importance, partial dependence, Shapley values, local surrogates, counterfactual explanations, reason codes) are estimates computed from a fitted model on a finite sample, and they inherit every weakness of estimation: sampling error, instability under correlated features, dependence on arbitrary choices (baseline, perturbation scheme) and, above all, a gap between describing the model and describing the world. This page treats each tool as a statistic: what it estimates, how noisy it is, what biases it, and what must be reported alongside it.

Part I · Importance and effects

1Importance is a property of a fit, not of the world Beginner

The simplest importance measures read the model directly: coefficients, standardised coefficients, split counts in a tree. They share a problem. When features are correlated, a model can shift weight between them at almost no cost to its fit, so the coefficient of each one is unstable from sample to sample, while their combined effect is stable. A ranking read off one fit is then largely arbitrary. The variance of a coefficient is inflated by 1/(1 − ρ²) for two features with correlation ρ (more generally by the variance inflation factor), and what is stable is the prediction, not its allocation among features. Report importance with a measure of its variability over refits, and group highly correlated features.

Var(b̂A) ≈ σ² / (n (1 − ρ²)) · Var(b̂A + b̂B) stays small

2Permutation, conditional and drop-column importance Beginner

Permutation importance shuffles one feature in held-out data and records how much the error rises: it measures how much the fitted model relies on the feature. With correlated features the shuffle creates combinations that never occur in the data, which penalises the model for inputs it was never meant to handle and inflates the importance of any feature that shares information with another. Conditional permutation shuffles only among similar cases and respects the correlation. Drop-column importance refits the model without the feature and measures the loss: it measures the information the feature adds, but hides the importance of a feature that has a near-copy. The methods answer different questions and are not interchangeable.

Importancej = Err(permute xj) − Err(original) · drop-column: Err(model without xj) − Err(full model)

3Partial dependence, ICE and interactions Intermediate

The partial dependence of a feature averages the model’s prediction over the other features while the chosen feature is set to each value in turn; individual conditional expectation (ICE) curves show the same for each case. The average can be flat or monotone while individual curves are not: an interaction (the effect of A depends on B) disappears from the average, and a policy conclusion drawn from the mean is wrong for a large share of customers. Partial dependence also evaluates the model at combinations of features that may not occur (the problem of chapter 2); accumulated local effects are the usual remedy. Plot ICE curves, report the spread of the individual effects, and say which population the average is over.

PD(a) = (1/n) Σi f(a, xi,−A) · ICEi(a) = f(a, xi,−A)

Part II · Attribution and local explanations

4Shapley values: baselines, variants and correlated features Intermediate

The Shapley value of feature j for one case is its marginal contribution to the prediction averaged over all orders of adding features; the credits add up to the difference between the prediction and a baseline prediction. It is the unique split satisfying efficiency, symmetry, dummy and additivity. The method needs a value function for “feature absent”. The interventional version fills absent features from a background sample, so that a feature the model ignores gets zero. The conditional version fills them with what is expected given the present ones, so that a correlated stand-in receives credit. The baseline (which background cases, what average) changes every number. A Shapley value is neither the effect of changing the feature nor a measure of its causal role.

φj = ΣS⊆N∖{j} [ |S|!(n − |S| − 1)!/n! ] · [ v(S ∪ {j}) − v(S) ]

5The sampling error of an explanation Advanced

Importance and Shapley values are averages over a finite sample of cases (and, for sampling-based algorithms, over random permutations and background draws), so they carry sampling error like any other estimate. When two features differ in importance by less than the noise, their order is not resolved, though a report ranks them on page one. The sampling error is of the same order as the differences that are read into the tables, unless the test sample is large. Bootstrap the test cases (and, for stability, the training sample) to give an interval for each importance, report the rank intervals and do not rank features whose intervals overlap. The same applies to approximations: the number of Monte Carlo permutations and background samples used by an approximate Shapley algorithm is a source of error that should be measured.

P(rank correct) = P(ÎA > ÎB) with sd(Î) ≈ s / √n

6Local surrogate explanations (LIME) Advanced

A local surrogate explains one prediction by fitting a simple model (a sparse linear model) to the black box’s outputs on perturbed points around the case, weighted by their closeness. The reported coefficients are those of the surrogate, not of the black box. Their quality depends on choices the user rarely sees: how the perturbations are generated (which also decides whether they are realistic), the kernel width, the number of samples and the surrogate’s fidelity in the neighbourhood. A wide kernel biases the explanation towards a global average, a narrow one makes it noisy, and different random seeds give different explanations. Check fidelity, repeat the explanation and report its variability; when the model is differentiable, compare with its gradient.

Minimise Σi πx(zi) (f(zi) − g(zi))² + penalty(g)

Part III · What explanations cannot say

7The Rashomon effect and the choice of the model Advanced

With correlated features and finite data many different models fit about equally well: the Rashomon set of near-optimal models. Their explanations can differ completely (one uses feature A, another leaves it out), and the single model chosen by a validation score is an arbitrary member whose explanation should not be read as a finding about the data. The size of the set grows with the tolerance for accuracy and with the correlation, and shrinks with sample size. Responsible practice is to report what is common to the whole near-optimal set (features that all use are robust) and what is not, and to say that the choice among near-ties was made on other grounds (stability, simplicity, cost, monotonicity constraints).

Rashomon set = { f : Err(f) ≤ Err(f*) (1 + ε) }

8Explaining the model is not explaining the world Expert

Every tool so far describes how the model turns inputs into outputs. Whether changing an input changes the real outcome is a causal question. A feature that stands in for a hidden cause gets credit in every explanation although intervening on it does nothing, and a feature with a real effect can get little credit if it is correlated with others. Using a Shapley plot or a partial-dependence curve to advise a customer (“reduce your balance and your risk falls”) or to design a policy requires identification of the causal effect by the methods of items 22 to 25. The explanation of a predictive model is the right object for auditing the model, and the wrong object for deciding what to do to the world.

Model slope = β + γ·Cov(U, X)/Var(X) (β: causal effect, U: hidden cause)

9Counterfactual explanations and recourse Expert

A counterfactual explanation gives the smallest change in the features that would flip the decision. It is intuitive and requires no model internals, but three issues limit it. It is computed against an estimated boundary, which moves when the model is refitted or the population changes, so that advice valid today may not be valid when the customer acts on it. The change must be feasible and actionable (income cannot be changed on request, age cannot be reduced), and must not change the outcome through other features. And many different counterfactuals exist, so the one shown reflects a choice of distance. Add a margin, report the cost and the share that would remain valid under refits, and treat recourse as a commitment that can be tested.

x* = argmin d(x, x′) subject to f(x′) ≥ threshold + margin

10Explanations of overfitted models, and null references Expert

Explanations are computed from the fitted model, so an overfitted model yields explanations of its overfit. Importance measured on the training data credits noise features, because the flexible model has used them to memorise noise, whereas on held-out data the same features receive about zero. Two safeguards follow: compute explanations on data not used for fitting, and calibrate against a null reference: the importance the method assigns to features known to be irrelevant, such as shuffled copies of the real ones or random columns added on purpose, or to a model trained on shuffled outcomes. An importance that does not exceed the null distribution has not been shown to be different from noise, which is the same logic as a hypothesis test, applied to an explanation.

Null importance distribution: refit with permuted outcome, record Îj(0); compare Îj with it

11Reason codes, stability and governance Expert

Reason codes on adverse-action notices and in model-risk reports are the most consequential use of explanations. Each code is a statistic of the fitted model and the case, and its stability under refits and resampling can be measured: how often two models trained on different samples of the same population give the same top reason for the same customer. With correlated features it can be low, even with a model that predicts identically. A defensible practice documents the method (variant, baseline), reports stability, groups correlated features into reasons that the customer can understand, tests that the stated reason is actionable, and keeps the explanation under the same monitoring and validation as the score itself (SR 11-7, item 15 and item 33).

Stability = P(top reason from model 1 = top reason from model 2 | same case)
