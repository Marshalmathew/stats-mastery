Graph and Network Statistics

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Graph and network statistics: reading structure in who is linked to whom, and knowing how far to trust it.

Money moves between accounts, devices are shared, customers refer each other: much of banking risk, from mule accounts and fraud rings to contagion of defaults and churn, lives in the links rather than in the individual records. Network data break the assumptions of ordinary statistics, because the units are not independent and the observed network is usually only a biased piece of the real one. This page covers degree structure and heavy-tailed hubs, small worlds, centrality, communities and their detectability limits, null models for judging whether a pattern is surprising, sampling and measurement error, neighbour-based and propagation features for fraud detection, detecting dense rings, and the confusion of homophily with influence. Every lab simulates a network with known structure so methods can be judged against the truth.

Part I · Describing networks

1Graphs, degrees and hubs Beginner

A graph is a set of nodes (accounts, customers, devices) and links (payments, referrals, shared attributes); links can be directed (a payment has a sender and a receiver) or weighted (by amount or count). The simplest description is the degree of a node, the number of links it has, and the degree distribution. In the classical random graph every pair is linked independently, so degrees cluster around the mean (a Poisson distribution) and no node is far above the others. Real networks are different: their degrees have a heavy, roughly power-law tail, as generated by preferential attachment (new nodes link to nodes that already have many links). The mean degree then describes almost no node, a small number of hubs carry a large share of all links, and anything that depends on the hubs (the reach of a contagion, the effect of removing a node, how the network can be sampled) behaves very differently from the random graph.

mean degree = 2m/n · random graph: D ~ Poisson · preferential attachment: P(D ≥ d) ∝ d^(−α+1) · hubs hold a disproportionate share of links

2Paths, clustering and small worlds Intermediate

Two local and global summaries describe how a network is organised. The clustering coefficient is the probability that two neighbours of a node are themselves linked (a measure of triangles: “friends of friends are friends”). The average path length is the mean number of steps along the shortest route between two nodes. In a regular lattice clustering is high and paths are long; in a random graph clustering is low and paths are short (about ln n / ln k). Real networks combine high clustering with short paths: the small-world property, produced by a few long-range links in an otherwise local structure. Short paths are therefore the rule: “within 3 steps of a flagged account” covers a large share of a connected network, and means little without a reference for how many accounts one would expect within 3 steps anyway.

C = 3 × triangles / connected triples · L = average shortest path · random graph: C ≈ k/n, L ≈ ln n/ln k · small world: C ≫ C_random, L ≈ L_random

3Centrality: who matters in a network Intermediate

Centrality measures rank nodes by position. Degree counts links; closeness is the inverse of the average distance to other nodes; betweenness is the share of shortest paths between other pairs that pass through the node (brokerage and control of flows); eigenvector centrality gives credit for having well-connected neighbours; PageRank adds damping and a random jump, so that credit from a node is shared among its links. The measures answer different questions and can disagree a lot: in a network made of communities, the nodes that bridge them have ordinary degree and very high betweenness. The measures are descriptive statistics of a single observed network, with uncertainty that comes from how the network was observed (chapters 6 and 11); a node’s rank deep in the list is mostly noise.

betweenness(v) = Σ σₛₜ(v)/σₛₜ · closeness(v) = (n − 1)/Σ d(v, u) · PageRank: x = α·Pᵀx + (1 − α)/n

Part II · Structure and inference

4Communities, modularity and detectability limits Advanced

A community is a group of nodes more densely linked among themselves than with the rest. Modularity, Q = Σc [ec/m − (dc/2m)²], scores a partition by comparing its internal links with those expected under random linking with the same degrees; algorithms (Louvain, Leiden, spectral methods) look for partitions with high Q. Two facts constrain interpretation. There is a detectability threshold: in the stochastic block model with two groups of average degrees a (within) and b (between), communities can be recovered above chance only if (a − b)² > 2(a + b); below it they exist but no algorithm can find them. And modularity is biased: even a random graph has partitions with substantial Q, so a high value is not evidence of communities. Treat detected communities as a hypothesis, check their stability under resampling and compare them with the structure found in null models.

modularity Q = Σ_c [e_c/m − (d_c/2m)²] · detectable only if (a − b)² > 2(a + b) · noise graphs also have Q > 0

5Null models: is this pattern surprising? Advanced

A network statistic means something only relative to a null model. The Erdős–Rényi null (same number of nodes and links, links placed at random) is almost always the wrong comparison: networks with hubs have many triangles, short paths and apparent communities even when linked at random given the degrees, so against the Erdős–Rényi null nearly everything looks significant. The configuration model keeps every node’s degree and randomises the pairing, and is generated in practice by repeated double-edge swaps (replace links a–b and c–d by a–d and c–b). The p-value is the share of null networks with a statistic at least as extreme, with (1 + count)/(1 + B) for B simulations. Stronger nulls (preserving also degree correlations, node attributes, time structure) answer more specific questions. State the null with every network result: “unusually clustered” is incomplete without “given the degrees”.

p = (1 + #{null ≥ observed})/(1 + B) · configuration model: fix all degrees, randomise links · double-edge swap a–b, c–d → a–d, c–b

6Observing only part of the network Advanced

A bank sees its own customers and the payments that pass through its system, not the whole network, and the part it sees is selected. Network statistics of a sampled network are biased and the bias depends on the sampling scheme. In node sampling (observe a random share f of the nodes and the links among them) mean degree falls to f times its value, clustering is partly kept, the tail of the degree distribution is truncated and hubs are misidentified; in link sampling mean degree is f times the truth and clustering falls like f² (a triangle needs all its links). Snowball and respondent-driven samples are selected towards high-degree nodes. There are corrections for particular statistics and schemes, but no general one: nodes are not independent, so the bootstrap and standard errors of ordinary statistics do not carry over. The first question for any network feature is who and what was observed.

node sampling: mean degree × f · link sampling: triangles × f³, clustering × f² · degrees of sampled nodes are not the true degrees

Part III · Network methods for risk

7Guilt by association: neighbour-based features Expert

The most used network idea in fraud and AML is that risky accounts link to risky accounts (homophily) so that the labels of an account’s neighbours predict its own. The simplest features are the count and the share of known-risky neighbours, extended to two hops and to counterparty types. Their value depends on three things: how strong the homophily is (with none, neighbours carry no information), how many labels are known (few known frauds make the feature sparse), and how the labels were obtained (investigations of earlier alerts, so selected). Practical care is needed with leakage (a neighbour’s label must have been known at the scoring date), with hubs (a merchant linked to many frauds is not itself fraudulent; use shares or degree-adjusted measures) and with the evaluation, which must exclude the labelled seeds and use later periods.

feature: #known-risky neighbours, share = # / degree · needs homophily · evaluate on accounts whose own label is unknown at the scoring date

8Finding rings: dense subgraphs and scan statistics Expert

Mule networks and fraud rings show up as groups of accounts with unusually many links among themselves. Densest-subgraph methods find the group maximising links per node, and peeling (repeatedly deleting the least-connected node) gives a fast approximate solution. The statistical issue is that the most extreme patch of a large sparse network is always denser than average: the maximum over many subsets has its own null distribution, so a found ring needs a p-value or limit from simulated null networks (scan statistic). Power depends on the ring’s size and internal density relative to the background: small or loosely linked rings cannot be told from chance, and many “rings” in a large network will be false. Combine the structural signal with behavioural evidence (timing, amounts, shared devices) and measure the false-positive rate in the null before operationalising.

density = links/nodes · null distribution of the maximum density by simulation · ring density p(k − 1)/2 versus background d/2

9Homophily or influence? Expert

Linked accounts have similar outcomes: they churn together, default together, adopt a product together. Two stories produce this. In influence (contagion) one account’s behaviour changes its neighbours’; in homophily accounts link because they are already alike (same employer, same city, same stress) and would behave alike with no interaction. Regressing an account’s outcome on its neighbours’ outcomes confounds the two (Shalizi and Thomas): with homophily on an unobserved trait the estimate is positive even when influence is zero. Controlling for observed traits helps only as far as they capture the hidden ones. To claim influence one needs the tools of the causal items: timing (neighbours’ behaviour changes first, with pre-trends checked), instruments, natural experiments, or randomisation, with cluster-level assignment because the units interact (interference). The decision matters: targeting the neighbours of churners helps only if there is contagion.

naive slope = influence + confounding by shared traits · homophily and contagion are generically confounded · interference: SUTVA fails in networks

10Propagation and random walks: PageRank Expert

PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random walk that follows a random link with probability α (the damping) and otherwise jumps to a node drawn from a teleport distribution. With a uniform teleport it measures importance; with the teleport concentrated on known risky accounts, personalised PageRank (a form of risk propagation or taint analysis) scores every account by how much of the walk starting from the seeds reaches it, with distance-weighted contributions from all hops. The damping sets how far risk is allowed to travel: small values reproduce the 1-hop count, large ones spread widely and dilute the signal in networks where everything is a few hops from everything. It is a feature, not a verdict: tune α on later periods, exclude seeds from evaluation, treat hubs specially, and check whether the extra hops add anything beyond the 1-hop count.

x = α·Pᵀx + (1 − α)·r · r = distribution on known risky nodes · α → 0: 1-hop scores, α → 1: global structure

11Measurement error in networks Expert

Links are measurements with error: transactions that are captured and ones that are not, other banks’ accounts that are invisible, links inferred from names, devices or time windows that may be coincidences. The effect on network statistics differs between them: every ranking degrades as noise grows, hub-based measures such as degree and PageRank usually hold up somewhat better, and betweenness, paths and clustering are the most fragile because they depend on the exact structure. The tool is a perturbation analysis: delete and add links at plausible rates and see how rankings, communities and flagged rings change, and report the stability rather than a single ranking. Include the process generating missingness (it is rarely random: large transactions are captured, small ones are not) in the analysis, and prefer robust features when data quality is uncertain. Treat the network analysis as an input to a case, with documentation of definitions, coverage and sensitivity.

robust: degree, PageRank, share of risky neighbours · fragile: betweenness, path counts, clustering · stability under perturbation as a result
