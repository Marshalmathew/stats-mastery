Model Selection

Model selection: AIC, BIC, cross-validation and honest error.

Every real modelling project involves choosing: which variables, how flexible, which family, how much to regularise. The danger is that the same data are used both to choose and to judge, and the winner of a contest among many candidates looks better than it really is. This page covers the bias–variance decomposition, why training error lies, the information criteria AIC and BIC and when they disagree, cross-validation done correctly (and the leaks that ruin it), selection bias and nested validation, the problems of stepwise methods, model averaging, and validation for time-ordered data. It completes Stage 3.

Part I · Error and criteria

1Bias, variance and the shape of prediction error Beginner

At a point x₀ the expected squared prediction error of a fitted model decomposes into three parts:

E[(y₀ − f̂(x₀))²] = σ² (irreducible noise) + Bias[f̂(x₀)]² + Var[f̂(x₀)]

A simple model (low flexibility) cannot follow the true curve: high bias, low variance. A flexible model follows the truth on average but changes wildly from sample to sample: low bias, high variance. Total error is U-shaped in flexibility, and model selection is the search for the bottom of the U, using only one sample. Note that the bias and variance here are properties of the procedure over repeated samples; you never see them in your single data set, which is why they must be estimated indirectly.

2Training error is optimistic Intermediate

The residual error on the data used to fit a model always understates its error on new data, because the fit has adapted to the noise in that sample. For a linear model with k parameters and error variance σ² the expected optimism is exactly 2kσ²/n: test error ≈ training error + 2kσ²/n. This is the basis of Mallows’ C_p (and of AIC for Gaussian models): estimate the test error without a test set by adding a complexity penalty to the training error. Anything chosen by minimising training error alone (R², RSS) will always choose the largest model.

Lab 2 · Training error, test error and the Cp estimate

Five of the predictors are real; the others are noise. For each number of predictors, over 60 data sets: the training MSE (red), the true expected test MSE (green) and the Cp estimate, training MSE + 2kσ²/n (blue). The Cp curve tracks the test curve; the training curve falls forever.

3AIC and BIC: two criteria, two goals Advanced

For a model with maximised log-likelihood ℓ̂, k parameters and n observations:

AIC = −2ℓ̂ + 2k AICc = AIC + 2k(k+1)/(n − k − 1) BIC = −2ℓ̂ + k log n

Pick the model with the smallest value. AIC estimates out-of-sample log-loss (Kullback–Leibler distance to the truth) and is efficient: it aims for the best predictions, tolerating a few useless extras (it overfits mildly even with infinite data). BIC approximates the log marginal likelihood (a Bayes factor) and is consistent: if the true model is among the candidates, it finds it as n grows; it penalises more strongly once log n > 2 (n ≥ 8) and tends to choose smaller models. AICc corrects AIC for small n and should be used when n/k < 40. There is no universal winner: use AIC/CV for prediction, BIC when you want a parsimonious “explanatory” model and believe a simple truth exists.

Part II · Cross-validation

4Cross-validation: K-fold, leave-one-out and the bias–variance of CV itself Advanced

Cross-validation estimates out-of-sample error by repeatedly holding data out. In K-fold CV the data are split into K parts; each part is predicted by a model fitted on the other K−1. Leave-one-out (LOO, K = n) is nearly unbiased but its n fitted models are almost identical, so the estimates are highly correlated and the result can be high-variance; for linear models it needs no refitting: LOO error = mean[(eᵢ/(1 − hᵢ))²], where hᵢ is the leverage. Small K (2 to 5) trains on much less data than the final model, so it is pessimistically biased; K = 5 or 10 is the usual compromise. The CV estimate is itself noisy, particularly with small n or rare events, so report it with a standard error.

5Leakage: the way cross-validation is most often ruined Expert

Cross-validation only works if every data-dependent step of the pipeline is repeated inside each training fold: feature screening, standardisation, imputation, resampling, encoding, tuning. If you select the 10 best of 1,000 predictors using the whole data set and then cross-validate a model on those 10, the held-out folds have already influenced the selection, and pure noise will appear to predict. The same applies to any target-aware step, to duplicates and near-duplicates (the same customer in training and validation folds), and to variables that encode the future. Such errors produce models that look excellent in development and fail in production, which is exactly what model-risk reviewers look for.

Lab 5 · A model with no skill that “validates”

The outcome is pure noise: no predictor has any relationship with it. We keep the k predictors most correlated with y and fit least squares. “Leaky CV” screens on all data first; “proper CV” screens inside each fold. A skill of 0 means no better than predicting the mean.

6The winner’s curse of selection, and nested validation Expert

Even a correctly done CV is not an unbiased estimate of the selected model’s performance. If you evaluate M candidates by CV and keep the best, its score is the maximum of M noisy estimates and is optimistic; the more candidates (and the noisier the CV), the worse. The remedy is a final, untouched test set, or nested cross-validation: an outer loop evaluates the whole “select-then-fit” procedure on held-out data while an inner loop does the selection. The outer score estimates the performance of the procedure, which is what you will actually deploy.

Part III · Selection in practice

7Stepwise selection and its false discoveries Advanced

Forward, backward and stepwise selection add or drop variables by p-values or F-statistics. They are fast and simple but have well-known defects: the final model’s R², F-test and coefficient p-values are all computed as if the model had been specified in advance, so they are badly over-optimistic; the coefficients of selected variables are biased away from zero; the choice is unstable under small changes in the data; and the procedure is not guaranteed to find the best subset. Regularisation, cross-validated selection, or pre-specification on subject-matter grounds are better.

8Model uncertainty: Akaike weights and model averaging Advanced

When several models fit nearly equally well, choosing one and acting as if it were known discards the uncertainty about the choice. Differences in AIC convert to Akaike weights, wᵢ = exp(−Δᵢ/2)/Σexp(−Δⱼ/2), the relative support for each model; models within about 2 AIC units of the best are essentially tied. A model-averaged prediction, Σwᵢf̂ᵢ, is usually more accurate than the single winner (it behaves like a smoothed version of selection and is less sensitive to small changes), and the weights are an honest summary of how much the data can say. Bayesian model averaging does the same with BIC-like weights.

9Validation for time-ordered data Expert

Random K-fold CV assumes that observations are exchangeable. When they are ordered in time (credit performance, transactions, markets) neighbours are correlated and the model will be used on the future. Random folds then put near-copies of the validation points in the training set and report error far below the true forward-looking error, especially for flexible models that can fit smooth, slowly-varying noise. Use out-of-time validation: train on the past, validate on the following period; for tuning, use rolling-origin (forward-chaining) CV with training windows that always precede the validation window. Regulatory model validation expects out-of-time and out-of-sample tests in addition to random splits.

10Selecting on the right metric: AUC, log-loss and calibration Advanced

Criteria differ in what they reward. AUC measures only the ranking and is insensitive to over-fitted probabilities; log-loss and the Brier score are proper scoring rules that also punish miscalibrated or over-confident probabilities. A model chosen on AUC may rank well yet give poor probabilities, which matters for pricing, provisioning and capital. Choose the metric that matches the decision: ranking or targeting (AUC, lift, KS), probability use (log-loss, Brier, calibration), cost-weighted decisions (expected cost at a threshold). Also see how selection by information criterion (AIC, BIC) lands relative to the best validation score.

11Instability, bagging and averaging over subsets Expert

Subset selection is a discontinuous procedure: a small change in the data can swap a variable in or out, and prediction error then has a large variance component. Bagging (bootstrap aggregation) re-runs the selection on bootstrap samples and averages the resulting coefficients, which smooths out the instability; averaging over models with Akaike weights does the same without resampling; shrinkage (ridge, lasso) is another smooth alternative. In practice all of these beat a single stepwise or best-subset choice, particularly when predictors are correlated and the effects are small.
