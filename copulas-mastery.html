Copulas and Dependence

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Copulas: modelling how risks move together, separately from how big each one is.

A correlation coefficient is a single number that summarises the middle of a joint distribution, yet the risks that matter (portfolio losses, defaults that cluster, fraud rings, a crisis that hits several books at once) live in the corners. A copula separates a joint distribution into its margins and its dependence structure, so each can be modelled and checked on its own. This page covers why linear correlation is not enough, Sklar’s theorem, rank correlations, the Gaussian, Student t and Archimedean families, tail dependence, the effect of dependence on portfolio risk, estimation and model choice, the conditional-correlation trap, and a credit-portfolio model. Every lab simulates from a known copula, so estimators and models can be judged against the truth.

Part I · Dependence beyond linear correlation

1Why correlation is not enough Beginner

The Pearson correlation measures the strength of the linear relationship and depends on both the dependence and the shapes of the margins. It can be identical for joint distributions that behave very differently where it matters: two assets can have a correlation of 0.5 and still differ greatly in how often both lose heavily on the same day. It is also not defined when the variance is infinite, it can be changed by a monotone transformation (such as taking logs) that leaves the relationship intact, and it can be zero when variables are strongly dependent. A copula is the joint distribution of the variables after each has been transformed to a uniform margin, so it describes only how the variables are tied together; the standard normal margins used in the first lab are just a convenient scale for looking at it.

Pearson ρ = Cov(X, Y)/(σₓσᵧ) · depends on the margins · same ρ, different joint tails

2Sklar’s theorem: margins and copula Intermediate

Sklar’s theorem says that any joint distribution function can be written H(x, y) = C(F(x), G(y)), where F and G are the margins and C is a copula (a joint distribution on the unit square with uniform margins). Conversely, any copula combined with any margins gives a valid joint distribution. This is a modelling licence: fit each margin with the distribution that suits it (a heavy-tailed severity, a skewed return), model the dependence separately, and recombine. The copula is unchanged by strictly increasing transformations of the variables, so anything computed from it is invariant to the margins; Pearson correlation is not, which is why it is the wrong summary of dependence for variables with skewed or heavy-tailed margins. For discrete margins the copula is not unique, a complication for default and count data.

H(x, y) = C(F(x), G(y)) · C(u, v) = P(U ≤ u, V ≤ v), U = F(X), V = G(Y) · invariant to increasing transformations

3Rank correlations: Kendall’s τ and Spearman’s ρ Intermediate

Two measures depend only on the copula. Kendall’s τ is the probability that two randomly chosen pairs are concordant minus the probability they are discordant; Spearman’s ρS is the Pearson correlation of the ranks. Both lie in [−1, 1], are unchanged by increasing transformations of the margins, exist for any distribution (no moments needed), and are robust to outliers. For the Gaussian copula they map exactly to the copula parameter, ρ = sin(πτ/2) and ρ = 2 sin(πρS/6), which gives a rank-based estimator of ρ that works when the margins are heavy-tailed. For an Archimedean copula, τ is a function of the single parameter. They are summaries of overall association and, like correlation, say little about the tails: two copulas with the same τ can have very different tail dependence.

τ = P(concordant) − P(discordant) · Gaussian copula: ρ = sin(πτ/2) = 2 sin(πρₛ/6) · Clayton θ = 2τ/(1 − τ) · Gumbel θ = 1/(1 − τ)

Part II · Copula families and tail dependence

4The Gaussian copula and its blind spot Advanced

The Gaussian copula takes two correlated standard normal variables and passes each through the normal cdf; it is the copula of the bivariate normal distribution and of every Gaussian factor model. It is flexible in higher dimensions (a correlation matrix), easy to simulate and to fit, and it underlies the Basel credit-risk formula. Its weakness is in the corners. The coefficient of tail dependence λ = lim P(Y extreme | X extreme) is zero for the Gaussian copula whenever ρ < 1: however high the correlation, joint extremes become asymptotically independent. At realistic levels (99%, 99.9%) the conditional probability is not zero, but it falls with the level, so the model understates joint tail events the more extreme they are. This is the heart of the criticism of Gaussian-copula pricing of CDOs before 2008.

λᵤ = lim_{q→1} P(V > q | U > q) · Gaussian copula: λ = 0 for ρ < 1 · C(u, v) = Φ₂(Φ⁻¹(u), Φ⁻¹(v); ρ)

5Archimedean copulas: Clayton, Gumbel and Frank Advanced

An Archimedean copula is generated by a single decreasing function φ: C(u, v) = φ⁻¹(φ(u) + φ(v)). With one parameter θ it is parsimonious and easy to simulate, and it can be asymmetric. Clayton has lower-tail dependence (joint small values) and none in the upper tail; Gumbel has upper-tail dependence (joint large values) and none in the lower; Frank is symmetric with no tail dependence, resembling the Gaussian. For a loss variable, joint large losses call for upper-tail dependence (Gumbel); for a quality or return variable, joint downturns call for lower-tail dependence (Clayton). Each family is a different qualitative statement about where things go wrong together, so the choice should follow the risk story rather than convenience. Their limitation is that they are exchangeable and, beyond two dimensions, restrictive: every pair gets the same dependence.

Clayton: C = (u⁻ᶿ + v⁻ᶿ − 1)^(−1/θ), λ_L = 2^(−1/θ) · Gumbel: C = exp{−[(−ln u)ᶿ + (−ln v)ᶿ]^(1/θ)}, λ_U = 2 − 2^(1/θ) · Frank: λ = 0

6The t copula and tail dependence Advanced

The Student t copula adds one parameter, the degrees of freedom ν, to the Gaussian: it is the copula of the multivariate t, where all margins share a common random scale that makes extreme moves arrive together. It is symmetric and has positive tail dependence in both corners, λ = 2 tν+1(−√((ν + 1)(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ))), which rises as ν falls and as ρ rises, and tends to the Gaussian (λ = 0) as ν → ∞. It is the standard first alternative to the Gaussian in practice and extends naturally to high dimensions, though it still has a single ν and symmetric corners. Tail dependence is easy to define and hard to estimate: it is a limit, and any sample has only a handful of points in the corner, so empirical estimates are noisy and biased, and the sample cannot tell a finite λ from a slowly vanishing conditional probability.

λ = 2 t_{ν+1}(−√((ν+1)(1−ρ)/(1+ρ))) · λ ↑ as ν ↓ and as ρ ↑ · ν → ∞ gives the Gaussian

Part III · Copulas in practice

7Dependence and portfolio risk Expert

For risk aggregation, what matters is the distribution of a sum (or any aggregate) of the losses, and for a given set of margins that distribution depends on the copula. With the same margins and the same rank correlation, different copulas give different VaR and ES for the sum, and the gap widens with the confidence level. The reason is the one-big-jump behaviour of heavy tails (item 31): a high quantile of the sum is reached when one loss is huge, and dependence in the upper tail adds the chance that several are huge together. In regulatory capital this appears as the diversification benefit claimed when aggregating risk types: it depends entirely on the assumed copula, and a Gaussian copula with a modest correlation can overstate it substantially. Reasonable practice is to test the aggregate against alternatives with tail dependence, and to present the capital figure as a range.

VaR_q(L₁ + L₂) depends on C, not only on ρ · tail dependence raises VaR most at high q · diversification benefit = Σ VaR_q(Lᵢ) − VaR_q(ΣLᵢ)

8Estimating a copula from data Expert

Estimation has two parts, the margins and the copula, and the usual routes differ in how they handle the margins. Full maximum likelihood fits everything jointly. Inference functions for margins (IFM) fits parametric margins first, converts the data with their fitted cdfs, and then fits the copula. Canonical maximum likelihood (pseudo-observations) replaces each value by its rank divided by n + 1, so the margins are estimated non-parametrically and cannot be misspecified; it is the default when the copula is the object of interest. A simple alternative for one-parameter families is inversion of Kendall’s τ. Misspecified parametric margins contaminate the copula estimate, because the transformed data are not uniform, so the copula parameter absorbs the margin error. For high dimensions the common approach is to fit pairwise or to use pair-copula constructions (vines), which combine bivariate copulas into a multivariate model.

Pseudo-observations: ûᵢ = rank(xᵢ)/(n + 1) · CML: θ̂ = argmax Σ ln c(ûᵢ, v̂ᵢ; θ) · inversion: θ̂ = g⁻¹(τ̂)

9Goodness of fit and choosing a family Expert

Given a sample, which copula? Formal tests compare the empirical copula Cn (the observed joint distribution of the pseudo-observations) with the fitted one: the Cramér–von Mises statistic Sn = Σ (Cn(ûᵢ, v̂ᵢ) − Cθ̂(ûᵢ, v̂ᵢ))², whose null distribution is obtained by a parametric bootstrap (the p-value must account for the estimation of θ). Other tools are graphical (a K-plot, Kendall’s transform), information criteria on the copula likelihood, and checks of the corners. All of them are low-powered in the tails, where the families differ most, since the data contain few joint extremes. The result is typically that several families are not rejected and the decision rests on judgment: which dependence story is credible, and how sensitive is the quantity of interest (a capital figure, a stress loss) to the choice. Report that sensitivity.

Sₙ = Σᵢ [Cₙ(ûᵢ, v̂ᵢ) − C_θ̂(ûᵢ, v̂ᵢ)]² · p-value by parametric bootstrap · low power in the tails

10Conditional correlation and stress dependence Expert

A recurring claim is that “correlations go to one in a crisis”. Evidence is often a correlation computed on the subsample of days on which one market moved a lot, and this is biased by selection. If X is restricted to a region where its variance is smaller (the upper tail above a threshold) the sample correlation with Y falls even when the true relationship is constant; if instead selection is on a high-volatility regime, the variance of the combined sample rises and so does the measured correlation, whether or not the underlying dependence changed (Forbes and Rigobon). The correct description of stress dependence uses quantities that are not distorted by selection: tail dependence coefficients, conditional exceedance probabilities, regime-switching or stochastic-correlation models, or copulas with tail dependence fitted to the whole sample. It is also useful to separate a change in volatility from a change in dependence before concluding that diversification fails when it is needed.

corr(X, Y | X > c) = ρ s/√(ρ²s² + 1 − ρ²) for a normal, with s² the variance of the selected X · s < 1 so it falls

11Credit portfolios and default correlation Expert

The factor model for default is a copula in disguise. Each obligor has a latent asset value Xi = √ρ Z + √(1 − ρ) εi and defaults if Xi falls below a threshold set by its probability of default; the common factor Z is what makes defaults cluster. For a large portfolio the loss fraction converges to a closed form, the Vasicek distribution, whose quantile is the basis of the Basel IRB capital formula: the 99.9% loss is Φ((Φ⁻¹(PD) + √ρ Φ⁻¹(0.999))/√(1 − ρ)). The Gaussian factor model has no tail dependence, so the model can understate clustering of defaults in a downturn; a t factor model (a common random scale) fattens the portfolio loss tail at the same average default rate and the same ρ. Capital is highly sensitive to ρ, which is difficult to estimate from short benign histories, so the model needs sensitivity analysis and stress scenarios, not a single calibrated value.

Vasicek: P(loss ≤ x) = Φ[(√(1 − ρ) Φ⁻¹(x) − Φ⁻¹(PD))/√ρ] · q-quantile = Φ[(Φ⁻¹(PD) + √ρ Φ⁻¹(q))/√(1 − ρ)]
