Core Distributions

Ten distributions, one family.

Normal, binomial, Poisson, exponential, gamma, beta, lognormal, χ², t and F look like ten separate things to memorise. They aren't. Each one is a story about how data get generated, and the stories are wired to each other. Learn the wiring and you can derive most facts instead of remembering them.

Part I · The map

1The family map Beginner

Start with the picture, then fill in the details. Every arrow is a theorem you will see verified by simulation in Chapter 12.

The family tree

Blue = counting successes · green = waiting and positive amounts · purple = the bell curve family · teal = the sampling distributions used in tests · yellow = probabilities themselves.

Five stories cover the whole map:

  1. Counting successes — a trial succeeds or fails (Bernoulli); count successes in n trials (Binomial); let trials be countless and each success rare (Poisson).
  2. Waiting — time between rare events is Exponential; the time to the k-th event is Gamma.
  3. Adding up noise — sums of many small independent effects are Normal. Square standard normal noise and add it up → χ². Divide a normal by an independent χ² → t. Divide two χ²s → F.
  4. Multiplying effects — products of many positive factors are Lognormal (log them and you're back at the Normal).
  5. Uncertainty about a probability — a quantity living in [0,1] with a flexible shape is Beta; it is the natural partner of the Binomial.

Part II · Counts and waiting

2Bernoulli and Binomial Beginner

A Bernoulli(p) variable is one yes/no trial: 1 with probability p, 0 otherwise. Mean p, variance p(1−p) — largest at p = ½, shrinking to 0 as the outcome becomes certain. A Binomial(n, p) counts the successes in n independent Bernoulli(p) trials:

P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ E[X] = np Var(X) = np(1−p)

Mean and variance follow instantly from the previous course: X is a sum of n indicators, so linearity gives np, and independence lets variances add.

The three assumptions (and how banks violate them)(1) fixed n, (2) the same p for every trial, (3) independence. Defaults cluster in time and sector, so Var(X) is usually larger than np(1−p): this is overdispersion. Allowing p itself to vary gives the beta-binomial (Chapter 11), and with pairwise correlation ρ the variance is np(1−p)[1+(n−1)ρ].

Lab 1 · Binomial explorer

Slide p toward 0.5 and n up: the shape becomes symmetric and bell-like (the normal approximation). Slide p toward 0.02 with small n: strongly right-skewed. Turn on the normal overlay and watch where it fails.

Normal approximation rule of thumb: use it when both np and n(1−p) are at least 10 (many say 5). With PD = 1% on 500 loans, np = 5 — skewed, so use exact binomial or Poisson methods.

3Poisson Beginner

The Poisson(λ) counts events in a fixed window when events are rare, independent and arrive at a steady average rate λ per window.

P(X = k) = e^(−λ) · λᵏ / k! E[X] = Var(X) = λ

Three facts to own: (1) mean = variance — a quick diagnostic (variance/mean ≫ 1 signals overdispersion; use a negative binomial). (2) Additivity: independent Poisson(λ₁) + Poisson(λ₂) = Poisson(λ₁+λ₂). (3) It is the limit of the binomial when n → ∞, p → 0 with np = λ fixed. Fraud alerts per day, account takeovers per week, ATM withdrawals per minute are all natural Poisson candidates.

Lab 2a · Poisson explorer

For small λ the distribution piles up at 0 and 1; around λ ≈ 20 it is nearly a normal bell. Check that the SD is always √λ.

Lab 2b · Binomial → Poisson

Blue bars: Binomial(n, λ/n). Yellow dots: Poisson(λ). Increase n with λ fixed and watch the bars snap onto the dots. TV distance is the largest possible difference in the probability of any event.

4Exponential Intermediate

If events arrive as a Poisson process with rate λ, the gaps between consecutive events are Exponential(λ). Counts per window are Poisson; waits between events are exponential — two views of the same process.

f(x) = λ e^(−λx) F(x) = 1 − e^(−λx) E[X] = 1/λ Var(X) = 1/λ² median = ln 2 / λ

Memorylessness

P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t). Having already waited s minutes tells you nothing about the remaining wait: the hazard rate is constant at λ. This is the exponential's defining property and its main weakness — real durations (time to default, customer lifetime) usually have changing hazards, for which the Gamma or Weibull is better. Another handy fact: the minimum of independent exponentials with rates λ₁…λₘ is exponential with rate Σλᵢ.

Lab 3a · Exponential explorer

The mean is 1/λ but the median is only 0.69/λ: a long right tail drags the mean above the median. Skewness is always exactly 2.

Lab 3b · One event stream, two distributions

We simulate a Poisson process, record the gaps (exponential) and the counts per unit time (Poisson), and test memorylessness: among gaps longer than s, how much longer do they last on average?

5Gamma Intermediate

The time until the k-th event of a Poisson process is the sum of k independent exponentials: a Gamma (called Erlang when k is an integer). Allowing k to be any positive number gives a flexible family for positive, right-skewed quantities such as claim sizes and balances.

X ~ Gamma(shape a, rate β): f(x) ∝ x^(a−1) e^(−βx) E[X] = a/β Var(X) = a/β² skewness = 2/√a
Rate vs scaleTextbooks and software disagree: some parametrise with rate β, others with scale θ = 1/β (R's rgamma takes rate by default, scipy takes scale). Always check which one a library wants. Here we use rate throughout.

Shape controls skew: a = 1 is the exponential; a < 1 puts a spike at 0; as a grows the Gamma becomes symmetric and approaches the normal. The coefficient of variation is 1/√a regardless of the rate — a Gamma model says “spread is proportional to the mean”, the same property that makes it popular in severity modelling.

Lab 4a · Gamma explorer

Set shape = 1 to recover the exponential. Raise the shape to 15 and watch skewness fall as 2/√a.

Lab 4b · Sum of k exponentials is a Gamma

Each of 4,000 trials adds up k independent Exponential(rate) waits. The histogram is compared with the exact Gamma(k, rate) curve.

Part III · The bell curve and its offspring

6Normal Beginner

The Normal(μ, σ²) is the limit law of sums (central limit theorem) and the most closed-under-operations family there is: any linear combination of normals is normal, so aX + bY ~ Normal(aμₓ + bμ_y, a²σₓ² + b²σ_y² + 2ab·Cov). It is fully described by its mean and variance.

f(x) = (1/(σ√(2π))) · exp(−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)) z = (x − μ)/σ 68% within 1σ, 95% within 1.96σ, 99.7% within 3σ

Standardising turns any normal into the standard Normal(0,1): a z-score counts how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean. And uniquely for the (jointly) normal family, zero correlation does imply independence — the one place the converse from the previous course holds.

Lab 5a · Normal explorer

μ slides the curve; σ stretches it. Skewness and excess kurtosis are always 0.

Lab 5b · z-score and tail calculator

7Lognormal Intermediate

X is Lognormal(μ, σ²) when ln X is Normal(μ, σ²). Since ln of a product is a sum of logs, the CLT says a product of many independent positive factors is approximately lognormal — compounding returns, multiplicative growth, transaction amounts built from many small multipliers.

median = e^μ E[X] = e^(μ + σ²/2) (always above the median) CV = √(e^σ² − 1)
Back-transform trapIf you model ln X and then exponentiate the average of the logs, you get the median (geometric mean), not the mean. The arithmetic mean needs the +σ²/2 correction. In expected-loss and revenue models this error is systematic and always in the same direction.

Lab 6a · Lognormal explorer

Increase σ: the mean and median drift apart and the right tail explodes (skewness rises very fast). With σ = 1.5 the SD of the mean is huge and sample averages become unreliable.

Lab 6b · Products become lognormal

Each trial multiplies m independent random factors, each between e^(−s) and e^(+s) (log-uniform). Left: the product is skewed; right: its logarithm is symmetric and bell-shaped as m grows.

8Chi-square Intermediate

If Z₁…Z_k are independent standard normals, Z₁² + … + Z_k² ~ χ²(k). It is also exactly a Gamma with shape k/2 and rate ½. Mean k, variance 2k, always right-skewed, approaching normal as k grows. It is additive: χ²(a) + χ²(b) = χ²(a+b) for independent pieces.

Why it matters: whenever we measure “total squared distance from expectation” we get a χ². In particular, the sample variance of normal data satisfies

(n − 1)·s² / σ² ~ χ²(n − 1)

which is the basis of confidence intervals for a variance. χ² is also behind goodness-of-fit and independence tests on tables (the Pearson statistic, previous course Ch. 6) and likelihood-ratio tests (2Δℓ ~ χ², Ch. 7).

Lab 7a · Chi-square explorer

With 1 degree of freedom the density explodes at 0 (it's a squared normal). With 30 it is nearly bell-shaped. Compare the 95% quantile with the mean to see how critical values work.

Lab 7b · Where χ² comes from

9Student's t Intermediate

When you standardise a mean using the estimated standard deviation, the extra uncertainty fattens the tails. Formally: if Z ~ N(0,1) and V ~ χ²(ν) independently,

T = Z / √(V/ν) ~ t(ν) t(ν) → Normal as ν → ∞ t(1) = Cauchy

The tails of t(ν) decay like a power law, so only moments below ν exist: t(2) has infinite variance, t(1) has no mean. Beyond testing, the t is a standard model for fat-tailed returns and residuals because it nests “normal” as a limit and “very heavy-tailed” at small ν.

Lab 8a · t explorer

Overlay the normal. At ν = 1–3 the t is visibly heavier-tailed; by ν ≈ 30 the two are almost indistinguishable. Watch the 95th percentile approach 1.645.

Lab 8b · The t-statistic in action

Draw samples of size n from a normal population, compute T = x̄ / (s/√n), repeat 6,000 times. The yellow curve is the t(n−1) law; the purple one is the normal that people wrongly use for small n.

10F distribution Intermediate

The ratio of two independent χ² variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom, is an F(d₁, d₂). It compares variances: if two independent samples of sizes n₁, n₂ come from normal populations with equal variance, then s₁²/s₂² ~ F(n₁−1, n₂−1). It also drives ANOVA (between-group variance ÷ within-group variance) and the overall F-test in regression.

F = (χ²(d₁)/d₁) / (χ²(d₂)/d₂) mean = d₂/(d₂−2) for d₂ > 2 t(ν)² = F(1, ν) F(d₁, ∞) = χ²(d₁)/d₁

Note that F is always positive and right-skewed, and that its mean is slightly above 1 even when the variances are equal (it is a ratio — Jensen again).

Lab 9a · F explorer

Small d₂ gives enormously heavy right tails. As d₂ grows the distribution tightens around 1.

Part IV · Probabilities of probabilities

11Beta Advanced

The Beta(a, b) lives on [0, 1], so it is the natural distribution for an unknown probability, rate or proportion (a PD, a response rate, a market share). It can be flat, U-shaped, bell-shaped, J-shaped or skewed depending on a and b.

f(p) ∝ p^(a−1) (1−p)^(b−1) E[p] = a/(a+b) Var(p) = ab / ((a+b)²(a+b+1)) Beta(1,1) = Uniform(0,1)

Conjugacy with the binomial. Start with a Beta(a, b) belief about p. Observe k successes in n trials. The posterior is exactly Beta(a + k, b + n − k). Read a and b as pseudo-counts: prior “successes” and “failures”. The posterior mean (a+k)/(a+b+n) is a weighted average of the prior mean and the observed rate k/n, with weight (a+b)/(a+b+n) on the prior — the more data, the less the prior matters. This is the cleanest way to estimate a PD for a segment with very few defaults.

Link to the Gamma: if X ~ Gamma(a), Y ~ Gamma(b) independently (same rate), then X/(X+Y) ~ Beta(a, b). Link to heterogeneity: if each account's p is drawn from a Beta and defaults are then binomial, the counts are beta-binomial — overdispersed relative to a plain binomial, with correlation ρ = 1/(a+b+1).

Lab 10a · Beta explorer

Try a = b = 0.5 (U-shape), a = b = 1 (flat), a = 2, b = 8 (typical PD-like shape), a = b = 20 (tight bell).

Lab 10b · Updating a PD estimate

A segment has a prior PD belief Beta(a, b). You observe k defaults in n loans. Compare prior (blue), posterior (red) and the raw observed rate (yellow line). With little data the posterior stays near the prior; with lots, it follows the data.

13Choosing a distribution for real data Expert

Pick a distribution the way a good analyst reads a crime scene: three clues narrow the field before any fitting.

  1. Support. Integers only → binomial/Poisson/negative binomial. Strictly positive → gamma/lognormal/exponential. In [0,1] → beta. Whole real line → normal/t.
  2. The variance–mean relationship. Variance ≈ mean → Poisson. Variance ∝ mean² (constant coefficient of variation) → gamma or lognormal. Variance far above mean for counts → overdispersion (negative binomial, beta-binomial).
  3. Tail and skew. Plot a histogram and a log-scale or QQ plot. Symmetric with outliers beyond 3σ → t. Right skew with a long tail → lognormal (heavier) or gamma (lighter). A spike at zero plus a continuous part → mixture (zero-inflated), not any single distribution here.
