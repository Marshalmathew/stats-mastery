Anomaly and Drift Detection

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Anomaly and drift detection: deciding when something is unusual, and when the world has changed.

Fraud and AML alerts, data-quality checks and model monitoring all rest on the same question: is this observation, or this stream, different from what we expect? The statistics of “different” are less simple than they look. This page covers what an anomaly is and why base rates decide how useful an alert is, robust and multivariate thresholds, distance, density and isolation methods, evaluating detectors when anomalies are rare, control charts and their run-length trade-offs, the multiple-testing problem of monitoring many features, drift measures (PSI, KS, Wasserstein) and their sample-size traps, the difference between covariate, concept and prior drift, window design, and the cost-based choice of thresholds. Every lab simulates a known truth so detectors can be judged against it.

Part I · What counts as unusual

1Anomalies, thresholds and base rates Beginner

An anomaly is an observation that differs from what the process normally produces: a fraudulent transaction, a faulty sensor, a corrupted record, a customer whose behaviour has changed. Statistically, one defines normal (a model of the reference data), scores how far each case is from it, and raises an alert above a threshold. Two error rates describe the detector, the detection rate and the false-alarm rate, but neither tells the user how much to trust an alert. That is the precision, which also depends on the base rate of anomalies: when they are rare, even a very low false-alarm rate produces more false alerts than real ones, because there are so many ordinary cases to raise them on. This is Bayes’ theorem (item 3) applied to monitoring, and it is why the first calculation for any detector is the expected alert volume and the share of it that will be false.

precision = π·TPR / (π·TPR + (1 − π)·FPR) · π = base rate of anomalies · alerts = N[(1 − π)FPR + π·TPR]

2Statistical thresholds and robustness Intermediate

The classical rule flags observations beyond the mean ± 3 standard deviations. It rests on two assumptions that often fail: the normal distribution (for heavy-tailed data a fixed multiple of the sd gives many more false alarms than the 0.27% of normal theory) and uncontaminated data. If the sample used to compute the mean and sd already contains outliers, they inflate both, and the limits move outwards so that the outliers escape: masking. Robust estimates, the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD, scaled by 1.4826 to match the sd under normality), have a breakdown point of 50%: up to half of the data can be contaminated before they are ruined. For skewed or heavy-tailed data transform the data or use the empirical or fitted tail, and set limits from a clean reference period.

robust z = (x − median)/(1.4826·MAD) · breakdown point: mean/sd 0%, median/MAD 50% · 3 sd = 0.27% only for a normal

3Multivariate anomalies and the Mahalanobis distance Intermediate

A case can be unremarkable on every variable and still be an anomaly because the combination is unusual: a customer whose income and spending are each typical but are almost never seen together. Checking variables one at a time cannot find these. The Mahalanobis distance measures how far a point is from the centre in units of the data’s own covariance structure, d² = (x − μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(x − μ); under a multivariate normal model d² follows a χ² distribution with p degrees of freedom, which gives a calibrated threshold. In practice μ and Σ must be estimated robustly (minimum covariance determinant, or from a clean period) or the contamination masks itself as in chapter 2; for many variables the covariance is poorly estimated and distances concentrate, so one usually works with principal components, an autoencoder’s reconstruction error, or another learned representation of normal.

d² = (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ) ~ χ²ₚ under normality · 99.9% limit for p = 2: 13.8 · robust μ and Σ (MCD) to avoid masking

Part II · Detection methods and monitoring

4Distance, density and isolation methods Advanced

When normal is not one elliptical cloud, shape-free methods are used. Distance-based methods score a point by its distance to its k nearest neighbours (large means isolated); density-based ones, such as the local outlier factor, compare a point’s local density with that of its neighbours, which handles clusters of different densities. The isolation forest builds random trees by splitting at random on random variables; points that need few splits to be isolated are anomalies, and the average path length gives the score s = 2^(−E[h]/c(n)). It is fast (sub-sampling, linear in n), needs no distance computation and is the usual baseline. All of them rest on the premise that anomalies are few and different, so they fail for anomalies that form their own dense group, or that differ only on a variable that the model ignores, and every one needs a threshold, a contamination rate or a number of alerts chosen from capacity and cost.

isolation score s(x) = 2^(−E[h(x)]/c(ψ)) · k-NN score = mean distance to the k nearest · Mahalanobis assumes one ellipse

5Evaluating detectors with rare anomalies Advanced

Evaluation is hard for three reasons. First, anomalies are rare, so every metric is estimated from few events and is noisy. Second, the choice of metric matters: the AUC ignores prevalence and can look excellent while the alert queue is mostly false; the precision–recall curve and average precision reflect what investigators experience, and precision at k (the top k alerts the team can review) is the operational figure. Third, labels are usually produced by investigating cases that were already alerted, so the labelled sample is selected by the detector (and by earlier detectors): precision of reviewed alerts can be estimated, but recall and AUC need a random audit sample, or inverse-probability weighting if the review probabilities are known. Also consider time: evaluate on future periods, not a random split, because fraud and attack patterns change.

AUC: independent of prevalence · average precision: depends on prevalence · precision@k at the review capacity

6Sequential detection: control charts and run lengths Advanced

A stream is monitored case by case, so the question is when to stop and sound an alarm. Three charts are standard. The Shewhart chart alarms when one observation passes ±3 sd. The EWMA chart alarms when an exponentially weighted average zₜ = λxₜ + (1 − λ)zₜ₋₁ leaves its limits. The CUSUM accumulates S⁺ₜ = max(0, S⁺ₜ₋₁ + xₜ − k) and alarms when it passes h; it is the optimal detector for a given shift (Page, Lorden). Performance is two numbers: the in-control average run length ARL₀ (time between false alarms) and the detection delay after a change. At equal ARL₀ the Shewhart chart is best for large jumps and poor for small shifts, while EWMA and CUSUM accumulate evidence and detect small persistent shifts several times faster. Fixing a false-alarm rate per observation is the wrong question for a stream: even 0.27% per case means an alarm every 370 cases.

Shewhart: |x| > 3 (ARL₀ ≈ 370) · EWMA: zₜ = λxₜ + (1 − λ)zₜ₋₁ · CUSUM: Sₜ = max(0, Sₜ₋₁ + xₜ − k), alarm if Sₜ > h · ARL₀ and delay trade off

7Monitoring many features at once Expert

Production monitoring tracks hundreds of features, scores and segments, each tested daily. With m independent tests at level α, the expected number of false alerts per day is mα and the chance of at least one is 1 − (1 − α)ᵐ, which is near 1 for realistic m: the dashboard is permanently lit. This is the multiple-testing problem (item 21) and the remedies carry over. Bonferroni (test each at α/m) controls the chance of any false alert but has little power when many features exist; Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery proportion among the alerts, which is the quantity that matters when every alert is investigated. Beyond correction, reduce the number of tests: monitor the model score and a few summary statistics, group features, weight by importance, and track alert volumes. Remember that features are correlated, so a single root cause (a data feed change) can trigger dozens of alerts that are one event.

P(at least one false alert) = 1 − (1 − α)ᵐ · Bonferroni: α/m · BH: largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ kα/m · control the false discovery proportion

Part III · Drift in deployed models

8Measuring distribution drift Expert

Drift scores compare a current batch with a reference. The population stability index, PSI = Σ (ci − ri) ln(ci/ri) over bins fixed on the reference, is the banking standard, with the rules of thumb 0.1 (investigate) and 0.25 (significant). It is a symmetrised KL divergence, so it is sensitive to empty bins and, in small samples, positively biased: even with no drift its expectation is about (bins − 1)(1/n₁ + 1/n₂). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic and test compare cumulative distributions; Wasserstein distance measures how much “mass” must move and has the units of the variable; χ² tests serve categorical variables. A significance test is not a drift measure: with enough data it rejects trivially small shifts. Use an effect size with limits grounded in the model’s sensitivity, and set the limits from the natural variability of the metric across stable periods.

PSI = Σ (cᵢ − rᵢ) ln(cᵢ/rᵢ) · E[PSI | no drift] ≈ (B − 1)(1/n₁ + 1/n₂) · KS = sup |F₁ − F₂| · W₁ = ∫|F₁ − F₂|

9Covariate, concept and prior drift Expert

Data drift is not one thing. Covariate drift: P(x) changes but P(y | x) does not; a well-specified model stays valid although inputs look different, and an input alarm does not by itself justify retraining. Concept drift: P(y | x) changes (customer behaviour, regulation, fraud tactics), inputs may look unchanged and the model is wrong; only outcomes reveal it. Prior (label) drift: P(y) changes, ranking may be preserved but calibration is lost, which a recalibration step can fix cheaply. Because outcomes arrive late (a default takes a year to observe), label-free proxies, the PSI of inputs and of the score, are used as early warnings, with the understanding that they cannot see concept drift. Good governance defines both layers of monitoring, the outcome lag, the diagnosis of drift type and the response matched to it (recalibrate, refit, redevelop).

covariate drift: P(x) ≠, P(y|x) = · concept drift: P(y|x) ≠ · prior drift: P(y) ≠ · label-free monitors cannot see concept drift

10Window size and the detection trade-off Expert

Monitoring a model’s error rate requires comparing recent cases with a baseline, and the length of the recent window is the design parameter. A window of w cases has a standard error √(p(1 − p)/w) on the observed rate, so a short window is noisy and generates false alarms, while a long window averages the change with old data and responds late. The set of achievable (false-alarm rate, detection delay) pairs is the monitor’s operating curve; the window moves a monitor along it. Methods that adapt the window (ADWIN), accumulate evidence (Page–Hinkley, CUSUM on the errors) or use richer signals than a 0/1 error (the score, a proper scoring rule, item 16) can move the curve itself. A drift smaller than a few standard errors cannot be detected quickly by any method, which should be stated with the monitor’s specification.

standard error of the windowed rate √(p(1 − p)/w) · delay ≈ w·(threshold ⁄ effect) for small windows · operating curve: false alarms against delay

11The cost of monitoring and setting thresholds Expert

A threshold is a decision, and decisions have costs. A false alarm costs analyst time or an unnecessary retrain; a late detection costs the loss incurred while the model is wrong; the base rate of real drift decides how many alarms will be false. With a CUSUM (limit h) the in-control run length ARL₀ grows roughly exponentially in h while the delay grows only linearly, so the cost-minimising h balances (1000/ARL₀)·cfa against (1000/T)·delay, where T is the average time between real drifts. Siegmund’s approximation gives ARL₀ and the delay in closed form for a normal stream. The conclusions are qualitative and robust: when false alarms are expensive or drift is rare, use a higher limit; when drift is frequent or damaging, a lower one. Record the costs and base rates that justified the thresholds in the model documentation (SR 11-7 style), and revisit them when the model’s importance or the data change.

ARL ≈ [e^(−2Δb) + 2Δb − 1]/(2Δ²), b = h + 1.166, Δ = μ − k · cost = (1000/ARL₀)·c_fa + (1000/T)·delay
