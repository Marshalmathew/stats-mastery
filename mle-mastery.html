Maximum Likelihood

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Maximum likelihood estimation.

Maximum likelihood (ML) is the general recipe behind logistic regression, Poisson and survival models, GARCH, mixed models and most of what you fit in practice. Write down a probability model, ask which parameter value makes the observed data most probable, and then use the curvature of that peak to say how uncertain you are. This page builds the idea from the likelihood function itself, derives the standard error from Fisher information, shows how to compute and test with it, and then breaks it on purpose: multimodal likelihoods, perfect separation, overdispersed counts and censored data.

Part I · The likelihood

1The likelihood function Beginner

A probability model gives the chance of any data for a given parameter: P(data | θ). Once the data are observed, flip your point of view: the data are fixed, and θ varies. The result, viewed as a function of θ, is the likelihood:

L(θ) = P(observed data | θ) for iid data: L(θ) = Π f(xᵢ; θ)

The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the θ that maximises L(θ), the parameter value under which the data you saw were least surprising. For k successes in n trials, L(p) = C(n,k) pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ and the peak is at p̂ = k/n.

A likelihood is not a probability distribution over θIt does not integrate to 1 (for the binomial, ∫L(p)dp = 1/(n+1)), and it says nothing about how probable each θ is. It compares θ values against each other using the same data. Turning it into a distribution requires a prior (Bayes page).

2Log-likelihood, the score and the MLE Beginner

Products are awkward, sums are easy. Work with the log-likelihood ℓ(θ) = Σ log f(xᵢ; θ); it has the same maximiser. Its slope is the score S(θ) = ℓ′(θ), and the MLE solves S(θ̂) = 0. Its curvature, ℓ″, tells you how sharply peaked the likelihood is.

InvarianceIf θ̂ is the MLE of θ, then g(θ̂) is the MLE of g(θ) for any function g. The MLE of an odds p/(1−p) is p̂/(1−p̂); the MLE of a mean lifetime 1/λ is 1/λ̂. (This is convenient, and the source of the small-sample bias you saw in the Sampling Distributions page.)

3Fisher information and standard errors Intermediate

How uncertain is the MLE? A sharply peaked log-likelihood pins the parameter down; a flat one does not. Fisher information measures the expected sharpness of one observation:

I(θ) = −E[ ∂² log f(X; θ) / ∂θ² ] = Var(score) SE(θ̂) ≈ 1 / √( n · I(θ̂) )

This is the Cramér–Rao bound from the previous page: no unbiased estimator can have a smaller variance than 1/(nI(θ)), and the MLE reaches it in large samples. The observed information −ℓ″(θ̂) is the actual curvature of your data's log-likelihood and is what software reports; the two agree as n grows.

4Confidence intervals: Wald, likelihood-ratio and score Advanced

Large-sample theory says θ̂ is approximately N(θ, 1/(nI)). There are three standard ways to turn that into an interval, and they differ greatly in small samples.

MethodIntervalCharacter
Waldθ̂ ± 1.96·SE(θ̂)Simplest; symmetric; not invariant to reparameterisation; poor near boundaries (collapses at p̂ = 0)
Likelihood ratio{θ : 2[ℓ(θ̂) − ℓ(θ)] ≤ 3.84}Uses the true shape of the log-likelihood; invariant; usually the best behaved; asymmetric

Coverage is not a single number. For a discrete model it is a function of the true parameter and jumps up and down as p moves, because the interval endpoints are tied to the integer k. Lab 4 computes that function exactly (no simulation noise).

Part II · Computing the MLE

5Newton–Raphson and when it fails Advanced

For most models S(θ) = 0 has no closed form, so the MLE is found numerically. The workhorse is Newton–Raphson: approximate ℓ by a parabola at the current point and jump to its peak.

θ(new) = θ(old) − S(θ(old)) / ℓ″(θ(old))

When ℓ is concave and you start near the peak, convergence is very fast (the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step). Replacing ℓ″ by its expectation, −nI(θ), gives Fisher scoring, which is what GLM software (IRLS) uses. Two things can go wrong: the likelihood can have several peaks (Newton finds whichever is nearest), and a step can leave the parameter space (a negative rate).

The curve is the log-likelihood (relative to its maximum). Dots are the Newton iterates from your starting value. The exponential likelihood is well behaved but a bad start can overshoot; the Cauchy-location likelihood has several local peaks.

Practical ruleAlways start from a sensible value (the sample median for a Cauchy location, 1/x̄ for a rate), try several starts for a likelihood that may be multimodal, and check the optimiser's convergence flag and the Hessian at the answer. Standard software silently returns local optima.

6Logistic regression is maximum likelihood Advanced

For a binary outcome with P(y = 1 | x) = σ(β₀ + β₁x), the log-likelihood is ℓ(β) = Σ [ yᵢ log pᵢ + (1 − yᵢ) log(1 − pᵢ) ]. It is concave, and Newton–Raphson (equivalently IRLS) gives the fit. The covariance of β̂ is the inverse of the observed information matrix, whose entries are Σ wᵢ xᵢxⱼ with weights wᵢ = pᵢ(1 − pᵢ).

The weights are largest where pᵢ is near ½ and tiny in the tails, so rare events carry little information per row: with few events the slope's standard error is large however many non-events you have. This is why “10 events per variable” rules of thumb exist.

7What the MLE guarantees, and what it does not Advanced

Under regularity conditions the MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal with variance 1/(nI(θ)), asymptotically efficient (it attains the Cramér–Rao bound) and invariant to reparameterisation. All of these are statements about large n. In small samples the MLE can be biased (typically by an amount of order 1/n, while its SE is of order 1/√n) and its distribution can be skewed.

A good example is the shape α of a gamma distribution (used for loss severities and waiting times). The MLE solves log α − ψ(α) = log x̄ − mean(log xᵢ), where ψ is the digamma function; it has no closed form but is easy to find numerically. Its asymptotic variance is α / (n(αψ′(α) − 1)).

Part III · Testing and trouble

8The likelihood-ratio test and Wilks' theorem Advanced

To test a restriction (H₀: θ = θ₀, or a smaller nested model) compare the maximised log-likelihoods:

Λ = 2 [ ℓ(θ̂) − ℓ(θ̂₀) ] → χ²_q under H₀ q = number of restrictions

This is Wilks' theorem. It works for any model with a likelihood, which is why it underlies deviance tests in GLMs, nested-model comparisons and AIC (AIC = −2ℓ̂ + 2·(number of parameters)). It needs large n and regularity conditions; the parameter must not sit on the boundary under H₀.

9When the model is wrong: quasi-likelihood and the sandwich Expert

ML assumes the model is right. If it is only approximately right, the estimate often still converges to the best-fitting parameter, but the standard errors computed from the information matrix can be badly wrong. The fix is the sandwich (robust) estimator:

Var(θ̂) ≈ A⁻¹ B A⁻¹ A = −ℓ″ (the “bread”), B = Σ (scoreᵢ)² (the “meat”)

If the model is correct, A = B and it collapses to the usual 1/(nI). Classic case: modelling counts as Poisson (variance = mean) when they are overdispersed (variance = φ · mean, φ > 1). The mean λ̂ = x̄ is still consistent, but the model-based SE √(x̄/n) is too small by a factor √φ.

Practical consequenceIn fraud and claims counts, overdispersion is the norm (clustered events, heterogeneous accounts). Use robust standard errors, a negative binomial model, or at least check the Pearson dispersion statistic. Model-based p-values from a Poisson fit are usually far too small.

10Censoring: when the likelihood is the right tool Expert

Time-to-default, churn and recovery data are right-censored: for accounts that have not yet defaulted you only know that the time exceeds the observation window. The likelihood handles this naturally. An observed event at time t contributes the density f(t); a censored observation contributes the survival probability S(c) = P(T > c). For an exponential lifetime:

ℓ(λ) = d · log λ − λ Σ tᵢ λ̂ = d / Σ tᵢ = (number of events) / (total exposure)

Two tempting shortcuts are both wrong: treating censored times as event times, and dropping the censored accounts. Each throws away or misreads exactly the information that the account survived.

11When the MLE does not exist: separation and penalised likelihood Expert

If a predictor perfectly separates the two classes (every event has x above some value and every non-event below it), the logistic log-likelihood keeps increasing as the slope grows: the maximum is at infinity. Software typically reports a huge coefficient with an enormous standard error or a convergence warning. It happens routinely with rare events, small samples, or a categorical level with no events.

The cure is to add a penalty, which makes the maximiser finite: maximise ℓ(β) − (λ/2)β₁². This is ridge-penalised likelihood, which equals MAP estimation under a Gaussian prior on the slope (Bayes page), and it is also a bias–variance trade-off (Sampling Distributions, Chapter 9): it biases the slope toward 0 in return for a finite, stable estimate. Firth's bias-reduced logistic regression is another standard fix.
