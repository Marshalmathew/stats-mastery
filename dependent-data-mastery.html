Dependent Data

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Dependent data: time series, panels, clusters and mixed models.

Most of the methods so far assumed independent observations. Monthly loss rates, daily returns, several loans per customer, branches with their own habits, the same account observed every quarter: none of these are independent, and treating them as such gives standard errors that are too small, p-values that are too good and models that fail out of sample. This page covers the two sources of dependence, time and grouping: autocorrelation, ARMA models, robust inference, spurious regression and cointegration, forecast evaluation, clustered errors, fixed and random effects, mixed models and shrinkage, clustered binary outcomes, volatility clustering and structural breaks.

Part I · Time series

1Why dependence breaks the usual formulas Beginner

The standard error of a mean, s/√n, assumes the n observations carry n independent pieces of information. If neighbouring observations are positively correlated, each new one repeats part of what you already know. For a stationary series with autocorrelation ρₖ at lag k, the variance of the mean is σ²/n × (1 + 2Σ(1 − k/n)ρₖ). For an AR(1) series with ρ the factor is about (1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ), so the effective sample size is n_eff ≈ n(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ): with ρ = 0.7, 200 observations are worth about 35 independent ones.

Var(ȳ) ≈ (σ²/n) · (1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ) n_eff = n · (1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ)

The same happens for grouped data (chapter 6). The consequences: confidence intervals too narrow, tests that reject too often, and apparent significance of trends that are only slow-moving noise. Negative autocorrelation does the opposite (effective n above n), which is rare in practice but occurs in over-differenced series.

2Stationarity, ACF, PACF and ARMA models Intermediate

A series is (weakly) stationary if its mean and variance are constant and the covariance between y_t and y_{t+k} depends only on the lag k. Dependence is summarised by the autocorrelation function ACF(k) = Corr(y_t, y_{t+k}) and the partial autocorrelation function PACF(k), the correlation at lag k after removing the effect of the lags in between. An AR(p) model, y_t = φ₁y_{t−1} + … + φ_p y_{t−p} + e_t, has an ACF that decays and a PACF that cuts off after lag p. An MA(q) model, y_t = e_t + θ₁e_{t−1} + … + θ_q e_{t−q}, has an ACF that cuts off after lag q and a PACF that decays. ARMA combines both and both functions decay. Stationarity of an AR model needs the roots of 1 − φ₁z − … − φ_p z^p to lie outside the unit circle; for AR(1), |φ| < 1.

A random walk y_t = y_{t−1} + e_t is not stationary: its variance grows with t and its sample ACF decays very slowly, which is the usual sign that the series needs differencing. In samples the bars of the ACF have a standard error of about 1/√n, so values within ±1.96/√n are not distinguishable from zero. Identification by eye is a starting point; compare candidate models by AIC/BIC and check that the residuals show no autocorrelation (Ljung–Box).

3Regression with autocorrelated errors: HAC standard errors Intermediate

In y_t = α + βx_t + u_t with autocorrelated errors, OLS still estimates β without bias (if x is exogenous), but the usual standard error is wrong when both the regressor and the error are autocorrelated: the variance of Σx̃_t u_t includes the cross-products x̃_t u_t x̃_{t−k} u_{t−k}. The Newey–West (HAC) estimator adds them with Bartlett weights:

Ŝ = Σ x̃ₜ² eₜ² + 2 Σ_{l=1..L} (1 − l/(L+1)) Σₜ x̃ₜ eₜ x̃ₜ₋ₗ eₜ₋ₗ SE(β̂) = √Ŝ / Σ x̃ₜ²

The lag truncation L must grow with n (a common rule is L = ⌊4(n/100)^{2/9}⌋); too small and the standard error stays too small, too large and it becomes noisy. HAC repairs the standard errors but not the efficiency; modelling the errors (ARMA errors, adding lagged y, or feasible GLS) can give a more precise estimate. If x is only correlated with past values of y (lagged dependent variable models with autocorrelated errors), OLS itself is biased, a different problem. Also remember that if the errors are autocorrelated because a variable is missing or the dynamics are wrong, fixing the standard error does not fix the model.

4Spurious regression, unit roots and cointegration Advanced

Regressing one random walk on another, completely unrelated one gives a “significant” slope most of the time, a high R² and residuals with a Durbin–Watson statistic near zero: the spurious regression of Granger and Newbold. The t-statistic does not follow its usual distribution because both series have stochastic trends; its spread grows with n, so more data makes the false positives worse. The same happens with any strongly persistent series (AR with ρ near 1), and with two series that merely trend. Rules of thumb: R² larger than the Durbin–Watson statistic is a warning, and a regression of levels needs a stationarity argument.

Remedies: test for a unit root (Dickey–Fuller: regress Δy_t on y_{t−1} and compare the t-statistic with non-standard critical values, about −2.86 with a constant at 5%); if the series is integrated of order one, difference it (returns instead of prices, changes instead of levels). There is one important exception: if two integrated series share a stochastic trend so that a linear combination y − βx is stationary, they are cointegrated. Then the level regression is not spurious (it is super-consistent), the residual is stationary (Engle–Granger test, with critical values about −3.3 for two variables), and the right model is an error-correction model that combines short-run changes with the correction of the gap. Differencing a cointegrated pair throws the long-run relation away.

5Forecasting and how to evaluate it Advanced

A forecast is judged on future data, so validation must respect time: train on the past, test on what comes after (a walk-forward or rolling-origin evaluation: move the forecast origin forward and repeat). Random k-fold cross-validation mixes the future into the training set, lets the model interpolate between neighbours, and gives optimistic errors when the data are autocorrelated. Always compare with benchmarks: the naive forecast (last value), the seasonal naive (the value one season ago) and the historical mean. A sophisticated model that cannot beat them is not adding value; the mean absolute scaled error (MASE) divides by the in-sample naive error so that series can be compared.

The error grows with the horizon. For a stationary series the forecast tends to the mean and the error to the unconditional SD; for a random walk the error grows without bound like √h. Which benchmark wins depends on the process: persistence favours the naive forecast at short horizons, mean reversion favours the mean at long horizons, seasonality favours the seasonal naive. Give prediction intervals, not just point forecasts, and evaluate them (coverage). In credit and finance, add leakage checks: features must be known at the forecast origin (macro series are often revised).

Part II · Clusters, panels and mixed models

6Clustered data: intra-class correlation and the design effect Advanced

Observations nested in groups (loans within customers, customers within branches, repeated measures within accounts) share group characteristics. The intra-class correlation ICC = σ²_between / (σ²_between + σ²_within) measures the share of variance that sits between groups. With clusters of size m the variance of a mean inflates by the design effect 1 + (m − 1)·ICC, so a sample of 1000 loans from 100 customers with ICC 0.5 is worth about 1000/5.5 ≈ 180 independent loans. For a regression slope the inflation depends on how the regressor varies: if x is constant within clusters (a branch-level policy), the whole design effect applies; if x varies only within clusters and the cluster effect is absorbed in the error, the usual standard error can even be too large.

Cluster-robust (sandwich) standard errors sum the score x̃ᵢeᵢ within each cluster before squaring, so arbitrary correlation inside clusters is allowed: V = (Σx̃²)⁻² Σ_g (Σ_{i∈g} x̃ᵢeᵢ)². They need many clusters (a rule of thumb is 30–50; with few clusters use t with G − 1 degrees of freedom, wild-cluster bootstrap or a mixed model), and they cluster at the level where the dependence or the treatment assignment lives.

7Panels: pooled, fixed effects and random effects Advanced

In a panel (N units observed T times) the model y_it = βx_it + α_i + e_it has a unit effect α_i. Pooled OLS ignores it and is biased if α_i is correlated with x. The fixed-effects (within) estimator removes α_i by demeaning each unit (or adding unit dummies): it is consistent whatever the correlation with x, but it cannot estimate the effect of variables that do not change within a unit and uses only within-unit variation. The random-effects estimator treats α_i as a random draw uncorrelated with x and applies quasi-demeaning with weight θ = 1 − √(σ²_e / (σ²_e + Tσ²_α)): it uses both within and between variation, so it is more efficient, but it is biased if α_i is correlated with x. θ = 1 gives fixed effects, θ = 0 pooled OLS.

The choice is a trade-off between bias and variance, informed by the Hausman test (FE and RE differ significantly: use FE) and, more importantly, by the question of whether unobserved unit characteristics plausibly correlate with the regressors (usually yes for observational data: customer quality correlates with product use). The between estimator, the correlated random-effects (Mundlak) model that adds unit means of x, and clustering of the standard errors by unit complete the toolbox.

8Linear mixed models and shrinkage Expert

A linear mixed model y_ij = x_ij′β + u_j + e_ij, with u_j ~ N(0, σ²_u) and e_ij ~ N(0, σ²_e), combines population-level (fixed) effects β with group-level random effects. Random slopes (u_j multiplying a covariate) let each group have its own relation. Estimation is by maximum likelihood or REML, which corrects the small-sample downward bias of the variance components. The group effects are predicted by the BLUP (best linear unbiased predictor), which for a random intercept is a shrunken group mean:

û_j = λ_j (ȳ_j − μ̂), λ_j = σ²_u / (σ²_u + σ²_e / n_j)

Groups with few observations or large noise are pulled strongly towards the overall mean (λ small); large groups keep their own mean. This partial pooling sits between no pooling (each group on its own: unbiased but noisy for small groups) and complete pooling (ignore groups: stable but ignores real differences), and beats both in mean squared error when groups are of different sizes. It is the same logic as empirical Bayes and credibility theory, and the reason hierarchical models are used for branch, product and segment effects with thin data. Random-effect variances can be estimated even with few groups, but their uncertainty is large; with fewer than about 5 groups treat the group as fixed.

9Clustered binary outcomes: conditional versus marginal effects Expert

For a binary outcome (default, churn) with a random intercept, logit P(y_ij = 1) = α + βx_ij + u_j, there are two different questions. The conditional (cluster-specific) effect β compares two customers in the same cluster. The marginal (population-averaged) effect, what a logistic regression that ignores the clusters estimates, averages over the clusters and is attenuated towards zero because the logistic curve is flattened by averaging over u: β_marginal ≈ β / √(1 + 0.346 σ²_u). Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. A scorecard that ranks customers uses the marginal association; a model of a customer’s own risk change uses the conditional one. The two are equal only for linear models.

The two modelling routes are the generalised linear mixed model (GLMM, conditional, estimated by Laplace or quadrature) and generalised estimating equations (GEE, marginal, with a working correlation and sandwich standard errors). With pooled logistic regression the point estimate remains consistent for the marginal effect, but the usual standard errors can be too small (notably when covariates are constant within clusters) and should be cluster-robust. Also note that the usual coefficient interpretation (odds ratios) must say which one is reported.

Part III · Volatility and breaks

10Volatility clustering, GARCH and Value-at-Risk Expert

Financial returns are close to uncorrelated but their squares are not: calm and turbulent periods cluster. ARCH/GARCH models describe it: r_t = σ_t z_t with z_t ~ N(0,1) and σ²_t = ω + α r²_{t−1} + β σ²_{t−1}. The ARCH term α reacts to the latest shock, the GARCH term β carries past volatility, α + β measures persistence (the half-life of a shock is log 0.5 / log(α + β)), and the unconditional variance is ω/(1 − α − β). The unconditional distribution has heavy tails even if the conditional one is normal. EWMA (RiskMetrics) is the special case ω = 0 with λ = β = 0.94 and α = 1 − λ.

For risk, the question is whether the Value-at-Risk (a quantile of the loss) is exceeded at the stated rate (backtesting: 1% exceedances for 99% VaR, Kupiec test) and whether exceedances are independent (Christoffersen test). A constant-volatility VaR will be too low in turbulent periods and too high in calm ones: the average exceedance rate may look acceptable while exceedances come in bursts, which is when losses are most dangerous. Similar dynamics (clustered delinquency, regime-dependent PD) appear in credit portfolios.

11Structural breaks, regime changes and false persistence Expert

Relationships change: a regulation, a pandemic, a new product, a change in the definition of default. A structural break (a shift in the mean, a slope or the variance) invalidates models estimated across it. Ignoring a level shift makes a stationary series look like a unit root: the estimated AR coefficient is pulled towards 1 and the Dickey–Fuller test fails to reject, so the analyst differences a series that did not need it. Tests for breaks: the Chow test when the date is known (a dummy variable for the later period, with robust standard errors), the sup-F / Andrews and CUSUM tests when it is unknown (with larger critical values because the maximum over dates is taken), and recursive or rolling estimates for monitoring. Remedies: model the break (dummy, regime-switching, time-varying parameters), estimate on the post-break window, or re-train and monitor with population stability and back-testing in production (see the calibration and rare-events pages).
