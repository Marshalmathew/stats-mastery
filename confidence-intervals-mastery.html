Confidence Intervals

Confidence intervals, from meaning to practice.

A point estimate without an interval is a number without a unit. A confidence interval says how much that number could move if you drew another sample, and it is the form in which most statistics reaches a regulator, a CEO or an A/B-test dashboard. This page covers what “95%” actually promises, how intervals are built from a pivot, how to plan sample sizes, which interval to use for proportions (and why the textbook one fails for rare events), exact and transformed intervals, profile-likelihood and bootstrap intervals, and the traps: overlapping intervals, many intervals at once, and confusing a confidence interval with a prediction interval.

Part I · Meaning and construction

1What “95% confident” really means Beginner

A 95% confidence interval is produced by a procedure that, over repeated samples from the same population, captures the true parameter in 95% of the samples. The parameter is a fixed number; the interval is the random thing. Once you have computed [2.1, 3.4] from your data, that particular interval either contains the truth or it does not; the 95% describes the method's long-run success rate, not the probability for this interval.

The classic misreading“There is a 95% probability that μ lies in [2.1, 3.4]” is a credible interval statement and needs a prior (Bayes page). For a confidence interval, the right sentence is: “this method gives intervals that contain the truth 95% of the time.”

2Building an interval from a pivot: z and t Intermediate

The standard construction starts from a pivot: a function of the data and the parameter whose distribution does not depend on the parameter. For a normal mean with unknown σ the pivot is

T = (X̄ − μ) / (s/√n) ~ t with n − 1 degrees of freedom ⇒ X̄ ± t_{1−α/2, n−1} · s/√n

Invert the statement P(−t ≤ T ≤ t) = 1 − α for μ and the interval falls out. If you used the z value 1.96 instead of t, you would be pretending s = σ. That is harmless for large n and costly for small n, because s is itself noisy and sometimes small, giving an interval that is too narrow.

Lab 2 · What using z instead of t really costs

Exact coverage (no simulation) of the “z-interval” X̄ ± z·s/√n with normal data, against the sample size. The correct t-interval has coverage exactly equal to the nominal level at every n.

Duality with testsA 95% interval for θ is the set of θ₀ that a 5% two-sided test would not reject. Intervals therefore contain everything a p-value tells you, plus the size of the effect and its uncertainty. Prefer reporting the interval.

3Width and sample-size planning Intermediate

The half-width of an interval (the margin of error) is z·SE. Setting it equal to a target margin m and solving for n gives the planning formulas:

TargetRequired n
Mean (σ known or from a pilot)n = (zσ/m)²
Proportionn = z² p(1−p) / m² (p = ½ is the worst case)
Difference of two proportions (A/B test), n per groupn = 2 z² p(1−p) / m² (equal groups, similar p)

Two consequences are worth internalising. Margin falls like 1/√n, so halving it costs 4× the data. And the level matters: moving from 95% to 99% raises n by (2.576/1.96)² ≈ 1.73×.

Part II · Proportions and exact intervals

4Intervals for a proportion: six methods Advanced

For a binomial proportion there are at least six standard 95% intervals, and they are not equivalent.

MethodIdeaBehaviour
Waldp̂ ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n)Textbook default; poor for small n or p near 0/1; collapses at p̂ = 0
Wilsoninvert the score testGood coverage; excellent default
Agresti–CoullWald after adding z²/2 successes and failuresSimple, slightly conservative
Clopper–Pearsoninvert two exact binomial tails (Beta quantiles)“Exact”: coverage is at least nominal, typically too conservative
JeffreysBeta(½,½)-prior credible intervalNear-nominal on average; recommended
Likelihood ratio{p : 2[ℓ(p̂) − ℓ(p)] ≤ 3.84}Good, invariant, asymmetric

“Exact” does not mean “has exactly 95% coverage”. A discrete distribution cannot deliver that; the Clopper–Pearson interval guarantees coverage no less than 95%, and pays for the guarantee with wider intervals.

5Rare events and zero events Advanced

Fraud rates, default rates, breach rates: the interesting cases have very few events, sometimes none. If you see k = 0 events in n trials, the Wald interval is [0, 0] (certainty), which is absurd. The honest statement is an upper bound. The rule of three: with zero events in n independent trials the 95% one-sided upper bound on the rate is about 3/n, because (1 − p)ⁿ ≈ e^(−np) = 0.05 at np ≈ 3.

More generally, whenever the count is small, the intervals above disagree sharply, and the choice matters for a conclusion such as “the default rate is below 1%”.

6Transformed and exact intervals for other quantities Advanced

When an estimator's distribution is skewed or bounded, build the interval on a scale where it is closer to normal, then transform back. Three standard cases:

  • Poisson count k: the Wald interval k ± z√k is poor for small k. The exact (Garwood) interval uses χ² quantiles: [½χ²_{α/2, 2k}, ½χ²_{1−α/2, 2k+2}]. A cheap alternative is the square-root transform (√k ± z/2)².
  • Correlation r: r is bounded by ±1 and skewed. Use Fisher's z: atanh(r) is approximately normal with SE 1/√(n−3); form the interval there and apply tanh.
  • Standard deviation (normal data): (n−1)s²/σ² ~ χ²_{n−1} gives [s√((n−1)/χ²_{1−α/2}), s√((n−1)/χ²_{α/2})], which is asymmetric and never negative.

Part III · Likelihood-based and resampling intervals

7Profile-likelihood intervals Advanced

When a model has several parameters and you want an interval for one of them (the parameter of interest), maximise the likelihood over the others for each value of the parameter of interest. This gives the profile log-likelihood, and the interval is {θ : ℓ_p(θ̂) − ℓ_p(θ) ≤ 1.92}. It respects the shape of the likelihood (asymmetric, bounded) and is invariant to reparameterisation. The Wald interval θ̂ ± 1.96·SE assumes the log-likelihood is a parabola.

The shape α of a gamma distribution is a good test case: its sampling distribution is skewed for small n, so a symmetric Wald interval is mis-centred and can even dip below zero.

8Bootstrap intervals Advanced

When no formula exists, resample. Draw B bootstrap resamples (with replacement), compute the statistic for each, and build an interval from the bootstrap distribution θ̂*:

IntervalFormulaNote
Normalθ̂ ± z · SE_bootAssumes a symmetric bootstrap distribution
Percentile[θ̂*_{α/2}, θ̂*_{1−α/2}]Simple and transformation-invariant; biased if the distribution is skewed or θ̂ is biased
Basic (reverse percentile)[2θ̂ − θ̂*_{1−α/2}, 2θ̂ − θ̂*_{α/2}]Reflects the bootstrap distribution about the estimate
BCa, studentisedbias- and acceleration-corrected, or bootstrap-tSecond-order accurate; preferred in practice (library implementations)

Bootstrap intervals are still approximations: with small n and skewed data their coverage falls short of nominal, often by more than the t-interval's. Lab 8 measures it.

Part IV · Traps

9Overlapping intervals do not mean “no difference” Expert

A very common error is to compare two groups by checking whether their separate 95% intervals overlap. That is the wrong test. The intervals overlap whenever the difference d is below 1.96·(SE₁ + SE₂), but the difference is significant whenever d exceeds 1.96·√(SE₁² + SE₂²), which is a smaller number. Between the two thresholds the intervals overlap yet the difference is significant. With equal SEs the zone runs from 2.77 SE to 3.92 SE.

Lab 9 · Overlap vs significance

Two group means and the interval for their difference. Thick bars: 95% intervals; thin bars: 83.4% intervals (for equal SEs, non-overlap of these approximates a 5% test).

RuleAlways form the interval for the difference (or ratio) directly. Do not compare two intervals by eye.

10Many intervals at once Expert

Each 95% interval fails 5% of the time. If you report m independent intervals (one per segment, branch, feature or coefficient), the chance that all contain their truths is 0.95ᵐ: for m = 20 only 36%, and you expect one miss. To get 95% simultaneous coverage the Bonferroni correction uses level 1 − α/m for each interval (Šidák: 1 − (1−α)^{1/m}), at the cost of wider intervals. This is the interval counterpart of the multiple-testing problem in the p-value course.

11Confidence vs prediction intervals Expert

A confidence interval is about a parameter, such as the mean. A prediction interval is about a future observation. The latter must cover both the uncertainty in the estimated mean and the individual's own scatter:

CI for μ: x̄ ± t · s/√n PI for the next observation: x̄ ± t · s · √(1 + 1/n)

As n grows the CI shrinks to nothing, while the PI converges to ±1.96σ and never goes below it. “The model's average error is known to ±0.1” does not mean any single prediction is within ±0.1. For regulators and customers the PI is often the relevant statement. (A tolerance interval goes one step further: it covers a stated proportion of the whole population with stated confidence.)
