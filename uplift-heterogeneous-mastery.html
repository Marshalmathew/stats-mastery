Uplift & Heterogeneous Effects

12 chapters · 11 labs · exam
Mastery: 0%

Uplift and heterogeneous treatment effects: who should be treated, how to estimate it, and how to know it worked.

An average effect answers “does the campaign work?”. A bank usually needs a different question: for whom does it work, and where does it do harm? A retention call helps customers who would otherwise leave, wastes money on those who would stay anyway, and annoys some into leaving. Predicting who will respond is not the same as predicting who will respond because of the treatment. This page builds the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) and its estimators (subgroups, meta-learners, honest causal trees, doubly robust learners), the evaluation tools that replace accuracy (Qini and uplift curves, calibration of heterogeneity), the decision layer (targeting, budgets, off-policy evaluation) and the practice issues of pilots, selection noise and fairness constraints. As before, every lab simulates data with a known truth.

Part I · Concepts and estimators

1Why the average hides the decision Beginner

The conditional average treatment effect is τ(x) = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | X = x]; uplift is the same quantity for a binary outcome (the change in conversion probability caused by the treatment). Marketing practice sorts customers into four types by their behaviour with and without the treatment: persuadables (respond only if treated: positive uplift), sure things (convert anyway: zero uplift, treatment is waste), lost causes (never convert: zero uplift) and sleeping dogs (react badly to contact: negative uplift). An average effect near zero can hide a large positive effect in one segment and a negative effect in another; a campaign is worth running only on customers whose uplift exceeds the cost of treating them.

The central confusion is between response and uplift. A model that predicts who will convert ranks sure things first and will happily target them; a model that predicts the effect ranks persuadables first. The value of a policy is Σ over treated customers of (value × uplift − cost), which depends only on τ(x), not on the baseline.

Lab 1 · The four types and what each policy earns

Persuadables: conversion 5% → 25%. Sure things: 50% → 50%. Lost causes: 5% → 5%. Sleeping dogs: 35% → 15%. A conversion is worth 100; contacting costs c. The remaining customers are split evenly between sure things and lost causes. Bars: net value per 1000 customers of contacting nobody, everybody, the 30% with the highest baseline response (a response model) and only those with positive net uplift (an uplift model).

2Estimating the CATE from an experiment: subgroups and interactions Intermediate

With randomised treatment, the effect in any subgroup defined by pre-treatment X is estimated by the difference in means within it, and a linear model Y = α + βT + γ'X + δ'(T·X) + ε gives a smooth CATE τ̂(x) = β + δ'x. The trade-off is the usual one: fine subgroups have little bias but each has a small sample (standard error ≈ σ√(4/n_g)), so the estimated effects are noisy, and the most extreme estimated subgroups are the most overestimated (the winner’s curse of the multiple-testing page). A constant ATE estimate has no variance from heterogeneity but is biased for every individual; a parametric interaction model borrows strength across x. When true heterogeneity is modest, shrinkage towards a common effect beats unrestricted subgroup estimates in mean-squared error.

3Meta-learners: S, T and X Intermediate

A meta-learner turns any regression method into a CATE estimator. The S-learner fits one model μ(x, T) with treatment as a feature and takes τ̂(x) = μ(x, 1) − μ(x, 0); flexible learners may shrink or ignore T, so heterogeneity is under-detected (in the lab, an additive treatment term cannot represent any). The T-learner fits μ₁(x) on treated and μ₀(x) on controls and subtracts them; it captures any heterogeneity but doubles the variance and is poor when one arm is small. The X-learner (Künzel et al.) imputes individual effects, D¹ = Y − μ̂₀(x) for treated and D⁰ = μ̂₁(x) − Y for controls, regresses each on x, and combines them weighted by the propensity: it borrows information from the large arm and is the usual choice with unbalanced treatment. Other options: the R-learner and DR-learner (chapter 7) and causal forests.

4Uplift trees, causal forests and honesty Advanced

A causal (uplift) tree splits the covariate space to maximise the difference in treatment effect between the children, not the difference in outcome. Trained and evaluated on the same data it overfits in a specific way: the split is chosen where the estimated effects happen to differ most, so the effect estimates in the leaves are biased away from the truth (a selection effect). Honest estimation (Athey and Imbens) splits the sample: one part chooses the partition, the other estimates the effect inside the leaves, which makes the leaf estimates unbiased with valid intervals. Causal forests average many honest trees grown on subsamples; they adapt to the data while giving pointwise confidence intervals and variable importance. In all cases the number of candidate splits (features × thresholds) is a multiple-comparisons problem: more noise features means more spurious heterogeneity.

Part II · Evaluation and honesty

5Evaluating uplift models: Qini and uplift curves Advanced

There is no ground truth for an individual’s uplift, so accuracy metrics (AUC, RMSE) do not apply. Evaluation uses a randomised test set and the idea of cumulative gain. Rank customers by predicted uplift, and for each top fraction φ compute the incremental outcome: the treated group’s outcome minus the control group’s outcome rescaled to the treated group’s size. The Qini curve plots this cumulative gain against φ; a random ranking gives a straight line from 0 to the overall effect, a good model bends above it, and the Qini coefficient or AUUC is the area between the curve and the random line. The uplift curve is the same idea in rates. A model ranked by baseline response scores near the random line (or below it) however accurate it is as a response model; this is the test that exposes it.

6Choosing who to treat: budgets, break-even and the winner’s curse Advanced

Given predicted uplift û(x), the profit-maximising rule treats a customer if V·û(x) > c (V the value of a conversion, c the cost of contact); with a budget, treat the top k. The profit curve rises while the marginal customer’s uplift exceeds c/V, peaks, and then falls as low- and negative-uplift customers are added. Two cautions. First, û is noisy: ranking by a noisy estimate and then looking at the selected customers’ predicted uplift overstates their true uplift (selection on noise, the same winner’s curse as for subgroups), so the predicted profit curve is too optimistic and the optimal cut-off is too generous. Second, reporting predicted uplift for the selected group needs shrinkage, a held-out randomised check or an honest estimate, not the model’s own output.

7Observational data: doubly robust learners and orthogonality Expert

When treatment was not randomised (past campaigns targeted by rules), CATE estimation needs the ignorability and overlap assumptions of the previous page, and the meta-learners inherit all their problems: a T-learner with misspecified outcome models carries confounding bias into τ̂(x). The DR-learner builds a pseudo-outcome that is unbiased for the effect if either the outcome models or the propensity model is right,

ψᵢ = μ̂₁(Xᵢ) − μ̂₀(Xᵢ) + Tᵢ(Yᵢ − μ̂₁(Xᵢ))/ê(Xᵢ) − (1 − Tᵢ)(Yᵢ − μ̂₀(Xᵢ))/(1 − ê(Xᵢ)), τ̂(x) = regression of ψ on X

and then regresses ψ on X with any learner. The R-learner (Nie and Wager) instead minimises a loss built from residuals of Y and T on X, and double/debiased machine learning uses cross-fitting so that flexible nuisance models do not contaminate τ̂. The common idea is Neyman orthogonality: the estimating equation is insensitive to small errors in the nuisance functions, so slow-converging ML models for μ and e still allow a fast, well-behaved τ̂. None of this repairs an unmeasured confounder.

8Is there heterogeneity at all? Calibration tests and subgroup hunts Expert

Before acting on a CATE model, test whether it carries real signal. The best linear predictor (BLP) test of Chernozhukov, Demirer, Duflo and Fernández-Val uses a held-out sample: with τ̂ trained on one half, regress the held-out outcome on T, the centred prediction d = τ̂(X) − mean(τ̂) and their product T·d. The coefficient on T·d is 1 if the predictions are well calibrated and 0 if they contain no heterogeneity signal; a t-test on it is a single pre-planned test of heterogeneity. The alternative, searching subgroups until one has a significant interaction, inflates the false positive rate: with eight candidate splits and no true heterogeneity, about one experiment in three “finds” a subgroup. Pre-specify subgroups, use one omnibus test, adjust for multiplicity, and confirm on new data.

Part III · Decisions and practice

9Evaluating a targeting policy: off-policy evaluation Expert

A policy π(x) says whom to treat. Its value V(π) = E[Y(π(X))] can be estimated from logged data collected under another (randomised or known) assignment rule, without deploying π. Three estimators: the direct method plugs in outcome models, V̂ = mean[μ̂(X, π(X))], and is biased when they are wrong; inverse-propensity weighting keeps only the customers whose logged treatment agrees with the policy, V̂ = mean[Y·1{T = π(X)} / P(T | X)], and is unbiased but noisy, especially when the logging probability of an action the policy wants is small; the doubly robust estimator adds the IPW correction to the model prediction and is consistent if either is right, with lower variance. Logging with some randomisation over all actions (“exploration”) is what makes this possible.

10From pilot to deployment: the value of experimenting Expert

An uplift model needs randomised data, and a pilot has a cost (customers treated or untreated for the wrong reasons) and a size. The question is how large a pilot pays for itself. Too small and the learned policy is noise; too large and the learning cost exceeds the gain. The deployed policy’s profit grows with pilot size towards the oracle and flattens: the useful size depends on the heterogeneity (large, easy-to-find differences need little data), the noise in the outcome, and the margin between benefit and contact cost. Practical designs: a permanent holdout group that is never contacted (so effects can be re-estimated and drift detected), exploration bands (random treatment for a share of customers), and phased roll-outs with sequential or adaptive allocation (chapter 11 of the experiments page).

11Constraints, fairness and governance of targeting Expert

Targeting by uplift allocates offers, reminders or retention effort unevenly, and the uneven allocation can correlate with protected or sensitive characteristics even when they are not used (through proxies). Regulators and customers care about differential treatment, and for credit-related actions, fair-lending and non-discrimination duties apply. A parity constraint (equal treatment rates across groups) is one option; it costs value, and the cost is the price of fairness, which should be measured and reported rather than argued. Other tools: equal-opportunity or calibration-within-group requirements, constraining by need rather than by predicted uplift, and monitoring outcomes by group after deployment. Governance for an uplift model: documented causal assumptions and the experiment behind the effect estimates, a holdout kept for monitoring, drift checks on the effect (not only on the score), human override rules, and model-risk validation of the CATE estimator including its calibration (chapter 8).
