Generalised linear models.

Linear regression assumes a continuous outcome with constant variance. Defaults, churn, fraud, claims and event counts are none of those. A generalised linear model keeps the linear predictor but changes two things: the distribution of the outcome (binomial, Poisson, gamma) and the link that connects the mean to the predictor. This page covers logistic regression end to end (fitting, interpretation, testing, separation, classification metrics), count models with exposures and overdispersion, deviance and likelihood-ratio tests, the choice of link, and what rare-event sampling does to a fitted model.

Part I · Logistic regression

1Why a straight line fails for a 0/1 outcome Beginner

For a binary outcome the mean of y is a probability, p(x) = P(y = 1 | x), and it must lie in [0, 1]. A straight line does not respect that: fitted to binary data (the linear probability model) it produces probabilities below 0 and above 1 and its errors cannot have constant variance, since Var(y | x) = p(1 − p). The GLM fix is to model a transformation of the mean. In logistic regression the transformation is the log-odds:

logit(p) = log( p / (1 − p) ) = β₀ + β₁x₁ + … + β_k x_k p = 1 / (1 + e^−(β₀ + β₁x₁ + …))

The S-shaped curve is steep in the middle and flat in the tails, which is how real probabilities behave: a unit of the predictor matters a lot near p = 0.5 and little near p = 0.01. (The linear probability model is still used for quick estimates of average effects, where its slope is close to the average marginal effect, but never for prediction.)

2The GLM recipe and how it is fitted Intermediate

A GLM has three parts: a distribution from the exponential family for y (binomial, Poisson, Gaussian, gamma…), a linear predictor η = Xβ, and a link g with g(E[y]) = η. Maximum likelihood has no closed form (except for the Gaussian case), but a Newton–Raphson step has a neat form: iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS). At each iteration compute the working weights wᵢ = (dμᵢ/dηᵢ)² / Var(μᵢ) and the working response zᵢ = ηᵢ + (yᵢ − μᵢ)/(dμᵢ/dηᵢ), then solve the weighted least squares problem β ← (XᵀWX)⁻¹XᵀWz. For the canonical link (logit for binomial, log for Poisson) this is exactly Newton’s method on the log-likelihood, which is concave, so it converges to the unique maximum, usually in 4 to 7 steps. The inverse (XᵀWX)⁻¹ at the end is the covariance matrix of β̂ (Fisher information), giving Wald standard errors.

3Interpreting coefficients: log-odds, odds ratios, probabilities Intermediate

A logistic coefficient β is the change in the log-odds per unit of x, so e^β is a constant odds ratio (OR). The odds ratio is the same everywhere, but the effect on the probability is not: the change in p is β·p(1 − p), largest at p = 0.5 and tiny in the tails. Reporting an OR of 1.8 says nothing about how many percentage points of risk it moves unless the baseline is stated. The odds ratio also approximates the risk ratio only when the event is rare (p below roughly 10%); for common outcomes it exaggerates. For summaries on the probability scale use average marginal effects: the mean over the observed data of the change in predicted probability.

4Wald, likelihood-ratio and score tests Advanced

There are three large-sample tests of H₀: β = β₀, all asymptotically χ²₁. The Wald test uses the estimate and its standard error, z = (β̂ − β₀)/SE, and needs only the full fit. The likelihood-ratio (LR) test compares the maximised log-likelihoods, 2(ℓ̂ − ℓ̂₀), and needs both fits. The score test needs only the null fit: it looks at the slope of the log-likelihood at β₀. They agree for large samples but differ when the sample is small, the event rare or the effect large. The Wald test is the least reliable (its standard error comes from the curvature at the estimate, which can be misleading); the LR test is usually the best, and is what you should use for important decisions and for CIs (profile likelihood).

5Separation, small-sample bias and Firth’s fix Expert

When a predictor perfectly (or almost perfectly) splits the outcomes, the likelihood has no maximum: the coefficient runs off to ±∞ and the Wald standard error explodes, so the software reports a huge coefficient with p ≈ 1. This separation is common with few events, rare categories and many dummies. Even without separation the MLE is biased away from zero in small samples (by about a 1/n term, important at fewer than 10 events per variable). Firth’s penalised likelihood adds a Jeffreys-prior term that removes the leading bias and always gives finite estimates; ridge-type penalties or Bayesian priors serve the same purpose.

6Classification: ROC, calibration and base rates Advanced

A logistic model outputs probabilities; a decision needs a threshold. At a threshold t, sensitivity (recall) is the share of true events flagged and specificity the share of non-events passed; the ROC curve traces both over all thresholds, and the AUC is the probability that a random event scores higher than a random non-event (0.5 = coin flip). AUC is blind to the base rate; precision (the share of flagged cases that are real) is not. With a 1% event rate even a strong model flags mostly false positives, which is why fraud and AML teams talk about alert precision. Separately, a model can rank well (high AUC) and still be miscalibrated, with predicted probabilities that do not match observed frequencies; calibration matters whenever the probability itself is used (pricing, capital, expected loss).

Part II · Counts and other families

7Poisson regression, rates and exposure Intermediate

For counts (claims, defaults, alerts, visits) the Poisson model uses the log link: log E[y] = β₀ + β₁x, so e^β is a rate ratio. When units are observed for different lengths of time or amounts of exposure E, the mean count is E times the rate, and the model needs log E as an offset, a term with its coefficient fixed at 1: log E[y] = log E + β₀ + β₁x. Leaving it out misreads exposure as rate; if exposure is related to a predictor, the coefficient is biased as well. Fitting least squares to raw counts ignores the skew and the variance that grows with the mean.

Lab 7 · The offset matters

8Overdispersion: quasi-Poisson, robust and negative binomial Advanced

A Poisson variable has variance equal to its mean. Real counts nearly always vary more (unobserved heterogeneity, clustering), which is overdispersion. The Poisson coefficients stay consistent, but the standard errors are too small, p-values too optimistic, and CIs undercover. Diagnose it with the Pearson statistic divided by its degrees of freedom (φ̂ ≈ 1 if the model is right). Remedies: quasi-Poisson (multiply the SEs by √φ̂), sandwich (robust) standard errors, or a negative binomial model, which adds a variance term Var = μ + αμ² and gives a proper likelihood (so AIC and LR tests still work). Zero-inflated and hurdle models are for an excess of zeros specifically.

Lab 8 · How wrong are Poisson standard errors?

9Deviance, likelihood-ratio tests and goodness of fit Advanced

The deviance is twice the log-likelihood gap between the fitted model and a saturated model that fits each observation perfectly: D = 2(ℓ_sat − ℓ̂). For nested models the drop in deviance, D₀ − D₁, is the LR statistic and is χ² with degrees of freedom equal to the number of added parameters. It is the GLM analogue of the F-test, and the tool for deciding whether a term (a square, an interaction, a whole group of dummies) is needed. AIC = −2ℓ̂ + 2p trades fit for complexity, and McFadden’s pseudo-R² = 1 − ℓ̂/ℓ̂₀ is the likelihood-based analogue of R² (values of 0.2 to 0.4 are considered excellent; it is not comparable to OLS R²). For binary data the deviance does not follow a χ² distribution by itself, so goodness-of-fit is judged by calibration and by comparing with richer models, not by the residual deviance.

10Choosing the link: logit, probit, complementary log-log Expert

The link is a modelling choice. Logit (log-odds) is the default because of its odds-ratio interpretation, and because it is the canonical link (simplest likelihood, sufficient statistics, works with case-control sampling). Probit assumes a normal latent variable; its curve is almost indistinguishable from the logit’s after rescaling (probit coefficients are roughly the logit ones divided by 1.7). The complementary log-log link is asymmetric, suited to events such as survival to a time point in discrete-time hazard models. A log link on binomial data gives risk ratios directly but can predict probabilities above 1 and fail to converge. Links disagree mainly in the tails, which is exactly where rare-event risk models live, so check calibration there.

Part III · Practice

11Rare events and undersampling Expert

With 1% events (fraud, default, churn in some books), analysts often keep every event and only a fraction r of the non-events, to speed up training. For logistic regression this is a well-understood case-control sample: the slope coefficients are unchanged (they are estimated consistently) but the intercept is pushed up by −log r, so predicted probabilities are inflated by a factor of about 1/r. Rankings and AUC are unaffected, but anything that uses the probability (expected loss, thresholds defined by cost, reporting to a regulator) is wrong until the intercept is corrected: α̂_corrected = α̂ + log r (log r is negative). Undersampling also throws away information about the non-events, so with enough computing power it is better to keep all the data and use weights or simply fit the full model.
