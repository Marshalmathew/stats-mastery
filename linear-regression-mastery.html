Linear Regression

Linear regression, done properly.

Regression is the workhorse of applied statistics and the foundation of almost everything in Stages 3 to 5 of the roadmap: logistic regression, regularisation, causal estimates, survival models and mixed models are all extensions of it. This page covers what a regression coefficient really means, the geometry behind “controlling for”, omitted-variable bias and bad controls, the sampling theory of the estimates, R² and overfitting, confidence versus prediction intervals, residual diagnostics, leverage and influence, heteroskedasticity and robust errors, measurement error and regression to the mean, and the interpretation traps of log transforms. Each idea is a lab you can break.

Part I · Meaning and geometry

1What regression estimates and how least squares picks a line Beginner

A regression models the conditional mean E[Y | X], the average outcome for units with given predictor values. The simple linear model is y = β₀ + β₁x + ε, and ordinary least squares (OLS) chooses the line that minimises the sum of squared residuals SSE = Σ(yᵢ − β₀ − β₁xᵢ)². The solution has a closed form:

β̂₁ = Cov(x, y) / Var(x) = r · (s_y / s_x) β̂₀ = ȳ − β̂₁ x̄

Consequences worth remembering: the fitted line passes through (x̄, ȳ); the residuals sum to zero and are uncorrelated with x; and the slope is the average difference in y between units one unit apart in x, an association, not a causal effect unless further assumptions hold (Chapter 3). The name “regression” comes from Galton’s observation that children of tall parents are shorter on average than their parents (Chapter 10).

2Matrix form, projection and “controlling for” Intermediate

With several predictors, write y = Xβ + ε with X the n × p design matrix (a column of ones plus the predictors). The OLS solution is β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy and the fitted values ŷ = Hy, where H = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ is the hat matrix: OLS projects y onto the space spanned by the columns of X, and the residual vector is perpendicular to that space. Its diagonal entries hᵢᵢ are the leverages used in Chapter 8.

The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem says what “holding the other variables constant” means: the coefficient on x₁ in a multiple regression equals the slope from regressing (y purged of the other predictors) on (x₁ purged of the other predictors). Each coefficient is the relationship between the parts of y and x₁ that the other variables cannot explain. When x₁ and x₂ are strongly correlated there is little left of x₁ after purging, and its coefficient becomes imprecise (the multicollinearity of the next course page).

Lab 2 · Frisch–Waugh–Lovell, visibly

y = 1·x₁ + b₂·x₂ + noise, with x₁ and x₂ correlated. Left: y against x₁ alone (short regression). Right: the partial-regression plot, the residuals of y on x₂ against the residuals of x₁ on x₂. Its slope is exactly the multiple-regression coefficient.

3Omitted variables, confounders and bad controls Advanced

If the true model is y = β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + ε and you regress y on x₁ alone, the slope converges to β₁ + β₂·δ, where δ is the slope of x₂ on x₁. The bias is the product of “how much the omitted variable matters” and “how correlated it is with the included one”. That is omitted-variable bias, the central threat to causal readings of regression.

The remedy is not “add every variable”. What to adjust for depends on the causal structure:

Role of the extra variable ZStructureAdjust for it?
ConfounderZ → X and Z → YYes: omitting it biases the effect of X
MediatorX → Z → YNot if you want the total effect; adjusting estimates only the direct effect
ColliderX → Z ← YNo: adjusting creates a spurious association between X and Y

Part II · Inference and fit

4How precise is a coefficient? Intermediate

Under the classical assumptions (a linear mean, errors with mean zero given X, constant variance σ², uncorrelated errors; plus normality for exact small-sample results), β̂ has mean β and covariance σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹. For a simple regression:

SE(β̂₁) = σ / √( Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² ) = σ / ( s_x √(n − 1) ) t = (β̂₁ − β₁) / SE ~ t with n − 2 degrees of freedom

Three levers on precision: more data (n), less noise (σ) and more spread in x, which is a design choice in experiments. By the Gauss–Markov theorem OLS is the best linear unbiased estimator under the first four assumptions; normality is only needed for exact t and F tests in small samples. Violations of the other assumptions have different costs: nonlinearity and endogeneity bias the coefficients; heteroskedasticity and correlated errors leave them unbiased but make the standard errors wrong (Chapters 7 and 9).

5R², adjusted R², the F-test and overfitting Advanced

R² = 1 − SSE/SST is the share of the outcome’s variance explained in the sample. It never decreases when a predictor is added, even a useless one, because a bigger model can always fit the training noise a little better. Adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − p − 1) penalises the extra parameters; the overall F-test and the information criteria of the model-selection course do the same job more formally. What matters for prediction is out-of-sample R², which for an over-fitted model can be negative: worse than predicting the mean.

6Confidence band versus prediction band Advanced

A regression gives two different intervals at a point x₀. The confidence interval for the mean response ŷ₀ ± t·s·√(h₀) covers the true average outcome at x₀; the prediction interval ŷ₀ ± t·s·√(1 + h₀) covers a new individual, and includes the irreducible scatter. Here h₀ = 1/n + (x₀ − x̄)²/Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² is the leverage of the new point: both bands are narrowest at x̄ and widen away from it, which is why extrapolation is dangerous. The prediction band never shrinks below ±1.96σ however much data you have.

8Leverage, outliers and influence Expert

Three ideas are often confused. Leverage hᵢ measures how unusual a point’s x is: for a simple regression hᵢ = 1/n + (xᵢ − x̄)²/Σ(x − x̄)², with average 2/n. An outlier has an unusual y given x (a large residual). A point is influential when removing it changes the fit substantially, which needs both: high leverage and a residual that is not small. Cook’s distance Dᵢ = rᵢ²/p · hᵢ/(1 − hᵢ) combines them (rᵢ the standardised residual, p the number of coefficients); values above about 4/n deserve a look and above 1 are alarming. A high-leverage point that lies right on the line is harmless; it just makes the fit more precise.

9Heteroskedasticity: robust standard errors and weighted least squares Expert

When the error variance depends on x, OLS stays unbiased but the classical standard errors are wrong, possibly in either direction. Fixes, in the order most analysts reach for them: heteroskedasticity-consistent (“robust”, “sandwich”) standard errors HC0–HC3, which estimate Var(β̂) by (XᵀX)⁻¹ XᵀΩ̂X (XᵀX)⁻¹ with Ω̂ = diag(eᵢ²) (HC0), rescaled by n/(n − p) (HC1) or divided by (1 − hᵢ)² (HC3, preferred in small samples because it corrects for leverage); weighted least squares if you know or can model the variance, which also gains efficiency; or a transformation of y. If observations are grouped (customers, branches), use cluster-robust errors instead.

Part IV · Pitfalls

10Measurement error and regression to the mean Expert

Two effects that look like findings but are artefacts of noise.

Attenuation. If a predictor is measured with random error, its estimated slope shrinks toward zero by the reliability λ = Var(true x)/(Var(true x) + Var(error)). A noisy credit score or self-reported income will understate its own importance, and its confounding adjustments will be incomplete.

Regression to the mean. Two imperfectly correlated measurements (before and after) will show that units selected for being extreme on the first measurement are, on average, less extreme on the second, even with no intervention: the expected shift is the fraction (1 − ρ) of the way back to the mean. A group flagged as “high risk this year” will look better next year whether or not you did anything.

11Transformations, interpretation and retransformation bias Expert

How to read a coefficient depends on the scale of the variables:

ModelA one-unit increase in x is associated with…
y on x (level–level)a change of β in y
log y on x (log–level)a change of about 100·β % in y (exactly 100(e^β − 1) %)
log y on log x (log–log)a 1% change in x goes with a β % change in y (an elasticity)
y on a dummythe difference in group means

A log model is natural for positive, right-skewed outcomes such as balances or revenue, and often fixes both curvature and heteroskedasticity. But predictions need care: exp(prediction of log y) estimates the median, not the mean, of y; the mean is larger by a factor of about exp(σ²/2). Duan’s smearing estimator (multiply by the average of exp(residual)) corrects this without assuming normality. Forgetting it makes totals (portfolio loss, revenue forecasts) systematically too low.
